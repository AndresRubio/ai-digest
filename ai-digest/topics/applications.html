Applications & case studies

Last updated: 2026-09-14

Current state

The week's substantial piece is a measurement problem, not a model one. A survey of the healthcare literature (Sep 12) totals up what analytics built on codes alone cannot see: at a London teaching hospital a year after a full Epic rollout, 516 patients' problem lists held 2,841 diagnoses and review of the notes found 1,722 more, so 37.7% existed only in free text; administrative-only HEDIS rates ran 20.4 points below chart-reviewed rates across 283 plans; Medicare claims coded 2.01% of beneficiaries as smokers where the BRFSS survey found 10.03%; the National Inpatient Sample codes obesity at 15.4% against measured BMI's 41.9%; 14.4% of a Kaiser cohort with eGFR between 10 and 59 carried a CKD code. The explanation is structural rather than sloppy — a code is produced at billing time under coding rules, so its presence is decent evidence a condition exists and its absence is weak evidence it does not. What makes this current rather than a standing grievance is the direction ambient scribes push it: across 20,302 matched primary-care visits, AI-scribed notes documented significantly more neuropsychiatric symptoms while the odds of a resulting referral, diagnosis code or prescription fell (adjusted OR 0.83 against unscribed visits; 0.97 for human scribes). Notes are growing roughly 20% per visit and the coded fraction is not, so the gap widens precisely where AI is being deployed. The requirements this implies for extraction — assertion and temporal handling so “denies” and “history of” are not counted as active, provenance to the source sentence, running on identified data inside the customer's environment, deterministic measure logic on top of extracted facts — are the same constraints the fact-level provenance piece named a week earlier, arrived at from the audit side instead of the measurement side. Elsewhere, Universal Music and ElevenLabs announced a licensed-catalogue remix platform (Sep 11), the second licensing-first launch in a fortnight after Suno v6's Warner and BMG deal — the binding constraint on generative music having moved from model quality to catalogue rights. Before this cycle, three items shared a through-line: the model was the easy part in all of them. A Sep 10 analysis proposes personalised cancer-vaccine design as the cleanest available test of whether genome language models learned transferable biology or fitted their corpus: five steps demanding different knowledge — identify a patient's mutations, predict which peptides are presented, predict which provoke a response, rank the targets, design the encoding RNA — each of which must be right for the output to be a candidate medicine, and no population average for a wrong answer to hide inside, because the target is one person. PostHog's Replay Vision makes the point from the engineering side: letting a model watch a session recording sounds like a model capability, but replays are DOM mutation streams rather than video, so the shippable work was a custom rasterizer running hundreds of concurrent jobs across many pods — over 370 years of recorded sessions turned into roughly 3.5 million videos since March. And Suno v6 (Sep 9) settles this page's longest-deferred question with money rather than argument: trained on music licensed from Warner, BMG and other majors, and shipped as a replacement for the previous models rather than a compliant tier beside them, which puts a revealed price on the training-data answer that every other media-generation vendor can now read. Before them, biology took the cycle's largest step by changing distribution rather than capability. DeepMind's AlphaGenome Atlas ships one petabyte of precomputed predictions covering the regulatory effect of all 9 billion possible single-nucleotide human variants, each with a single impact score, queryable without writing code or running the model — which removes the inference budget as a prerequisite for using it, and that was the actual barrier for most labs. GenBio's AIDO Cell attacks the other half of the problem, simulating a cell's natural state and its responses to successive perturbations for K562 and HepG2, with an academic early-access programme forming; following predicted consequences across biological levels one intervention at a time is exactly what wet-lab work does slowly. Outer Biosciences is the early, unvalidated third entry, keeping donated human skin tissue alive and training on how it responds to compounds — a training signal from live human tissue rather than published assays, with no model or benchmark published yet. In professional services the pattern is memory rather than modelling. Harvey II lets legal agents start each task with the context, memory and preferences accumulated from prior ones, which targets the actual cost in that work: the same matter, client conventions and document set re-explained every session. Harvey also post-trained a Kimi K3 base with asynchronous RL inside long-horizon legal environments and routes specialized diligence, review-table and firm-knowledge capabilities as tools — all vendor-reported. a16z's adoption data points the same way from outside any one vendor: since February the fastest-growing Codex adopters have been non-engineering functions, with legal up 108×, sales and recruiting 41×, marketing 26× and healthcare 24×, which are multiples off small bases but describe knowledge work being reshaped into agent-shaped tasks rather than code being written. And one anecdote is worth keeping for what it says about where expertise now binds: an Anthropic employee whose formal mathematics ended after one semester of high-school geometry used the Claude phone app, prompting mostly “keep going,” and produced a Riemann-adjacent result a Stanford number theorist called the most impressive AI mathematics result so far. Two of this cycle's items are about what has to be true for an AI system to be admissible rather than impressive. The FDA's December 2025 real-world-evidence guidance, operational since February 2026, treats relevance and reliability as per-fact properties: a reviewer asking where a single value came from should get the source document, the exact span, the model and prompt version, the extraction confidence, the conflicts detected and the rule or reviewer that resolved them. Meeting that takes 42 distinct capabilities across ingestion, extraction, privacy, reasoning, audit and versioning — two to three years of engineering — and several of them, notably per-fact source coordinates and point-in-time reconstruction, only work if designed in from the first sprint. The consequence that generalizes past healthcare is about model choice: a hosted frontier model is neither deterministic across runs nor pinnable across years, so an extraction step run through one cannot be reproduced when that version is retired, and a peer-reviewed comparison puts a healthcare-specific small model at 96% F1 on PHI detection against 79% for GPT-4o at over 80% lower cost. Alongside it, Rentosertib — designed with AI for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis — showed reductions in biological age markers across six “aging clocks”, themselves ML models predicting morbidity and mortality; a secondary observation, years from approval even for its original indication. On the generative side, Runway's Solaris pushes interface generation past markup entirely, producing real-time interactive interfaces frame by frame with rendering and interaction handled jointly and no intermediate representation, aimed as much at training agents in environments that change under them as at building UIs. And OpenAI's Rosalind Workbench packages life-science tooling and specialized biology models into the ChatGPT app as a research preview — the same bet as Claudeforce, aimed at a field where the tooling, not the model, is the barrier. Before all of that, video was where retrieval went agentic. Gemini's agentic video understanding stops sampling at a fixed frame rate and lets the model choose what to inspect and through which channel — frames, audio, or transcript — skimming, searching, zooming and rewatching only where the question requires it, for up to 88% fewer tokens, 66% lower cost and 7% better accuracy. The win comes from not ingesting most of the input, which is the same principle behind the agentic-RAG work this page has tracked in text. Alongside it, World Labs' Atlas adds precise camera-path control to world generation and reconstructs scenes from sparse images into point clouds and Gaussian splats — artifacts a robotics, VFX or game pipeline can consume, as opposed to a rendered clip it cannot. The strongest evidence yet that a chatbot can do measurable clinical good in one sitting arrived Aug 12: a randomized controlled trial of overit, a Claude Sonnet 4.5 app for post-breakup distress, found a single ~20-minute conversation cut Breakup Distress Scale scores from 35.3 to 26.6 after seven days against 35.9 to 32.2 in the control arm, still separated at one month, with 61.7% of users reporting a “sudden insight” against 19.3% of controls (n=171). Single-session matters because multi-session therapeutic chatbots lose users to attrition before the benefit lands. The transferable engineering is the two-call structure: every user turn triggers one model call to judge which of five therapeutic milestones the conversation has reached and a separate one to generate the reply, which is what keeps the model challenging a self-limiting belief rather than sycophantically affirming it — a template for any agent that has to push back on the person it is talking to. The newest entry (Aug 3) opens a product-search thread: Onton's Ontology 1 is a neurosymbolic e-commerce search model that doesn't trust seller labels — there is no product filter for "pet-friendly," so it reasons from properties more likely to be objective (fiber, weave, construction) inside an explicit, inspectable knowledge graph, and flags claims the product data contradicts. On Subtext-Decor-90, scored by three independent LLM judges, it reaches P@10 0.630 against Google Shopping's 0.543 and Amazon's 0.469 while indexing roughly 1% of their catalogs, winning 52 of 90 queries outright (judge agreement is modest at Krippendorff's alpha 0.465, though all three judges rank the engines identically). Live on Onton.com; no public API or open weights. It is the clearest applied datapoint yet that explicit, inspectable reasoning can beat scale in a domain where the incumbent advantage is catalog size. Before that, the newest healthcare entry (Aug 1) reframes a familiar risk: clinical LLMs don't just make novel errors, they inherit one of the best-studied problems in patient safety — cognitive bias in diagnosis. Give a frontier model a clinical note about a 52-year-old with chest pain and its recommendation can be steered by the note's framing, the same anchoring effect documented in physicians since Graber, Franklin, and Gordon's 2005 diagnostic-error work. Because models learn from clinician-written notes, the bias arrives with the training data; it isn't a problem AI invented, and (as the July 31 evals thread argued) it isn't caught by knowledge-style benchmark passes either. Before that, AI-assisted drug discovery entered the page (July 31): Stanford University researchers, using AI to analyze human protein data, identified a naturally-occurring peptide that delivers results comparable to Ozempic (the GLP-1 obesity drug) while reducing side effects — a non-synthetic alternative surfaced by model-driven analysis of existing biological data rather than synthetic chemistry, and a concrete instance of AI shortcutting the discovery step of the pharma pipeline. The Maven Clinic case study below now has a sequel that shows what happened after launch. William Horton returned to the Vanishing Gradients podcast (July 25) four months after Maven Assistant's initial rollout: the healthcare agent is now available to 100% of users, up from the 20% it launched to, weekly conversation volume has grown roughly tenfold, and real usage overturned part of the original roadmap — 50-60% of early conversations turned out to be basic health questions rather than the provider-search and appointment-booking tools the team had built out first. The team also moved from Gemini Flash models toward newer OpenAI models, now runs over 1,000 deterministic test scenarios alongside LLM judges, and treats a model swap itself as a trigger to re-audit the harness rather than assume improvement — a newer model, in one case, correctly flagged a mocked "upcoming" appointment that had already happened, which is a good outcome but still a change in behavior the team had to go verify rather than take on faith. It's a rare look at the gap between launch-day assumptions and four months of real conversation data, and a concrete argument for the "convert production failures into the eval suite" practice the original case study described. The same week, John Snow Labs' Medical LLM – Medium ranked first on all 15 clinical/biomedical benchmarks in the MedHELM suite (July 25), averaging 80.9 against 76.5, 75.0, and 74.7 for the newest OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google frontier releases respectively. It runs on a single GPU with per-server annual licensing (no per-token cost), modeled at roughly 12-29x cheaper than frontier APIs at population scale (1 million patients) — directly extending the deployment-economics cost model already on this page — though margins over frontier models are near-zero on saturated exam-style benchmarks and widest on operational tasks (clinical-note error-catching, medication Q&A), a useful caveat against reading a single average score as uniform superiority. The earlier strand below is production agent architecture in a regulated, high-stakes domain: William Horton (Staff AI Engineer, Maven Clinic) described how Maven Assistant, a healthcare agent for Maven Clinic's women's and family-health platform, was built and evaluated the day after it reached its first external users. The design keeps the toolset small on purpose — a lead agent routes to four narrower specialists (appointments, provider search, health questions, Maven support) covering just 15-20 tools total, with hard guardrails running before dynamic routing — and it ships with a consequence-scaled release bar rather than one pass rate for everything: a wrong tool call might be accepted at nine-out-of-ten passes, but answers that could influence tens of thousands of dollars in benefits, or self-harm language, are withheld or routed straight to a human. The practice worth generalizing is turning production failures directly into the eval suite — a bad trace becomes a regression case, deterministic code checks tool calls, and LLM judges (calibrated against human labels) cover behavior that can't be reduced to an exact expected output. This slots alongside the existing healthcare-economics and evaluation-realism threads below as a third healthcare-AI angle: not cost, and not benchmark validity, but how a real deployed healthcare agent is architected and kept safe to ship. Several other strands of applied work remain visible. The next is deployment economics: a detailed cost model for patient-level healthcare AI argues that, at production scale, the deployment model (local vs. frontier API) dwarfs the choice between API vendors — locally deployed medical LLMs run ~1.35× cheaper at pilot volume but ~18–29× cheaper at a million patients, because per-token pricing is linear in data volume while per-server licensing plus infrastructure grows sublinearly. The same source has since widened the healthcare thread from cost to evaluation realism, arguing that headline "medical benchmark" wins for general-purpose models reflect single-turn question-answering rather than the multi-step, record-grounded clinical work that decides deployment value (see Evals & benchmarks for the methodology dispute). A third healthcare signal is on the data side: NeuroVFM (University of Michigan, Nature Medicine) shows a generalist brain-imaging model can be trained on 5.24M uncurated clinical MRI/CT volumes with no labels — a template for turning the idle scan archives every hospital already holds into open foundation models, and the clearest sign yet that medical AI's labeled-data bottleneck is loosening (method detail on the Research papers page). Speech remains a strong applied area on both ends of the pipeline: on generation, Miso Labs' MisoTTS brings emotive open-weights text-to-speech (~110ms latency, one-shot voice cloning); on transcription, two production-grade speech-to-text releases — Microsoft's MAI-Transcribe-1.5 (2.4% WER, 43 languages, an hour of audio in under 15 seconds, on Azure AI Foundry) and NVIDIA's Nemotron 3.5 ASR (600M streaming, 40 locales, fully local via ONNX) — make fast, multilingual transcription a commodity building block. Real-time translation is the newest branch: Gradium's stt-translate and s2s-translate collapse the usual ASR→translation→TTS stack into single end-to-end models streaming across five languages, reportedly beating gpt-realtime-translate on both accuracy and latency — a ready benchmark target for multilingual voice agents. The build-vs-buy decision behind all of this got a systematic framework July 27, when Hamza Farooq's voice-agent deep dive argued every architectural choice — cascading STT→LLM→TTS pipelines vs. native speech-to-speech models (OpenAI's Realtime API, Google's Gemini Live), open self-hosted components (Whisper, Kokoro) vs. closed APIs — is really a decision about how to spend a roughly 700-800ms per-turn latency budget, with speech-to-speech buying latency at the cost of the inspection and compliance checkpoints a cascading pipeline provides; the piece also flags a widening trust gap, with consumer preference for human agents over AI reportedly climbing even as blind listening tests show AI voices are now largely indistinguishable from human ones. On creative media, the open stack keeps filling in (Stability AI audio, ByteDance unified video). On physical AI, NVIDIA's Cosmos 3 open world model targets robotics, AVs, and warehouse monitoring. On knowledge work, a Perplexity/Harvard study quantifies the agent-vs-chat productivity gap: workers using an autonomous agent ("Computer") finished tasks in 87% less time at 94% lower cost than search alone. The common shift: applied AI is moving from generic LLM wrappers to domain-specific agent loops with their own verification — and, increasingly, to deployment choices driven by total cost at scale.

Creative media

The newest entry is a rights deal rather than a model: Universal Music and ElevenLabs are launching a platform for remixes and mashups drawn from UMG's licensed catalogue (Sep 11), a fortnight after Suno v6 replaced its entire model line with one trained on licensed Warner and BMG material. Two launches in two weeks, both leading with provenance rather than fidelity, is enough to call the shift: in generative music the scarce input is now catalogue clearance, and model quality has stopped being the thing that distinguishes an offering. Open-weights generative audio is the other recent entry. Combined with ByteDance's recent unified video and existing open image generators, the open creative-media stack is now broadly usable end-to-end. MisoTTS extends this into emotive speech: an 8B model conditioning on text plus prior audio (residual vector quantization over 32 codebooks) for tone-tracking output, claiming ~110ms latency vs. cited 700ms/300ms for ElevenLabs/Sesame — though it's half-duplex and single-turn today. Runway's Solaris (Sep 1) extends generative media into the interface itself: an Interface World Model producing real-time interactive interfaces frame by frame as the user acts, with rendering and interaction handled jointly and no intermediate representation emitted for a browser to draw. Set against Atlas and Worlds 2, it is the same interactivity threshold applied to a different surface — and Runway names the second use plainly, training agents in environments dynamic enough to be worth training in, which is the harder half of what agent training environments currently lack. Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash's 360p draft mode, meanwhile, closes the loop on cost from the other direction: iterate at a third the price, then upscale the take you keep. The licensing question this category has deferred got its first expensive answer on Sep 9. Suno v6 is trained on music licensed from major labels including Warner and BMG, and it replaces the previous models rather than shipping beside them as a compliant tier — retraining the product and retiring the version that prompted the copyright suits, instead of defending it. Whatever the litigation eventually decides, the industry now has a revealed price for the answer, and every other media-generation vendor can read it.

Speech-to-text in production

Transcription is becoming a fast, multilingual commodity. Microsoft's MAI-Transcribe-1.5 leads the accuracy-speed Pareto frontier at its tier (2.4% WER, 43 languages, best-in-class FLEURS, an hour of audio in under 15 seconds, up to 5× faster than Gemini 3.1 / Scribe v2 / GPT-4o-Transcribe on long audio) and is available on Azure AI Foundry for cloud workloads. NVIDIA's Nemotron 3.5 ASR covers the on-device/streaming case — one 600M checkpoint, 40 locales, native punctuation and runtime language switching, fully local via ONNX. Between them, applications spanning meeting capture, captioning, and voice agents now have both a hosted high-accuracy option and a local real-time option. See Model releases for model detail.

Voice agent architecture & latency budgets

Building a voice agent is, in practice, an exercise in allocating a fixed latency budget — roughly 700 to 800 milliseconds per conversational turn — across pipeline stages before a conversation stops feeling human. Cascading architectures (separate STT, LLM, and TTS stages) preserve inspection points for guardrails, compliance, and error-catching, but latency accumulates at every handoff; native speech-to-speech models (OpenAI's Realtime API, Google's Gemini Live) remove those handoffs and buy back latency, at the cost of the checkpoints where a cascading pipeline would catch a transcription error or block a compliance violation. Open components (Whisper for transcription, Kokoro for synthesis) are competitive on quality but carry real self-hosting infrastructure costs that only beat closed APIs past a certain scale. The practical guidance: voice earns its keep in hands-busy, phone-native, or accessibility contexts — not simply wherever a user could otherwise type — and the honest current failure modes (noisy-environment transcription errors, barge-in handling, PII exposure) are the real gating question for 2026 deployments, not whether a voice agent can be shipped at all.

Healthcare AI & deployment economics

Before any deployment question comes a measurement one, and a Sep 12 survey of the literature makes it concrete: healthcare analytics computed from structured data alone miss about 37.7% of documented diagnoses, because a diagnosis code is a billing artifact and a note is a clinical record. The specific gaps are large and repeatedly measured — HEDIS rates from claims alone 20.4 points below chart-reviewed rates across 283 commercial plans, Medicare smoking prevalence at 2.01% against a survey's 10.03%, coded inpatient obesity at 15.4% against measured BMI at 41.9%, CKD codes present for 14.4% of a Kaiser cohort whose labs defined the condition. The finding that changes the trajectory concerns ambient scribes: in 20,302 matched primary-care visits, AI-scribed notes recorded more neuropsychiatric symptoms while the odds of a referral, new diagnosis code or prescription dropped (adjusted OR 0.83 versus unscribed, 0.97 for human scribes). More documented, less coded, in the same encounter. That reframes the economics this section tracks: an AI deployment that lengthens the note by ~20% a visit widens the measurement error in every downstream quality rate, risk score and gap-closure list unless something reads the note, and the requirements for that something — validated accuracy on clinical text, assertion and temporal handling, provenance to the source sentence, execution inside the customer's environment on identified data, with deterministic logic computing the measure over extracted facts — are the same ones the fact-level provenance work arrived at from the regulatory direction. A production case study sharpens what "shipping" a healthcare agent then requires. Maven Clinic's Maven Assistant — described by Staff AI Engineer William Horton the day after it reached its first external users — routes member requests through a lead agent to four narrower specialists (appointments, provider search, health questions, Maven support), deliberately holding the toolset to 15-20 tools rather than exposing one model to hundreds of choices, with hard guardrails running before dynamic routing. The release discipline is consequence-scaled rather than uniform: "for a lot of our tool-call evaluation, I'll accept that it runs ten times and passes nine times... going for ten out of ten is just not worth the effort," per Horton, while answers that could influence tens of thousands of dollars in benefits are withheld and self-harm language is routed directly to human support. The team's core practice is converting production failures into the permanent eval suite — a bad trace becomes a regression case, deterministic code verifies tool calls, and LLM judges (calibrated against human labels) cover clinical-accuracy behavior that resists an exact-match check — so the test set and the product both improve from real usage rather than only from synthetic benchmarks. The planned four-month follow-up happened on schedule: Horton returned to the podcast (July 25) to report the agent now serves 100% of users (up from the 20% launch rollout), weekly conversation volume is up roughly tenfold, and real usage overturned part of the original roadmap — 50-60% of early conversations turned out to be basic health questions rather than the provider-search and appointment-booking tools the team had prioritized building. The team has since moved from Gemini Flash models toward newer OpenAI models, expanded to over 1,000 deterministic test scenarios alongside LLM judges, and now treats every model swap as a trigger to re-audit the harness rather than assume the newer model is simply better in every dimension — in one instance, a newer model correctly flagged a mocked "upcoming" appointment that had already happened, a behavior change the team had to go verify rather than take on faith. The regulatory version of that instinct now has a specification. The FDA's December 2025 real-world-evidence guidance, operational since February 2026, makes relevance and reliability per-fact properties of a submission: every clinical assertion carries its source document, exact span, extraction model and prompt version, confidence, detected conflicts and resolution. The write-up of what that costs to build — 42 capabilities across ingestion, extraction, privacy, reasoning, audit and versioning, two to three years of engineering, with per-fact source coordinates, parallel identified/de-identified datasets and point-in-time reconstruction all unretrofittable — is the most concrete answer this page has seen to what “regulated deployment” actually means. Its sharpest consequence generalizes well past healthcare: a hosted frontier model is neither deterministic run-to-run nor pinnable across years, so an extraction step run through one cannot be reproduced in 2028 when that version has been retired. The same source reports a healthcare-specific small model at 96% F1 on PHI detection against 79% for GPT-4o, 83% for AWS Comprehend Medical and 91% for Azure, at over 80% lower cost — which turns “use a small specialist model” from a cost argument into an auditability one.

Healthcare is emerging as a setting where the unit of work is a whole patient — a large object (a cancer patient's record can run to thousands of pages, modeled at ~1M tokens) processed in multiple passes (de-identification, extraction, summarization/reasoning, conflict resolution with explicit chain-of-thought for auditability). At that scale, per-token API pricing collapses: David Talby's (John Snow Labs) cost model puts a locally deployed medical LLM at ~$95K (10K patients) to ~$1.04M (1M patients), versus $128K–$300K up to $12.8M–$30M via frontier APIs (GPT-5.4, Gemini-3.1-Pro, Claude-Opus-4.6). The multiple grows from ~1.35× at pilot scale to ~28.75× at a million patients because per-token cost is linear while per-server licensing plus infrastructure is sublinear (a 100× patient increase needed only 15× the nodes). The deeper point: when the marginal token is free, teams stop rationing and process every page of every record, changing what the data work can attempt. Talby notes the cost case sits on top of a separate accuracy case (the Medical LLM ranks first or tied-first across 13 clinical/biomedical benchmarks; 98.4% primary-site accuracy in a PHUSE study). That accuracy case sharpened on July 25: John Snow Labs' Medical LLM – Medium ranked first on all 15 clinical/biomedical benchmarks in the MedHELM suite, averaging 80.9 against 76.5, 75.0, and 74.7 for the newest OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google frontier releases respectively — running on a single GPU with per-server annual licensing (no per-token cost), modeled at roughly 12-29x cheaper than frontier APIs at population scale (1 million patients). The margin over frontier models is near-zero on saturated exam-style benchmarks and widest on operational tasks (clinical-note error-catching, medication Q&A) — a reminder, consistent with the construct-validity dispute below, that an average leaderboard score can hide where a specialized model actually earns its edge. See Infrastructure for the compute angle.

Beyond cost, the same author has pressed the evaluation question — do benchmark wins translate into clinical value? A July 2026 Nature Medicine Brief Communication reported general-purpose models (GPT-5.2, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.6) beating commercial clinical tools OpenEvidence and Wolters Kluwer's UpToDate Expert AI, and its title generalized that to outperforming "specialized clinical AI tools on medical benchmarks" — drawing a retraction request and a rebuttal from Talby. His deployment-side point: the tests measured single-turn general medical Q&A, whereas applied clinical AI is multi-step and record-grounded — chart summarization, discharge instructions, tumor-stage extraction from pathology, medication reconciliation, catching an error in a note — so exam-style leaderboards don't establish real-world readiness. Benchmark-methodology detail on the Evals & benchmarks page.

A related failure mode is inherited rather than measured: cognitive bias (Aug 1). Clinical LLMs trained on clinician-written notes absorb the documented diagnostic biases of the clinicians who wrote them — anchoring being the canonical case, one of the best-studied problems in patient safety since Graber, Franklin, and Gordon's 2005 work in Archives of Internal Medicine. A frontier model handed a clinical note about a 52-year-old with chest pain can be steered by the note's framing even when its underlying medical knowledge is correct — which connects this page's deployment thread to the evals-side argument (July 31) that bias failure modes need their own deterministic benchmarks, separate from knowledge tests.

A third healthcare thread is the training-data unlock. NeuroVFM (University of Michigan, published in Nature Medicine) is a neuroimaging foundation model trained on 5.24M uncurated clinical MRI and CT volumes pulled straight from hospital systems — no radiology-report labels, no curation — using the self-supervised Vol-JEPA architecture. The applied significance is that it turns the messy scan archives every health system already stores into a generalist brain-imaging model, sidestepping the labeled-data bottleneck that has capped medical AI; the weights are open. It reframes "we don't have labeled data" as "we already have the data" — the real unlock for medical AI at scale. (Architecture/method detail on the Research papers page.)

Knowledge-work productivity

Evidence is accumulating that autonomous agents, not chat, drive the largest applied productivity gains. A Perplexity/Harvard study tracked knowledge workers over three months using an autonomous agent ("Computer") versus search alone, finding tasks completed in 87% less time at 94% lower cost with higher satisfaction. The framing — a measured shift from chat interfaces to autonomous agents — gives applied teams a concrete benchmark for where agent investment pays off. (Study angle on the Research papers page.) PostHog's Replay Vision (Sep 10) is a useful corrective on where that work actually goes. Letting a model watch a session recording sounds like a model capability, but session replays are DOM mutation streams rather than video, so the shippable engineering was a custom rasterizer rendering them to frames across hundreds of concurrent jobs on many pods — more than 370 years of recorded sessions turned into roughly 3.5 million videos since it went to production in March. The pattern recurs across this page: the model was ready for the modality before the data existed in a shape any model could consume, and the deployable artifact was the conversion pipeline.

Agentic data science

Hugo Bowne-Anderson is framing "agentic data science" as a domain reframing of agentic AI: use agents to unlock the original promise of data science (better decisions) rather than treat it as another vertical for generic coding agents. The proposed shape includes agent harnesses with causal eval layers to measure outcome improvement, and agentic dashboards that replace static BI with chat-based inquiry into models.

Drug discovery & biomedicine

Beyond deploying LLMs in care delivery (the Maven and Medical-LLM threads above), AI is showing up one step earlier in the pipeline: discovery. The opening entry is Stanford’s July 31 result using AI over human protein data to identify a naturally-occurring peptide with Ozempic-comparable efficacy for obesity treatment and reduced side effects — model-driven mining of existing biological data standing in for synthetic-chemistry search. Worth tracking separately from clinical deployment because the evaluation regime (wet-lab validation, trials) and the economics (one discovery amortized across a drug program) differ entirely from per-conversation agent economics. Rentosertib (Sep 8) is the first candidate in this thread to produce a result outside the indication it was designed for: developed with AI assistance for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, trial data now shows it also reduced biological age markers as scored by six separate “aging clocks” — themselves ML models trained to predict morbidity and mortality, so the readout is one model's output evaluated by others. It is a secondary observation rather than an endpoint, and the drug remains years from approval even for lung disease. The tooling side moved the same week: OpenAI's Rosalind Workbench entered research preview inside the ChatGPT app, giving life-science users one environment for their existing tools, specialized biology models and recurring analysis workflows — packaging rather than discovery, but packaging is the stated bottleneck in a field where the tooling has always been the barrier. Two releases in this cycle change what a lab without frontier compute can attempt. DeepMind's AlphaGenome Atlas (Sep 9) converts a model into a queryable resource: one petabyte of precomputed predictions for the regulatory effect of every possible single-nucleotide human variant — all 9 billion — each carrying a single impact score for triage, with no code to write and no inference to run. For fundamental biology, disease research and treatment development, the binding constraint was rarely the science and often the compute, and this removes it. GenBio's AIDO Cell (Aug 19) is a world model of a cell that simulates both its natural state and its response to successive perturbations, initially for K562 and HepG2, with an academic collaborator programme being prepared; the successive part is what distinguishes it from single-shot property prediction, since hypothesis generation depends on following consequences through several steps. Outer Biosciences is the speculative counterpart, training on measured responses of living donated human skin tissue rather than on published assay data — a genuinely different training signal, with nothing yet published to evaluate it against. A Sep 10 analysis proposes personalised cancer-vaccine design as the cleanest available test of whether genome language models have learned transferable biology or merely fitted their corpus. The pipeline runs five steps that each demand different knowledge — identify a patient's mutations, predict which resulting peptides are presented on the cell surface, predict which of those provoke an immune response, rank the targets, design the RNA that encodes them — and every stage has to be right for the output to be a candidate medicine rather than a plausible sequence. What makes it a test rather than another application is the denominator: the target is one person, so there is no population average for a wrong answer to hide inside, which is exactly the property the benchmark-shaped results in this field usually lack.

Product search & e-commerce

A newer applied thread is search that reasons rather than matches. Onton's Ontology 1 (Aug 3) is a neurosymbolic search model for e-commerce: instead of trusting seller labels, it reasons from properties more likely to be objective — fiber, weave, construction — in an explicit knowledge graph you can inspect, and flags claims the product data contradicts. On Subtext-Decor-90 (three independent LLM judges) it reaches P@10 0.630 vs Google Shopping's 0.543 and Amazon's 0.469 while indexing ~1% of their catalogs, winning 52 of 90 queries outright; judge agreement is modest (Krippendorff's alpha 0.465) but all three judges rank the engines identically. Live on Onton.com with no public API or open weights. The pattern worth tracking: an inspectable-reasoning layer beating catalog scale, and benchmark scoring by LLM-judge panels (with agreement statistics reported) becoming the norm for search quality.
