Training, post-training & RL

Last updated: 2026-09-18

Current state

Today's single entry is a reframing rather than a result about training quality. Google Research's Retrieve-for-Train takes reinforcement learning out of the serving path: the RL runs once, offline, and is compiled into a 53.9M-parameter diffusion model that generates every retrieval direction in a single pass instead of deciding them sequentially at query time. At batch size 8 that is 0.07 seconds against roughly 1.46, reported as a 12× to 20× speedup. Most of this page treats RL as something a model carries into production — a policy learned in post-training and executed at inference. R4T points out that where RL learns a search policy, it need not run as a policy at all: it can be amortised into a generator, turning an inference-time cost into a training-time one. The limit is built into the trade, since it only works where the decision space can be generated rather than explored, so it does not transfer to agent loops that must react to what a tool returns. For any fan-out settled before the results arrive it is the right shape, and it is the first item on this page to treat RL as a compilation step.

Today's result is about where the training budget goes rather than what it buys. A write-up on RL for LLMs starts from a failure averages conceal: standard RL performs disproportionately badly on a training set's hardest problems, and one scalar value per problem is too thin a signal to judge a large model's progress on it. The fix proposed is budgetary rather than algorithmic — spend less compute on problems the model already solves and redirect it to the hard ones — with substantial reported improvement on difficult tasks. The reasoning is that easy problems stop producing gradient signal long before their allocated rollouts are exhausted, so uniform sampling keeps paying for steps that teach nothing while the informative problems stay undersampled. That arrives at the same place as this page's failure-driven environment work by a cheaper route: TRACE builds an environment around a repeated failure, this one just resamples toward the failures already present. The post reports direction rather than a benchmark table, so neither the size of the gain nor its cost on the easy half can be checked from here.

The prior cycle's single result reinforced the page's running argument that data construction, not compute, is where post-training gains now come from. Google Research's ToolGrad (Sep 14) inverts how tool-use training data is made. The usual pipeline invents a plausible user request and then hopes an agent can find a working call sequence for it — which fails whenever none exists, and quietly trains the model to attempt impossible compositions. ToolGrad constructs a verified API chain first and writes the user question that chain answers afterwards, making validity structural rather than something filtered in afterwards: a 99.8% success rate across 16,000 real APIs. The downstream figure is the one worth carrying — a Gemma 3 12B fine-tuned on 500 of these examples matched Gemini 2.5 Pro on a tool-use test with APIs neither model had seen. That is the same shape as the environment-quality results below, reached from the supervised side: where a task has checkable structure, generating from the structure toward the prompt beats sampling prompts and throwing away the failures, and it makes 500 well-formed examples worth more than a much larger filtered corpus.

Earlier cycles

Two Sep 11 results argue, from opposite scales, that compute is not the constraint people assume. Salesforce found that training smaller models directly on expert agent trajectories can hurt once the harness around them has already been optimised — distillation and scaffolding are shaping the same behaviour, and applied in that order the second undoes the first. That is the training-side statement of the argument the agents page makes about harnesses: model and scaffold are not separable variables, and tuning them independently can leave a system worse than tuning either alone. At the small end, little-lm puts a floor under what pretraining costs: 3.8 billion parameters, 65.3 billion tokens, eight rented B200s for 43 hours, $998 total, scoring 0.384 on CORE against GPT-2's 0.2565 and 0.310 for a comparably priced nanochat run — published with the architecture, configuration, failed experiments and budget optimisations, which is what makes it a recipe rather than an anecdote. Magic sharpened its own claim the same day, now saying its recipe matched DeepSeek V4 with 50× fewer FLOPs and that a larger $4 million run beat public open base models, attributed to dozens of accumulated improvements across architecture, optimisation, data and stability rather than one trick — self-reported, on a model that is not public. Take the three together and the picture is consistent with the environment argument below rather than against it: the scarce input is not FLOPs.

A Sep 10 analysis argues this page's environment layer has stopped being one input among several and become the binding constraint. Under the name recursive synthetic improvement it enumerates where model-generated material has displaced human data across the whole pipeline — the judge that scores outputs, the corpus trained on them, the teacher that distils, the curriculum that orders tasks, and the RL environments the policy learns in — and draws the infrastructural conclusion: once models generate, evaluate and improve their own training data, the scarce inputs are scalable environments and on-policy learning capacity, which is what carries capability past imitation of an existing corpus. It is the general statement of two specific results already here — GLM-5.3 reaching the top of the open tier on agent-built, agent-graded environments with no architecture change, and Ornith-1.5 closing the loop by generating its own training tasks — and it inherits their caveat, that every self-improving-loop claim on this page is still vendor-reported.

The August contribution was smaller and more precise: train the agent inside the harness it will actually run in. The usual approach reconstructs an agent's tool loop inside the training rig, which optimises the policy against an approximation of the system that will be deployed; instead, running each rollout inside the deployed harness lets TRL train on the model calls the agent genuinely made and on hidden-test rewards, so the policy is shaped by the real loop including its quirks and failure modes. It is the train/serve skew problem arriving at the harness layer, where it has been quietly costing agent performance, and the fix is the one the rest of ML settled on years ago — train on the serving path rather than a replica of it.

Two results in the preceding cycle argued that the field has been crediting the wrong component. An analysis of pretraining progress from 2019 to 2025 finds that 3.24× more compute-efficiency gain came from data improvements than from model improvements, that the two are largely independent rather than compounding, and that most model research amounted to removing constraints on scaling rather than improving the architecture per token — with data quality mattering most at small scale, which is exactly where this year's surprising models keep appearing. Magic makes the adjacent claim from the other direction, saying its pretraining recipe is more than ten times more compute-efficient than leading open-weight base models because a lab without frontier compute has no other axis to compete on; that multiple is self-reported against an unfixed baseline and should be tracked, not built on.

On the post-training side, Cameron Wolfe published a standalone reference tracing the whole policy-gradient lineage — VPG establishes the shape, REINFORCE makes it a Monte Carlo estimate with a baseline, PPO adds a critic plus importance ratios and clipping, GRPO removes the critic by comparing completions within a group, and DAPO, Dr. GRPO, GSPO, TIS and CISPO refine loss aggregation, reward normalization, batch selection, clipping and per-token versus per-sequence ratios. Its most consequential claim is not in that spine but in the advanced sections: on-policy RL is naturally robust to catastrophic forgetting where SFT is not, because on-policy updates stay close to behaviour already plausible under the model — observable as low KL drift from the initial model, and correlated with reduced forgetting. If that holds, new capabilities can be added in sequential RL phases without the multi-task retraining that has been assumed necessary.

The tooling caught up in the same window. Miles v0.1 packages rollouts, sandboxing, asynchronous training, replay, model updates and multi-hardware support into one open system, joining Verifiers v1 and AgentENV in treating RL plumbing rather than RL algorithms as the missing piece. Agent Lightning v1.0 fits harnessed agentic RL into 3,500 lines and reports a 14.6-point gain for Qwen3.5-9B on SWE-bench Verified from 6,000 training examples — sample efficiency being the number that matters. Progressive Point Matching gives long-horizon RL partial credit without changing the optimal objective, which is the property reward shaping usually sacrifices. And two vendor results push expertise into the training signal rather than the scaffold: TaoLive's Harness-Aware Training varies skill names, tool schemas, prompt structure and hooks during SFT and agentic RL so a 35B model reads whichever harness it is handed instead of memorizing one, and Harvey post-trained a Kimi K3 base with asynchronous RL inside long-horizon legal environments, then trained separate diligence, review-table and firm-knowledge capabilities the main system routes to as tools. Both evaluations are vendor-authored and domain-bound, but both test the same proposition the section below already argues.

The cheapest entry point into RL yet circulated this week: take Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base, skip supervised fine-tuning entirely, and run GRPO on Countdown-Tasks-3to4 with a rule-based reward that checks only whether the arithmetic target was reached. No cluster, no reward model to train — and therefore no reward model to be gamed. It is offered as a suggested experiment rather than a validated recipe, but it puts the whole loop within reach of a single machine, which is where most people's first real understanding of GRPO will now come from. At the other end, Thinking Machines put up to $50K of Tinker credits behind open-weight safety research — safer open models, hazardous-data filtering, tamper-resistant safety training, reward hacking — turning the fine-tuning API into a research programme with a stated agenda, and paying in compute rather than cash so it reaches groups that actually intend to run experiments.

Before both, a sharp argument arrived in late August about where domain knowledge belongs. Thinking Machines showed that scaffolding a text-to-SQL model helps only up to a ceiling set by the base model's own capability — so the expertise encoded in that scaffold is being applied at the wrong layer, paid for on every inference instead of once during training. Their claim is that expert judgment belongs inside the RL signal, because that is the part that scales. It is a useful counterweight to the harness-centric view dominating the Agents page this month: harness engineering demonstrably buys real capability, and this names the point where it stops.

The pretraining-data side got its first serious refresh in two years with Hugging Face's The Stack v3 (Aug 5): 4.9 trillion tokens across 713 languages from 173M repositories in the filtered training set, 113.7TB raw in the full release, with a knowledge cutoff of August 2025. The change that matters for agent training is structural rather than scalar — v2 recorded which files composed a repository but shipped only identifiers, while v3 delivers repositories whole, which is the form needed to learn how a definition in one file constrains a call in another, the thing coding agents are actually asked to do. Cross-language deduplication with measured rather than estimated overlap also corrected a v2 bug that had been discarding more files than intended. Two caveats are stated by the authors and worth carrying: licence labels come from error-prone automated detection and unlicensed files are retained in the training set, and a 2025 GitHub crawl necessarily contains a large volume of model-written code — so the next generation of coding models trains partly on the output of this one.

Moonshot open-sourced AgentENV (July 28), the RL sandbox/environment infrastructure that powered Kimi K3's agentic RL training — a second concrete instance, after Prime Intellect's Verifiers v1, of treating environment throughput itself (not just the policy model) as core agentic-RL infrastructure worth open-sourcing on its own.

Post-training and RL work now splits along two axes: training agents in the environment they will run in, and targeted post-training that fixes a specific failure mode. On the agent-training axis, two complementary patterns are visible. NVIDIA's Polar made RL work against black-box coding agents by proxying API calls to reconstruct token-faithful trajectories, removing the white-box requirement; Harness-1 takes the inverse approach, training a 20B retrieval agent inside its stateful search harness so the policy learns the tool-use loop directly (0.730 curated recall, open weights and harness code). The through-line there is environment-native RL — training where the agent operates, and the newest release makes the point at the infrastructure level: Prime Intellect's Verifiers v1 rebuilds its environment library into composable tasksets, harnesses, and runtimes wired to an Environments Hub, so any task can be turned into a reusable RL environment via the Prime CLI — as post-training shifts from static datasets to RL environments, that environment stack is becoming the new infrastructure layer everyone builds on.

The newest entry, Stanford's TRACE, ties the two axes together: it watches an agent fail the same way repeatedly, then automatically builds a capability-targeted RL environment around that exact weakness and trains a LoRA adapter to close it — a four-step, LLM-driven pipeline with no hand-authored training tasks, so recurring failures become the training signal and you patch the specific capability gap instead of running a full fine-tune (agent-reliability angle on the Agents page). On the failure-mode axis it joins Liquid AI's Antidoom, which uses Final Token Preference Optimization (FTPO) to target the pathology where reasoning models get stuck repeating or spiraling instead of converging, cutting doom-loop rates by as much as 90% (reported cases down to ~1.4%) while improving eval scores as a side effect of less looping — a rare lightweight, open training fix that pays for itself. DeepReinforce's Ornith-1.0 adds a third idea: models that learn their own RL task-decomposition scaffolds during training rather than relying on a fixed inference harness.

On the scaling side, NVIDIA's Nemotron 3 Ultra showed frontier open MoEs are increasingly built via multi-teacher on-policy distillation from many domain-specific teacher models rather than single-teacher distillation — and the distillation thread got its strongest datapoint yet on Aug 3: Thinking Machines Lab's Inkling-Small (276B/12B-active), distilled in part from the lab's own 975B Inkling, beats its teacher on SWE-bench Verified (80.2% vs 77.6%), Humanity's Last Exam (31.6% vs 29.7%), and ARC-AGI-2 (40.1% vs 36.5%) at a quarter the size, while factual recall regresses sharply (SimpleQA Verified 20.6% vs 43.9%) — a clean empirical picture of what distillation transfers (reasoning capability) and what it loses (parametric knowledge).

RL on black-box models

The pattern emerging this week is to wrap a commercial coding agent (Codex, Claude Code, Qwen Code) and reconstruct training signal from the proxy layer. GRPO is the workhorse algorithm. The implication is that organizations no longer need to choose between using the best frontier coding agent and being able to fine-tune their stack. At the opposite end of the budget, the same algorithm is now demonstrable on a laptop: a circulated recipe runs GRPO directly on Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base with no SFT stage at all, using Countdown-Tasks-3to4 and a rule-based reward that simply checks whether the arithmetic target was hit (Sep 8). The rule-based reward is what makes it tractable — nothing to train, and nothing for the policy to exploit except the task — which is also why it teaches the mechanics honestly while proving nothing about how the loop behaves once the reward is a model.

RL inside the agent harness

A complementary approach trains the agent directly within the stateful environment it will operate in, so the policy learns the full tool-use loop rather than just next-token prediction — and Salesforce's Sep 11 result shows what happens when that coupling is ignored. Training a smaller model on expert agent trajectories degrades its performance if the harness it runs in has already been tuned: the two optimisations target the same behaviour, so the later one partly reverses the earlier. The practical consequence is an ordering constraint nobody had written down — distil first and tune the harness after, or accept that a fine-tune applied to a mature scaffold can be a regression rather than an upgrade. It also explains a puzzle this section has recorded twice: teams reporting that a well-supported small model beat their own distilled version of it. Harness-1 (20B, on GPT-OSS-20B) is the constructive case, trained with RL inside a search harness and learning how and when to search, not just what to retrieve — reaching 0.730 average curated recall with both weights and harness code open.

This "environment-native" RL is the open-source mirror of Polar's proxy approach: instead of instrumenting a closed harness, build an open one and train inside it. See Agents → agentic retrieval for the deployment angle. The August write-up on training a coding agent with TRL makes the sharpest version of the argument. Rather than recreating the agent's tool loop inside the training rig, each rollout runs inside the deployed harness itself, so TRL trains on the model calls the agent actually issued and rewards them against hidden tests. What this preserves is the loop's genuine behaviour — its retries, its tool-call formatting, the places where it stalls — none of which survives a reconstruction faithfully, and all of which the policy will meet in production. Framed that way it is ordinary train/serve skew, the problem feature stores were invented to solve, showing up one abstraction layer higher. The cost is that the harness has to be instrumentable and cheap to run thousands of times, which is why this direction keeps favouring open harnesses over closed ones.

Distillation at frontier scale

NVIDIA's Nemotron 3 Ultra (550B/55B-active) was trained via multi-teacher on-policy distillation from 10+ domain-specific teacher models — a signal that the largest open MoEs are increasingly assembled by distilling many specialist teachers on-policy rather than relying on a single teacher or pure pre-training. (Model detail on the Model releases page.) The student-beats-teacher result arrived Aug 3: Thinking Machines Lab's Inkling-Small, distilled in part from the 975B Inkling, outscores it on SWE-bench Verified, Humanity's Last Exam, and ARC-AGI-2 at a quarter the total size (276B/12B active) — while SimpleQA Verified falls from 43.9% to 20.6%. The pattern to internalize: distillation can concentrate reasoning ability into a smaller model beyond the teacher's own level, but parametric factual recall is what gets paid for it, which argues for pairing distilled models with retrieval rather than treating them as drop-in replacements.

Google Research's Retrieve-for-Train (Sep 16) distils something other than a model: it distils the reinforcement learning itself out of the serving path. RL runs once, offline, and is compiled into a 53.9M-parameter diffusion model that then generates every retrieval direction in one pass rather than deciding them sequentially at query time. At batch size 8 the fan-out takes 0.07 seconds against roughly 1.46 seconds, which Google reports as a 12× to 20× speedup depending on the setting. The framing is the part that generalises past search. Most of this page treats RL as something a model carries with it — a policy learned during post-training and executed at inference — and R4T points out that when RL is used to learn a search policy, the policy never has to run as a policy in production. It can be amortised into a generator that emits the whole decision at once, which converts an inference-time cost into a training-time one. That trade is only available where the decision space can be generated rather than explored, so it does not transfer to agent loops that must react to what a tool returns; but for any fan-out chosen before the results arrive, it is the right shape.

Targeted post-training for failure modes

Distinct from training the whole agent loop, a lighter class of post-training targets one specific pathology. Liquid AI's open Antidoom uses Final Token Preference Optimization (FTPO) to attack "doom loops" — the failure mode where a reasoning model repeats or spirals instead of converging on an answer, silently burning tokens and latency with no useful output. In testing, doom-loop rates fell by up to 90% (reported cases dropping to roughly 1.4%), and because less looping means cleaner trajectories, eval scores improved across the board rather than trading off. Code and blog are public. It is a template for a broader pattern: cheap, open, preference-based fixes aimed at named production failure modes rather than general capability.

Sep 16 adds the version of that instinct that operates during training rather than after it. A write-up on RL for LLMs titled after the idea of never giving up observes that standard RL performs disproportionately badly on a training set’s hardest problems, and that one scalar value per problem is too little to judge a large model’s progress on it. The remedy is budgetary: reduce the compute spent on problems the model already solves and redirect it toward the hard ones, reportedly with substantial improvements on difficult tasks. The mechanism is that easy problems stop yielding gradient signal long before the rollouts allocated to them are exhausted, so uniform sampling keeps buying steps that teach nothing while the informative problems stay undersampled — the same diagnosis the failure-driven environment work below reaches from the other end, with TRACE constructing an environment around a repeated failure instead of resampling toward one. The difference is where the concentration happens, and this is the cheaper of the two, since it needs no new environments. What is not available is the arithmetic: the post reports direction rather than a benchmark table, so neither the size of the gain nor what the reallocation costs on the easy problems can be checked from outside.

Failure-driven RL environments

A distinct idea is to let an agent's own recurring failures generate its training curriculum. Stanford's TRACE watches for a failure that repeats, then runs a four-step, LLM-driven pipeline to synthesize a capability-targeted RL environment around that specific weakness and trains a LoRA adapter to fix it — no hand-authored training tasks. Where the environment-native methods above build one harness and train inside it, TRACE builds a fresh, narrow environment per failure mode; where Antidoom targets one general pathology (doom loops), TRACE targets whatever this particular agent keeps getting wrong. The result is a self-improving loop in which every reproducible bug becomes a small, cheap post-training run rather than a prompt patch or a full fine-tune. (Agent-reliability framing on the Agents page.)

RL environment infrastructure

As post-training moves from static datasets to RL environments, the tooling for defining those environments is hardening into shared infrastructure. Prime Intellect's Verifiers v1 (0.2.0) overhauls its environment library by decomposing an RL environment into three composable parts — tasksets (the problems), harnesses (how the agent interacts), and runtimes (where it executes) — tightly integrated with the Environments Hub so any task can be init'd, developed, evaluated, and pushed as a reusable environment through the Prime CLI. Where TRACE and Harness-1 build environments for a specific failure or agent, Verifiers v1 is the plumbing layer beneath them: an open, composable standard for the environment itself, the kind of quiet primitive an ecosystem ends up standardizing on. Moonshot's AgentENV (open-sourced July 28) is a second instance of the same idea at production scale: the RL sandbox that actually powered Kimi K3's agentic RL training, framed by Moonshot as addressing the overlooked half of agentic RL — environment throughput — while most public attention stays on the policy model itself. The funding side of that infrastructure is starting to be treated as a lever too: Thinking Machines is granting up to $50K of Tinker credits for open-weight safety research (Aug 28), specifically on safer open models, hazardous-data filtering, tamper-resistant safety training and reward hacking. Paying in compute rather than cash selects for groups that will actually run experiments, and it turns a fine-tuning product into a directed research programme — which is a quieter form of the same influence over what gets studied that owning the environment already confers.

Miles v0.1 (Aug 19) is the third full instance of this pattern and the most complete: an open system bundling rollout generation, sandboxing, asynchronous training, replay, model updates and multi-hardware support, with the canonical worked shape being many copies of a coding agent attempting tasks in isolated environments, scoring the attempts, feeding results back, and pushing updated weights to workers without halting the pipeline. Agent Lightning v1.0 (Aug 20) approaches the same layer from minimalism — 3,500 lines, explicitly designed to accept an arbitrary agent harness so that retokenization and training-stability problems can be studied rather than hidden — and reports a 14.6-point improvement for Qwen3.5-9B on SWE-bench Verified from only 6,000 training examples. Between Verifiers v1's composable tasksets/harnesses/runtimes, AgentENV's production sandbox, Miles' asynchronous pipeline and Agent Lightning's harness-agnostic loop, the environment layer now has four credible open implementations and no shared standard, which is roughly where the serving layer stood two years ago. A Sep 10 analysis argues that this layer is no longer one input among several but the binding constraint. Under the name recursive synthetic improvement it enumerates where model-generated material has displaced human data across the whole pipeline — the judge that scores outputs, the corpus trained on them, the teacher that distils, the curriculum that orders tasks, and the RL environments the policy learns in — and draws the infrastructural conclusion: once models generate, evaluate and improve their own training data, the scarce inputs become scalable environments and on-policy learning capacity, which is what lets capability extend past imitation of an existing corpus.

It is the general statement of specific results already on this page. GLM-5.3 reached the top of the open tier on agent-built, agent-graded environments with no architecture change, and DeepReinforce's Ornith-1.5 closed the loop one step further by generating its own training tasks. Google Research's ToolGrad (Sep 14) applies the same inversion to supervised tool-use data, and the result is the cleanest statement yet of why environment construction beats environment sampling. The standard pipeline invents a plausible user request and then hopes an agent can find a call sequence that satisfies it — which silently produces training data teaching a model to attempt things no API composition supports. ToolGrad builds a verified API chain first and writes the question that chain answers second, so validity is structural rather than filtered: 99.8% success across 16,000 real APIs. A Gemma 3 12B trained on 500 such examples matched Gemini 2.5 Pro on tool use with APIs neither had seen, which puts the data-efficiency gain on the same order as the environment-quality gains recorded above and for the same underlying reason. Where a task has checkable structure, generating from the structure toward the prompt dominates sampling prompts and discarding failures. The analysis is a synthesis rather than a result, and the caveat it inherits is the one attached to each of its examples: every claim of a self-improving loop on this page is still vendor-reported.
