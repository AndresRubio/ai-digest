Embodied AI & robotics

Last updated: 2026-09-17

Current state

Today's three releases split along the two things that have actually been holding this field back, and only one of them is a model. Odyssey-3 is the model, and it makes the broadest claim recorded on this page: a foundation world model on an autoregressive diffusion transformer, trained across highly diverse scenarios and offered as general-purpose physical intelligence for controlling robots, powering humanoids, driving vehicles, piloting drones, training other models and playing games from one learned account of physics, dynamics, causality and human behaviour. That is precisely the transfer question every world-model entry here has been circling, asserted rather than demonstrated — no cross-domain benchmark accompanies it, so whether “understands physics” names one competence or a set of domain-specific ones sharing a vocabulary remains open. The architecture is worth keeping regardless: autoregressive diffusion pairs sequential commitment with iterative refinement, which are the two properties the rollout-quality work keeps trading against each other, and treating them as separable is a real position.

The other two are hardware and neither is about what a robot can do. Agility Robotics showed a humanoid engineered to work beside people without a cage, grading its response by proximity — move aside, stand still to let someone pass, or squat into a seated position when a person gets too close — with early access in the first half of 2027 and general availability by the end of that year. The squat is the interesting part, since a stopped humanoid is still a heavy mass at head height, and lowering the centre of gravity is a different safety claim from halting; the constraint it targets is the separation distance certification demands, which is what keeps humanoids in fenced cells rather than any limit on capability. Agility published none of the numbers a safety case is made of — sensing range, false-negative rate, the standard being certified against. Enactic's OpenArm addresses the data constraint from the same direction: an open-source humanoid arm for teleoperation, imitation learning and physical AI research, where the value is standardisation rather than price. The expensive input behind every policy result here is the demonstration, and demonstrations captured on bespoke per-lab rigs carry those rigs into the dataset, so shared hardware is what makes one group's trajectories usable by another.

The Sep 11 addition answered a scaling question this page has had to take on faith: web-video pre-training keeps paying off on real robots, and how much it will pay can be predicted before a robot is involved. Measured through Direct Video-Action models, larger video models and more pre-training compute improve real-world task performance, with the advantage widening on genuinely hard physical work such as complex industrial unpacking rather than saturating on easy tasks. The mechanism identified is that robot gains track better prediction of held-out web video — and, usefully, that DINO FD, a pre-training quality metric computed entirely offline, predicts downstream robot efficiency. That gives the field a cheap offline signal for a decision that has until now required building the policy and running it on hardware, which is the expensive half of every result on this page. It also supports the bet behind the cross-embodiment work below: if the useful representation comes from video prediction, it is not robot-specific, and pooling data across embodiments should help rather than dilute.

Before it, two August items named constraints that are not control algorithms. NVIDIA's WorldTrace is a training-free framework for memory in video world models: it keeps compressed memories reachable through long autoregressive rollouts by assigning them stable, in-distribution positional offsets, so a model can attend to earlier state without its positional encodings drifting outside the range it was trained on. Training-free is the load-bearing word — it is a decoding-time change to an existing model, which turns long-horizon rollout consistency from a training-budget problem into a deployment one, and consistency under action is exactly the property that separates a world model usable as a training environment from a video generator.

The other constraint is data, and it is being addressed by hiring. Robotics companies are recruiting tens of thousands of workers in India to record their own work in first person and feeding the footage into systems meant to teach robots physical tasks, because real video of humans doing mundane manual work barely exists — nobody had reason to collect it. That is the embodied counterpart to the web-scrape moment in language modelling, with the difference that this corpus has to be commissioned rather than found, which makes it a cost line and a supply chain rather than a windfall.

Earlier cycles

The August demonstrations and the August caveats had arrived together, which made the preceding cycle a good one for calibration. At the World Humanoid Robot Games in Beijing, Chinese humanoids broke human records including Usain Bolt's 100-metre mark, across 51 events and more than 1,000 competitions involving over 2,000 robots. The experts quoted alongside the results were blunt that mass deployment remains distant and these machines are still mainly used for demonstrations, performances and research — and the reason is visible in the events chosen: sprinting is controlled and repeatable, which is the class of task robots already handle. Unitree's founder supplies the honest threshold in the same window: humanoids reach the mass market once they handle 80% of voice-instructed tasks in unfamiliar surroundings, which he puts two to ten years out, because robots perform well in test conditions and execution collapses once objects or surroundings shift even slightly. Nearly half of Unitree's recent IPO proceeds go to embodied AI on exactly that reasoning.

Autonomy on the road is further along and the constraint has moved. Amazon's Zoox now carries public passengers with no dashboard, pedals or steering wheel — bidirectional, four seats facing each other — across San Francisco and Las Vegas, while Tesla's similarly control-free Cybercab has not received the same approval; regulatory clearance, not capability, is what currently separates them. Waymo meanwhile built its first custom silicon on TSMC's 5nm process to convert raw sensor data into driver responses faster, bringing the inference path in-house behind a stack already validated across 220.6 million reported rider-only miles.

Two of the field's open questions moved this cycle, and neither was about capability. On manufacture: XPeng started production of its IRON humanoid on a line where more than 80% of core processes run automatically, targeting mass production by year end — the number that separates a demonstrator from a product, and a sharper signal than any gait video. Hugging Face attacked the same problem from the other end with the Microduck, a $399 25cm open-source robot that waddles, lifts 800g with its beak, rights itself and roller-skates, shipping with SDK, simulator and the full RL training stack, so sim-to-real becomes something you practise rather than read about. On money and models: Veeda, founded by former NVIDIA researchers including Sanja Fidler, raised a $90M seed led by Khosla and Radical for multimodal world models — one of the largest Canadian seed rounds ever, and roughly the sixth well-funded world-model startup, which is the clearest collective statement yet that the field considers LLM-only robotics a dead end. ByteDance is entering the same race with a real-time spatial video model responding to Pico headset users' voices and movements, possibly next month; unlike Runway, World Labs and Google it already owns the hardware to ship it into. And Qwen-Drive narrows the same idea to driving: a vision-language foundation model trained in stages that integrate perception, language and planning, whose stated achievement is what it does not lose — specialized driving competence while retaining broad visual understanding and instruction-following, on a recommended 24GB+ GPU.

Before all of this, the August follow-up on Gemini Robotics 2 supplied what the launch withheld and complicated the headline. Whole-body pick-ups land between 45.7% (from the floor) and 76.3% (from a shelf), fine finger work between 32% and 92%, and the gap inside that finger range is the most honest result in the release: unscrewing a light bulb works 92% of the time, screwing one in 36%, because the reverse direction has to establish alignment before it can apply rotation. Google published no model card, parameter count, or training-data breakdown, and no third party has evaluated the model. Shipped with it is ASIMOV-Agentic, a safety benchmark whose finding applies to the whole field rather than one model: nothing tested, Google's own systems included, could both catch hazards to a nearby person and avoid stopping for nothing — keeping needless stops under 5% meant missing over 40% of genuine proximity events — which is why the recommendation is to run these models alongside conventional physical safety hardware, not instead of it.

Google DeepMind is the newest mover (July 30): Gemini Robotics 2 ships three physical-AI models — a VLA, an embodied-reasoning VLM, and an on-device VLA — with a single checkpoint driving the Apollo 2 humanoid with two different hands plus a Franka Duo gripper. The framing is whole-body control rather than table-top manipulation (the robot walks to the shelf, then places the object), and the release is unusually candid about limits: multi-finger dexterity still runs 32–92% depending on the task — unscrewing a bulb is solved, tying a trash bag is not. That moves DeepMind from the "mostly closed generalist" slot in the July landscape below to shipping physical-AI models at the cadence of the open labs. Before that (July 28) came Black Forest Labs' flow-based model extended to robot control — reported only as a headline item in this week's MarkTechPost roundup ("a Flow Model That Drives Robots") with no further technical detail available in the digest.

World models had a reproducibility moment in late July before that. Induction Labs released Photon-1 (July 26), its first "imagination model," trained on the equivalent of roughly 18 years of screen recordings with no action labels; from a single pretraining run it can simulate desktops, play tournament-level checkers (after fine-tuning on 20,000 games), and model billiard-ball physics — a single pretrained model generalizing across strikingly different simulated domains rather than a bespoke model per task. The same day, Open-Dreamer, a JAX/Flax NNX reproduction of the Dreamer 4 world-model pipeline, published its complete training recipe (code and configs), trained on Minecraft/VPT-style data — the kind of published, reproducible recipe (not just weights) that is what actually lets a technique like Dreamer 4 spread beyond the lab that invented it, complementing the LingBot-World-Infinity/Cosmos 3 world-model thread already tracked below. NVIDIA also reportedly put a world model on a robot with "Cosmos 3 Edge" (July 22), mentioned only as a headline item in a MarkTechPost weekly roundup with no further detail available.

Physical AI has gone from a side thread to the dominant open-model story of early July, driven almost entirely by Ant Group's Robbyant, which open-sourced four models in five days that together span the full embodied pipeline: LingBot-Vision (1B boundary-centric perception), LingBot-VLA 2.0 (6B vision-language-action across 20 robot embodiments), LingBot-World-Infinity (a causal world model with an agentic harness), and LingBot-VA 2.0 (a video-action policy that predicts while acting). Two structural ideas recur across the stack: re-grounding on real observations at every control step so policies stop drifting the way open-loop rollouts do, and borrowing the "model + harness" scaffolding pattern from coding agents and applying it to world modeling (a pilot agent plans behavior while a director agent seeds the environment). MarkTechPost's July 11 landscape frames the field around who actually ships: NVIDIA as the platform everyone builds on (Cosmos world models, Isaac Lab sim-to-real, GB300 compute), Robbyant as the open-source surge, and Google DeepMind, Physical Intelligence (the π0.5/π0.7 reference policies), Unitree, Figure + Tesla Optimus, and Agility Robotics as the leaders by open impact and real-world deployment. The open-vs-closed split mirrors the LLM world — China's labs are betting on open weights to lead the hardware wave, while US pure-plays keep vertically-integrated stacks closed and win on fleet-scale deployment data (Figure 03 in BMW plants, Agility's Digit moving 100k+ totes at Amazon).

A mid-July entry widens the field beyond China's open labs and the US pure-plays: Mistral released Robostral Navigate, an 8B model that steers a robot from a single RGB image plus a plain-language instruction — no LiDAR, no depth sensors, no map — hitting 76.6% on R2R-CE (validation unseen), built fully in-house and trained entirely in simulation on ~400,000 paths across 6,000 environments. Map-less, single-camera navigation is the cheapest hardware path to useful robots, so SOTA-class numbers on commodity sensors are what pull robotics toward hobbyist and low-cost commercial builds.

The through-line: embodied AI is consolidating into reusable open infrastructure — cross-embodiment action spaces, geometry-first perception, and self-grounding world models — rather than one-off per-robot policies. (This page splits out the physical-AI thread that grew inside Model releases across July 8–11; model-lineup detail still lives there, business framing on Industry & business, and the underlying methods on Research papers.)

Robbyant's open embodied stack

Ant Group's robotics unit Robbyant shipped a deliberately layered set of open releases meant to be composed, not used in isolation — described by MarkTechPost as covering "how a robot sees, how it acts, how it imagines the world, and how it controls its own body in real time." The four layers: LingBot-Vision (perception — dense spatial geometry), LingBot-VLA 2.0 (action — one policy across 20 embodiments), LingBot-World-Infinity (imagination — a controllable world model), and LingBot-VA 2.0 (real-time control — a video-action policy). All are open-weight and freely downloadable, which is what makes the release notable: it is the most aggressive open push in embodied AI so far, and a clear signal that China's labs intend to lead the hardware wave with open models the way they have been pushing open LLMs.

Perception foundations

LingBot-Vision is a 1B self-supervised Vision Transformer built for dense spatial perception — pixel-precise depth and surface boundaries — rather than the semantic-label invariance most vision backbones optimize for. It leads NYU-Depth v2 RMSE (ahead of Meta's DINOv3) and reportedly rivals or beats foundation models up to 7× larger on dense tasks. The design bet is that robots don't fail because they can't name an object; they fail because they don't know precisely where its edges are in 3D space, so prioritizing geometry over semantics is the right base layer for everything stacked on top. (Release detail on the Model releases page.)

Vision-language-action & video-action policies

The scaling evidence under this whole category firmed up on Sep 11: larger video models with more pre-training compute produce better real robot performance, measured through Direct Video-Action models, and the gains are largest on hard physical tasks rather than concentrated on easy ones. Two details matter for anyone building here. First, the improvement tracks how well the model predicts held-out web video, which says the transferable thing being learned is general physical prediction rather than anything robot-specific — the assumption the cross-embodiment policies below rest on, now with a measurement behind it. Second, DINO FD, computed offline on the pre-trained model, predicts downstream robot efficiency, so a scaling decision can be made without building a policy and putting it on hardware. Two complementary control models sit on top of perception. LingBot-VLA 2.0 is a 6B vision-language-action model that maps 20 different robot configurations into one shared action space, trained on robot trajectories plus 10,000+ hours of egocentric video and targeting precision manipulation, long-horizon execution, and cross-embodiment generalization — a direct shot at robotics' hardest problem, which is not one smart robot but one model that works across every robot a company owns. LingBot-VA 2.0, billed as the industry's first embodied-native video-action world model, takes a different tack: an autoregressive diffusion model that predicts while acting, interleaving video and action tokens in a single causal sequence via a Mixture-of-Transformers and re-grounding on the robot's latest real observation every step. A partial-denoising trick plus an asynchronous inference pipeline let it run high-frequency closed-loop control, reportedly outperforming SOTA policies — including Physical Intelligence's π0.5 — on long-horizon and precision manipulation. Re-grounding on reality every step (while staying causal) is what the release argues separates a lab demo from a deployable robot. Physical Intelligence's π0.5/π0.7 remain the reference cross-embodiment policies most new VLA work benchmarks against. (Method angle on the Research papers page.)

World models

LingBot-World-Infinity (the roundup name for what the July 10 brief covered as LingBot-World 2.0) is described as the only interactive world model to hit hour-level, effectively infinite generation without visual drift, running coherently for a full hour on a single GPU. A distilled real-time variant drives 720p at 60fps, and it ships as a 14B model plus a 1.3B single-GPU version. Its distinguishing feature is an agentic harness: a pilot agent plans character behavior while a director agent seeds new environment elements, turning a passive frame predictor into a self-sustaining world. The conceptual leap is borrowing the "model + harness" pattern from coding agents (Codex-style scaffolding) and applying it to world modeling — a robot that can imagine the near future can plan against it. This sits alongside NVIDIA's open Cosmos 3 world-foundation-model family (see Model releases) as the open-weight side of world modeling. (Architecture angle on the Research papers page.) Black Forest Labs added a generative-modeling angle on the same convergence (July 28): a flow-based model extended to drive robot control, per this week's MarkTechPost roundup — reported only as a headline item with no further technical detail available. The commercial weight behind this thread grew sharply in late August. Veeda — ex-NVIDIA researchers including Sanja Fidler, with University of Toronto ties — raised a $90 million seed led by Khosla Ventures and Radical Ventures to build multimodal world models, one of the largest seed rounds ever raised by a Canadian startup, and by the reporting there are now around half a dozen comparably funded world-model startups.

ByteDance adds a fourth serious platform entrant alongside Runway, World Labs and Google, preparing a real-time spatial video model that generates interactive worlds responding to Pico VR users' voice and movement, possibly within the month. The distinction that matters across all of them is the one Atlas and Solaris already drew: generating a world is not the same as generating one that stays consistent while someone acts inside it, and only the interactive ones are useful as training environments. NVIDIA's WorldTrace (Aug 12) attacks the consistency half directly and cheaply. Long autoregressive video rollouts degrade because compressed memories of earlier state fall outside the positional range the model was trained on; WorldTrace assigns those memories stable, in-distribution positional offsets so they stay reachable however far the rollout runs. It requires no retraining, which matters more than the mechanism: it means rollout coherence can be improved on models that already exist, rather than being fixed on the next training run.

That does not by itself make a generated world interactive — it addresses whether the world remembers, not whether it responds correctly to being acted upon — but memory over long horizons is a precondition for the interactive case, and it has been the cheaper of the two to get wrong.

Odyssey-3 (Sep 16) makes the largest scope claim this section has recorded and supplies the least evidence for it. Odyssey Systems describes a foundation world model on an autoregressive diffusion transformer, trained to simulate highly diverse scenarios and positioned as general-purpose physical intelligence — one learned understanding of physics, dynamics, cause and effect and human behaviour, applied to controlling robots, powering humanoids, driving vehicles, piloting drones, training other models and playing video games. The open question every entry above has been circling is exactly the one this asserts: whether physical understanding acquired in one domain transfers to another, or whether “understands physics” is a label over a set of domain-specific competences that share a vocabulary and nothing else. Odyssey publishes no cross-domain benchmark, so the claim stands on the architecture and the framing. The architecture is the more interesting half regardless of how the breadth claim resolves. Autoregressive diffusion combines sequential commitment with iterative refinement, which maps onto the two properties the rollout work above keeps trading off — a policy needs to commit to an action now, and a simulator needs the frame to be right — and treating them as separable rather than as a single knob is a real design position, whatever the generality turns out to be worth.

The competitive landscape

MarkTechPost's July 11 ranking of who is "actually leading in physical AI" — scored on model quality, open-source impact, and real deployment rather than valuation or press — reads: 1. NVIDIA (the platform: Cosmos world models, the GB300 stack, Isaac Lab sim-to-real; wins by powering everyone else); 2. Ant Group / Robbyant (the open-source surge: four open models in five days); 3. Google DeepMind (the generalist: Gemini Robotics and the RT lineage, but mostly closed); 4. Physical Intelligence (π) (the pure-play: π0.5/π0.7 reference policies, founded 2024 by Stanford's Chelsea Finn, openpi on GitHub); 5. Unitree Robotics (accessible hardware); 6. Figure AI + Tesla Optimus (the deployment bet); 7. Agility Robotics (the warehouse proof). The recurring divide is open vs. closed: the top open players (Robbyant, π, Unitree) publish weights and SDKs the field can build on, while the vertically-integrated hardware players (Figure, Tesla) keep model stacks closed and compete on proprietary fleet-scale training data. (Business framing on the Industry & business page.) That "mostly closed" framing aged quickly: with Gemini Robotics 2 (July 30) — whole-body control, dexterity, and multi-robot collaboration across three shipped models, including an on-device VLA — DeepMind is now releasing physical-AI models at the cadence of the labs ranked around it, not just publishing research. The driving segment is now being contested with the same architecture.

Alibaba's Qwen-Drive (Sep 8) is a vision-language foundation model for autonomous driving trained in stages that integrate perception, language and planning objectives, and the claim it makes is about retention rather than peak performance: it reaches specialized driving competence while keeping broad visual understanding and instruction-following, on a recommended 24GB+ GPU. That is the same argument the cross-embodiment VLA work makes — that specialization should not cost generality — applied to the one embodiment with a mature, safety-critical incumbent stack to displace. Underneath the whole landscape sits a supply question that August made visible: tens of thousands of workers in India are being paid to film their own jobs in first person so the footage can train robots to do them. The bottleneck is not hardware and increasingly not algorithms — it is that first-person video of ordinary manual work does not exist at scale, because until recently there was no reason to record it. Unlike text, this corpus cannot be scraped; it has to be commissioned, which turns training data into a recurring operating cost and a labour supply chain rather than a one-time acquisition. That is a structural advantage for whoever can commission at scale, and it goes some way to explaining the geography of the competitive picture above.

Hardware & real-world deployment

Beyond models, the credibility test is robots doing real work. Unitree ships the most accessible full-size humanoids — the G1 from $16,000 and the research-grade H1 at $90K, both with open SDKs, ROS 2 support, and 3D LiDAR — and has demonstrated reliability with a 1.9km autonomous marathon (April 2026) and synchronized multi-robot performances. Figure AI has Figure 03 live in BMW's Spartanburg and Leipzig plants (12,000 units/year capacity) and raised $675M from NVIDIA, Microsoft, Jeff Bezos, and OpenAI; Tesla Optimus targets 50,000+ units at its own factories — both winning on the flywheel of proprietary data generated by deployed robots. Agility Robotics is the quiet proof-of-concept: 75 Digit units inside Amazon's Spanaway, WA fulfillment center (as of April 2026) have moved over 100,000 totes, and Agility became the first humanoid-robot company to go public ($2.5B deal). Moving 100k totes in a live warehouse is, per the brief, the most credible deployment data in the field.

Two September datapoints extend the deployment picture at opposite price points. XPeng began production of its IRON humanoid on a line running more than 80% of core processes automatically, with mass production targeted by the end of the year — the automation percentage being the harder claim, since it speaks to unit economics rather than to whether the robot walks convincingly. At the other end, Hugging Face's Microduck puts a complete sim-to-real loop at $399: a 25cm open-source robot that picks up 800g with its beak, rights itself after a fall, crouches and skates, with behaviours trained in simulation and deployed directly to hardware, and the SDK, simulator and full RL training stack published. The field's data flywheel has always favoured whoever has robots in the world; a $399 platform with the training stack attached is the first credible attempt to widen who can spin one. August produced the sharpest available contrast between demonstration and deployment. The Beijing World Humanoid Robot Games put 2,000+ robots through 51 events and 1,000+ competitions, with Chinese humanoids breaking human records including the 100-metre sprint — on tasks that are controlled and repeatable, which is where robots have always done best, and experts on the record said mass deployment remains distant. Unitree's founder named the threshold that would falsify that caution: 80% of voice-instructed tasks completed in unfamiliar surroundings, two to ten years away, with the specific failure being that execution collapses when objects or surroundings shift slightly.

On the road the picture is inverted, because the technology is ahead of the paperwork: Amazon's Zoox carries public passengers in vehicles with no dashboard, pedals or steering wheel across San Francisco and Las Vegas, while Tesla's Cybercab — equally control-free — lacks the approval to do so. Waymo added its own 5nm silicon to cut the latency between raw sensor data and driver response, a vertical-integration move made from a position of 220.6 million rider-only miles rather than in place of them.

Two Sep 16 releases attack the two constraints that actually gate humanoid deployment, and neither is about capability. Agility Robotics showed a humanoid built to work beside people without a cage, taking precautions autonomously when it detects someone at a distance — moving aside, standing still to let them pass, or squatting into a seated position when a person comes too close — with early access in the first half of 2027 and general availability by the end of it. The graded response is the design worth isolating, and the squat in particular, because a stopped humanoid is still a heavy mass at head height and lowering the centre of gravity is a materially different safety claim from halting. The constraint it targets is the separation distance a safety certification demands, which is what keeps humanoids in fenced cells rather than any deficiency in what they can do. What is missing is everything a safety case is built from: sensing range, false-negative rate, and the standard the behaviour is being certified against. OpenArm from Enactic addresses the other constraint by publishing an open-source humanoid arm for teleoperation, imitation learning and physical AI research. Set against the recruitment of tens of thousands of human demonstrators described above, the point is not that the hardware is cheap but that it is shared: the expensive input to every policy on this page is the demonstration, and demonstrations collected on bespoke per-lab rigs carry those rigs' idiosyncrasies into the dataset, so a common platform is what makes one group's trajectories worth anything to another. No performance or durability figures were published with it.

Low-cost & map-less navigation

A cost-down thread runs alongside the full embodied stacks: getting useful navigation out of the cheapest possible sensor suite. Mistral's Robostral Navigate (8B, the lab's first embodied model) takes an RGB image plus a plain-language instruction and moves a robot through a space with no LiDAR, no depth sensors, and no pre-built map, reaching 76.6% on R2R-CE (validation unseen). It was built entirely in-house and trained purely in simulation on ~400,000 paths across 6,000 environments — evidence that vision-language navigation can be learned in sim and deployed on commodity cameras. The significance is economic: hitting SOTA-class numbers on a single RGB camera removes the sensor bill-of-materials that keeps most capable robots in the lab, and it marks a European frontier lab entering a field so far led by China's open releases and the US hardware pure-plays.
