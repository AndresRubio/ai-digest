Research papers

Last updated: 2026-09-18

Current state

Today's two papers both remove a structural assumption rather than tune one. Sakana AI's PC-ALM is a layer-local learning rule with no global backward pass that trains networks a thousand layers deep, reaching 77.75% on the repository's reference cell against 78.66% for backpropagation and 68.13% for standard predictive coding. Closing nine tenths of the gap to backprop while discarding the backward pass is the result; backprop still winning, on small image benchmarks that are the only place either was tested, is the qualification. The MIT-licensed JAX code running on CPU is substantive rather than incidental, because the case for local rules has always been a hardware case — a rule that never needs the whole graph resident has a different memory and parallelism story, and depth is where that is supposed to pay. Nobody has yet shown it holding at a scale where the saving would be worth collecting.

Meta's FLAT removes a different assumption: that the retrieval encoder and the generative decoder need separate representations of the same image. It maps images and text into one flexible-length sequence of continuous tokens serving both, with nested dropout ordering information coarse to fine along the sequence. The ordering is what makes variable length useful rather than merely possible — early tokens carry the coarse content, so truncating degrades detail gracefully instead of dropping an arbitrary slice, and a caller decides at inference time how much computation an image is worth. Multimodal stacks have not had that knob; the usual alternative is a model per resolution or a patch budget fixed during training. The open question is the one every shared-representation result raises: retrieval rewards discarding, generation rewards preserving, and no head-to-head against separate specialised encoders is reported.

Today's paper attacks recursive self-improvement at the layer its advocates usually skip. Dream-RSI argues the binding constraint is exploration rather than the improvement step itself, and closes the loop there: online exploration collects discovery histories, replay simulators are built from them over the search space already realised, meta-exploration strategy is refined against those simulators in a phase the authors call dreaming, and the upgraded policy is redeployed online — with reported gains in both the effectiveness and efficiency of discovery across several settings. The idea worth extracting is that an exploration history is a cheap environment rather than only a log, so strategy can be rehearsed at no new environment cost and only the redeployed policy spends real budget. That inverts the usual economics of self-improvement, where every candidate has to be tried for real. It also inherits this page's standing caveat in a sharper form: a policy tuned against a simulator assembled from its own history can improve on that distribution while learning nothing transferable, and until the settings are enumerated there is no way to distinguish the two. It sits beside Noam Brown's statement the same day that recursive self-improvement is OpenAI's clear priority — one a published mechanism, the other a stated intention.

Earlier cycles

The prior cycle's paper questioned the one layer every other entry on this page takes for granted. Sakana's PC-ALM (Sep 15) is a local alternative to backpropagation: it extends predictive coding by giving each layer a feedback control dynamical system that distributes and propagates supervision credit, and on that basis trains residual MLPs up to 1,000 layers while nearly matching backprop's performance using only layer-local dynamics. Depth is the right axis to judge it on, because local-learning rules characteristically work shallow and degrade as depth grows — which is precisely where a global backward pass earns its cost — and 1,000 layers is far past where earlier rules stopped competing. Two limits belong with it: the architecture is residual MLPs rather than transformers, so it says nothing yet about frontier training, and no wall-clock or energy comparison is claimed, which is the whole practical reason to want a rule that needs no global backward pass. Everything else on this page — attention, decoding, retrieval, world models — sits on an optimisation procedure no other entry has thought to question.

Both sides of the Navier–Stokes attribution dispute have now published timelines, and the numbers change what the argument is about. OpenAI says it heard the rumour on Sep 1, launched agents at every open Millennium Prize problem, reached a resolution on Sep 5 roughly 88 hours later, and spent a further 17 hours on Lean formalization via GPT-6 Astra. Across all attempted problems the agents sent 4.9 million messages and used about 300 billion output tokens — Navier–Stokes alone taking 2.7 million messages and 130 billion — a campaign costing on the order of $15 million at Astra’s public rates, though the internal model’s economics are not disclosed. It states that no researcher or agent saw the other team’s work before publication and no specific user data was accessed, while conceding it cannot rule out that de-identified data derived from their year of Codex usage improved its models. Buckmaster says he asked twice whether the model was trained on those sessions and was answered only about lookup. OpenAI offered a joint announcement, but told him Alpöge could not be a co-author because he works for Anthropic — which is the detail that converts a provenance question into an institutional one.

The generalisation is more uncomfortable than the incident. If hearing that a problem has been solved is enough to trigger a $15 million agent campaign that solves it independently four days later, then a rumour is now an actionable input, and the same dynamic is already visible in security, where knowing a vulnerability exists is enough to set agents hunting for it. Terence Tao draws the conclusion for mathematics directly: the incentive now points away from sharing promising research directions at all, which would reverse centuries of open-science practice. The method side of this page stays unmoved by all of it — no new result was produced — but it does sharpen the standard the page has been applying. The Fermat proof recorded below is a machine-checkable Lean development whose priority nobody disputes; a Lean formalization demonstrates that an argument is valid, not that it was the prover’s to make, and only publication order settles the second question. This cycle’s one genuine architecture arrival, the Recurrent Looped Transformer (Sep 14), is a useful contrast in candour: a causal encoder paired with a recurrent decoder carrying its final hidden state and a layerwise sliding-window cache across every prompt and response token, so reasoning depth is unbounded in time rather than fixed by stacked layers — published with reasoning gains, hardware efficiency and RL scaling all explicitly listed as not yet established.

The cycle's own contribution is an inference about a closed model rather than a result from an open one. Sebastian Raschka argues (Sep 10) that GPT-6 Astra most likely uses a variant of the looped transformer — reusing a block of layers for a variable number of passes instead of stacking depth — buying better modelling performance at a fixed compute budget and moving computation inside the architecture rather than across emitted tokens, with computer use the capability where the leap is largest. The reading that changes how the model should be handled concerns its unusually short reasoning traces: on this account brevity is a symptom of a model making fewer mistakes and reasoning internally, not a trained stylistic preference, so trace length now says much less about effort than it used to. He is explicit that this is inference from published behaviour, not disclosure. Set beside the recurrent-depth concern, the two describe one property from two sides — the architecture that improves performance per unit of compute is the architecture that moves reasoning out of the artifact a monitor can read.

It shares that discipline with the August papers carried here for how sharply they bound their own claims. Specula automates bug finding by writing a specification rather than reading code — deriving TLA+ specs from an implementation, validating conformance by trace, model-checking for concurrency bugs, then reproducing each at the code layer as an integration test with the timing needed to trigger it. The accompanying analysis is unusually direct about the limit: it works for what it does, but it sidesteps composition, so it says nothing about whether per-module guarantees add up to a system-level guarantee. That is exactly where formal verification has always stopped, and naming it is more useful than the tool. The second is a static retrieval model trained on 660 million pairs and quantized hard enough to fit in 8MB, which embeds the whole of English Wikipedia on a laptop in under eight minutes. Static embeddings give up the contextual sensitivity of a transformer encoder; what comes back is retrieval with no serving infrastructure, no GPU and no per-query cost, which changes which applications can justify retrieval at all rather than making existing ones better.

The headline result of the previous cycle was also the one with the largest asterisk. OpenAI says an internal system resolved the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem, showing that smooth three-dimensional fluid dynamics can develop a finite-time singularity, and produced both an analytical proof and a Lean formalization — reportedly from about 10,000 agents exchanging 2.7 million messages and generating 130 billion output tokens over 88 hours. It has not been independently reviewed or accepted by the Clay Mathematics Institute, and NYU's Tristan Buckmaster says his team had already fed related unpublished drafts into OpenAI's Codex, with OpenAI declining to say whether those drafts entered training data. Coming two days after Claude's computer-verified Fermat proof, the pair mark a real change in what these systems produce and, simultaneously, the moment the field's evaluation machinery stopped being adequate to check it: a Lean formalization proves the argument is valid, not that the argument was the model's to make. Provenance is now the load-bearing question in AI mathematics, and no lab currently publishes enough to answer it.

DeepMind's AlphaGenome Atlas makes the opposite kind of contribution — one petabyte of precomputed predictions covering the regulatory effect of all 9 billion possible single-nucleotide human variants, each with a single impact score, queryable without writing code or running the model. Shipping a model as a lookup table is a distribution decision rather than a research one, and it is the decision that determines whether a genomics lab without an inference budget can use the work at all. On architecture, two quieter items push at the same constraint from different budgets: Microsoft's full-bandwidth transformers feed the previous token's top-layer hidden state back in alongside the next token embedding so latent computation survives the token boundary — an extra input rather than a rewrite — and a detailed walkthrough of Thinking Machines' Inkling documents a frontier model that admits images and audio with no separately pretrained encoder in front of them, which is a rare fully specified alternative to the standard bolted-on vision tower.

The decoding-side result worth carrying forward this cycle is Speculative Programmatic Tool Calling: predict the likely tool calls from partially generated code and launch them early, so searches, sub-agents and API calls run alongside token generation rather than after it. The authors frame it as a JIT compiler for the agent loop and report a 1–1.2× runtime speed-up, concentrated exactly where the overlap is real — memory-bound local models and high-volume serving. The modest multiplier is the honest part: this is a scheduling win, not an architectural one, and it only pays where tool latency and generation latency are comparable.

Before it, two August papers share a shape: both take a task an LLM does badly in one pass and decompose it into an evaluate-then-act loop. Agentic ASR (Shanghai Jiao Tong, Zhejiang, Fudan, Xiaoice) stops asking a model to rewrite a transcript and instead has it classify each turn as confirmation, addition, or correction, then locate the span, infer the intent, and apply the edit — an editor rather than a rewriter — cutting semantic error from 21.5% to 3.5% on GigaSpeech and 28.6% to 1.4% on code-switched ASRU2019 over ten turns. Stanford's SecureForge applies the same separation to code security, using the GEPA genetic algorithm with a static analyzer's vulnerability reports as the fitness signal to evolve the developer-editable system prompt, and halving vulnerable output (11.8% against 20.1%) relative to simply asking for secure code and naming the risks — across ten models from CodeLlama 7B to Claude Sonnet 4.6. The same decomposition shows up in the clinical trial on the Applications page, where splitting each turn into an evaluate call and a generate call is what stops the model agreeing with the user instead of challenging them.

The method set now spans two poles: infrastructure-flavoured work that makes existing models cheaper, more trainable, or better grounded, and — newly — a domain foundation model showing the self-supervised JEPA recipe scaling on raw, unlabeled real-world data. That newest result is NeuroVFM, a neuroimaging foundation model from the University of Michigan trained with Vol-JEPA (a volumetric joint-embedding predictive architecture in Yann LeCun's JEPA line) on 5.24M uncurated clinical MRI and CT volumes — pulled straight from hospital systems with no radiology-report labels and no curation — learning anatomy and pathology directly from raw data; published in Nature Medicine with open weights, it is a template for turning idle clinical archives into foundation models (medical-deployment angle on the Applications page).

On the infrastructure axis, the standout architecture result is MiniMax Sparse Attention (MSA): a two-branch block-sparse attention (a local branch for recent context plus a global branch for long-range dependencies) that cuts per-token attention compute at 1M context by 28.4× versus dense GQA with on-par quality, yielding 14.2× faster prefill and 7.6× faster decode on an H800, trained on a 3T-token / 109B MoE. Crucially it is GQA-native, so it drops into existing architectures without a redesign — the kind of architecture-level result that tends to propagate into every long-context serving stack within a year (serving angle on the Infrastructure page). A second methodologically interesting release is Liquid AI's LFM2.5 embedding/ColBERT pair, built by patching a causal decoder into a bidirectional encoder rather than training a retrieval encoder from scratch — an unusual reuse of a 28T-token generative pretrain that nonetheless beats larger dedicated embedding models (model detail on the Model releases page).

These join the standing method set: NVIDIA Polar (RL on black-box coding agents), EAGLE 3.1 (speculative decoding without attention drift), Harness-1 (RL trained inside a stateful search harness, see Training & RL), and Flash-KMeans, an IO-aware exact K-Means that applies the FlashAttention "redesign-around-GPU-memory" recipe to a classical clustering primitive for ~200× speedups over FAISS.

The July embodied-AI wave added two architecture ideas worth tracking as methods: Robbyant's LingBot-VA 2.0 interleaves video and action tokens in one causal sequence via a Mixture-of-Transformers and re-grounds on the robot's real observation every step (a "predict-while-acting" loop that reportedly beats π0.5 on long-horizon manipulation), while LingBot-World-Infinity wraps a causal world model in an agentic harness — a pilot agent plans while a director agent seeds the environment — importing the coding-agent "model + harness" pattern into world modeling (full detail on the Embodied AI & robotics page).

The empirical standout remains a Perplexity/Harvard field study of autonomous agents versus chat in real knowledge work: over three months, workers using an autonomous agent finished tasks in 87% less time at 94% lower cost than search alone, with higher satisfaction — a rare quantified measurement of agent productivity beyond benchmark scores.

Framing the architecture side, a July 15 interview with Sebastian Raschka (tied to his new 440-page book on LLM architecture) reads the 2026 landscape as three converging shifts: inference-time scaling moving capability from the weights into the agent harness around them, hybrid architectures (attention interleaved with state-space / linear-attention layers) becoming the long-context default, and fine-tuning economics collapsing far enough that open models now set the pace at which ideas spread across the ecosystem.

Learning rules beyond backpropagation

Local learning rules have always worked at toy depth and collapsed as networks grow — which is exactly where backprop’s global backward pass earns its cost. Judge entries here on depth first.

2026-09-15

Sakana’s PC-ALM trains 1,000-layer networks with a layer-local rule and no backward pass

Augmented Lagrangian Predictive Coding extends standard predictive coding, which couples layers diffusively, by giving each layer a feedback control dynamical system that distributes and propagates supervision credit through the network. It reaches 77.75% on the repository’s reference cell against 78.66% for backpropagation and 68.13% for standard predictive coding — nine tenths of the gap closed while removing the global backward pass entirely — and that holds for residual MLPs up to 1,000 layers, well past where earlier local rules stopped competing.

Two limits travel with it: residual MLPs rather than transformers, so nothing here yet bears on frontier training, and no wall-clock or energy comparison — which is the whole reason to want a local rule. Testing is on small image benchmarks only, and backprop still wins there. MIT-licensed JAX, runs on CPU, which is itself the point: a rule that never needs the whole graph resident has a different memory and parallelism story.

Attention & long-context architecture

Two constraints sit on the same mechanism: what attention costs per token once context is long, and the fact that latent computation is thrown away at every token boundary. Judge cost results on whether quality held at the claimed context length, and state-carrying results on whether anything was measured beyond the proposal.

2026-09-14

Recurrent Looped Transformer carries latent state across every prompt and response token

The Recurrent Looped Transformer pairs a causal encoder with a recurrent decoder that carries its final hidden state, and a layerwise sliding-window attention cache, across every prompt and response token rather than resetting per step. The encoder builds a global key–value memory while the decoder extends one continuous latent computation as the sequence grows, so reasoning depth is unbounded in time rather than fixed by the number of stacked layers.

The authors present it as three kinds of co-design at once — latent reasoning with unbounded temporal depth, model with hardware, and model with RL algorithm — and are explicit that reasoning gains, hardware efficiency and RL scaling all remain to be established. That candour is the reason to record it: it is published as a proposal with its claims unmade rather than as a result.

Project page · via TLDR AI (Sep 14)
2026-09-07

Random Attention — uniformly sampling the KV cache matches learned eviction, with less overhead

Salesforce AI Research kept a uniformly sampled subset of generated KV-cache entries instead of relying on learned importance signals or attention statistics. Across several reasoning benchmarks and model families it matched or exceeded more complex eviction methods while cutting the eviction overhead itself.

A negative result about the value of cleverness: if random selection is competitive with importance estimation, the estimation machinery in deployed cache-eviction schemes may be paying for itself in overhead rather than quality.

2026-08-17

Microsoft’s full-bandwidth transformers let latent computation continue across decoding steps

Full-bandwidth transformers feed the previous token’s top-layer hidden state back into the model alongside the next token embedding. The consequence is that computation is no longer forced to terminate at each token boundary — latent state carries forward instead of being discarded and reconstructed from the token alone. It attacks the same constraint as recurrent-depth designs and test-time training, from the cheapest possible angle: an extra input rather than an architectural rewrite or a per-user weight set.

Retrieval-model architecture

Retrieval keeps being rebuilt out of parts meant for something else — a generative decoder, a shared multimodal sequence, a quantized table of static vectors. The question each answers differently is what a representation must preserve, and what serving it costs. Judge them on the concession, which is always stated somewhere.

2026-09-17

Meta’s FLAT puts images and text in one variable-length continuous token sequence

FLAT encodes images and text into the same flexible-length sequence of continuous tokens, usable for both retrieval and generation, and applies nested dropout so that information is ordered coarse to fine across the sequence. The consequence is a knob that did not previously exist: a caller varies the number of tokens spent on an image and trades computation against visual detail at inference time, without a separate model per resolution. Both halves of the multimodal stack — the retrieval encoder and the generative decoder — read the same representation, which removes the usual translation step between them.

The unresolved question is the one every shared-representation result raises: retrieval rewards representations that discard, generation rewards representations that preserve. No head-to-head against separate specialised encoders is reported.

Decoding & inference

Both entries buy latency by guessing ahead and paying for the misses — tokens in one case, whole tool calls in the other. The number to look at is not the speed-up but the cost of a wrong guess, which rises sharply once the speculated work has side effects.

2026-08-25

Speculative Programmatic Tool Calling — launch the tool call before the code is written

sPTC predicts likely tool calls from partially generated code and pre-launches them, so searches, sub-agents and API calls run alongside token generation instead of after it. The authors describe it as a JIT compiler for the agent loop and report a 1–1.2× runtime speed-up, concentrated where it should be: memory-bound local models and high-volume serving, where generation and tool latency can genuinely overlap.

The modest multiplier is the honest part of the result — this is a scheduling win, not an architectural one. The failure mode is the one speculation always has: a mispredicted call is work done for nothing, which matters more when the call has side effects than when it is a wasted forward pass.

IO-aware kernels & data-pipeline primitives

The FlashAttention move — redesign an algorithm around the memory hierarchy rather than the FLOP count — generalises past attention to the classical primitives underneath a retrieval stack. Judge these on whether the result stays exact, because an approximate speed-up is a different and much easier claim.

2026-06-16

Flash-KMeans — IO-aware exact K-Means, ~200× over FAISS

An IO-aware reimplementation of exact K-Means using Triton GPU kernels with batched, memory-efficient access patterns, redesigning the algorithm around the GPU’s SRAM/HBM hierarchy. Standard implementations, FAISS included, are IO-bound and waste cycles on redundant memory transfers. Results are exact, not approximate; reported ~200× faster than FAISS, ~30× than cuML, and 17.9× end-to-end on an H200.

Because K-Means underpins vector quantization, embedding clustering and RAG index building, the result is directly useful for retrieval pipelines; open source on GitHub. (Serving/cost angle on the Infrastructure page.)

World models & embodied-policy methods

Robotics is importing the language-model agent’s two ideas wholesale: model everything as one causal sequence, then wrap the model in a harness that plans. Judge these on the closed loop — whether the policy re-grounds on a real observation, and whether the world model drifts once nobody is watching.

Robbyant LingBot-VA 2.0 & LingBot-World-Infinity — predict-while-acting and agentic-harness world models

Two embodied methods from Ant Group’s Robbyant. LingBot-World-Infinity wraps a causal world model in an agentic harness — a pilot agent plans character behaviour while a director agent seeds new environment elements — to sustain hour-scale generation without visual drift, porting the coding-agent “model + harness” pattern into world modeling. RL on agents

Training an agent means training inside the harness it will run in, not against a static task. The entries differ in what they had to build to get there — a proxy, a stateful search environment, a simulator made of past trajectories. Judge each on what the training environment omits.

2026-09-16

Dream-RSI turns an agent’s own discovery history into a replay simulator it can rehearse in

Dream-RSI argues that progress in recursive self-improvement is limited by exploration rather than by the improvement step, and closes the loop at the exploration layer. It collects discovery histories through online exploration, builds replay simulators from them over the search space already realised, refines its meta-exploration strategy against those simulators — the step the authors call dreaming — and redeploys the upgraded policy online, reporting gains in both the effectiveness and the efficiency of discovery across several settings.

The reusable idea is that an exploration history is not just a log but a cheap environment: rehearsing strategy against past trajectories costs nothing new, and only the redeployed policy spends real budget. The standing caveat for self-improvement results applies with force here, since a policy tuned against a simulator built from its own history can improve on that distribution while learning nothing transferable, and “several settings” is doing load-bearing work until the settings are enumerated.

Architecture analysis & practitioner synthesis

Writeups rather than releases: a closed model read from its behaviour, an open one read from its code, a tool read by its own critic. The useful ones name what they cannot see. Judge them on whether the author separates inference from disclosure — each of these does, explicitly.

2026-09-10

Raschka reads GPT-6 Astra as a looped transformer, and shorter traces as a symptom rather than a choice

Sebastian Raschka’s architecture analysis argues GPT-6 Astra most likely uses a variant of the looped transformer — reusing a block of layers for a variable number of passes rather than adding depth — which yields better modelling performance at a fixed compute budget and lets the model spend more computation inside the architecture instead of across emitted tokens. He also singles out computer use as where the leap is largest.

The reading that matters operationally is his account of the model’s notably short reasoning traces: on this analysis they are a side effect of a model that makes fewer mistakes and can reason internally, not a deliberately trained brevity. It is inference from published behaviour rather than a disclosed design, and he says so; read next to the recurrent-depth concern, the same property that makes the model cheaper to run makes it harder to read.

2026-08-19

Inkling’s architecture is documented: no separate encoder for images or audio, and a thinking-effort dial

A detailed walkthrough of Thinking Machines’ Inkling — the lab’s first model trained from scratch, with Apache 2.0 weights on Hugging Face — covers its architecture, position encoding, thinking-effort setting, and the choice to admit images and audio without a separately pretrained encoder in front of them.

CRC Monitor — one calibrated safety score matches a whole history of them

Mona Schirmer, Metod Jazbec and colleagues (Amsterdam, Wisconsin-Madison, Johns Hopkins) show that streaming safety monitors analysing a series of per-step scores can be replaced by a single check: compare the latest step’s verifier score against a threshold calibrated by conformal risk control, padding the measured false-alarm rate so the guarantee holds on data the calibration set never saw.

On FineHarm at a 20% user-set false-alarm budget it caught 99.5% of harmful outputs, matching the multi-score baseline while flagging after 14% of the conversation. On MATH it detected ~80% of incorrect solutions, equal to the baseline, after 35% of the reasoning rather than 40%. The one clear loss is on Anthropic Red Teaming (32% detected against 54%) — but it alarms after 26% of the conversation instead of 55%, which is the trade the paper is actually proposing: catch less, catch it far earlier, and pay less inference to do it.

Agentic ASR — treating transcription as an editing loop instead of a single pass

Researchers at Shanghai Jiao Tong, Zhejiang, Fudan and Xiaoice pair a speech-to-text engine (Qwen3-ASR-1.7B) with an LLM (Qwen3-32B) that classifies each user turn as confirmation, new input, or correction, and on a correction decomposes the fix into three steps: locate the span, infer what the user meant, apply the edit. The insight is that asking an LLM to rewrite a transcript wholesale introduces fresh errors, especially on names — acting as an editor beats acting as a rewriter.

They propose S²ER, the share of attempts that fail to preserve intended meaning: on GigaSpeech it fell from 21.5% to 3.5% over 10 turns, on the name-heavy AISHELL-NER from 19.9% to 2.0%, and on the code-switching ASRU2019 from 28.6% to 1.4%, with most of the gain arriving in the first few turns.

SecureForge — optimizing the system prompt cuts LLM-generated security flaws almost in half

Houjun Liu, Lisa Einstein, John Jang and colleagues at Stanford show that asking a model to write secure code barely helps, but searching for the right system prompt does. They turned the top 25 MITRE CWE entries into benign-looking coding requests, kept the 500 that actually produced vulnerable code, expanded 250 of them into 80,000 variants, and used the GEPA genetic algorithm with Semgrep’s vulnerability reports as the fitness signal to evolve the developer-editable portion of the system prompt.

Across ten models from CodeLlama 7B to Claude Sonnet 4.6 and GPT-5.4, SecureForge produced flawed code 11.8% of the time against 20.1% for explicitly asking for secure code and listing the issues to avoid. Freely available for commercial use; the stated limit is that it was optimized against known vulnerability classes, so its reach on unknown ones is untested.
