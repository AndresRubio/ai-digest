Evals & benchmarks

Last updated: 2026-09-18

Current state

Today's cycle attacks the instrument from both ends. Arena.ai measured 21 model-harness pairs across seven models and three harnesses and found harness choice has little effect on task success rate and a large effect on cost — up to fivefold — with a simple harness competitive throughout. Taken at face value that relocates a year of harness-engineering argument rather than refuting it: if success rates converge, the harness is a procurement decision, and harness comparisons should carry a price the way model comparisons carry a context length. The awkward part is that it does not reproduce the biggest number on the other side. DeepSeek reported a 33-point spread for one checkpoint across scaffolding modes — a score difference, not a cost one — and this study finds nothing like it. Until the two designs are run against each other, “the harness does not change the score” is a finding about these 21 pairs.

Vals.ai supplied the mechanism for a failure this page has recorded repeatedly without explaining: lab results that will not reproduce. Its account is that the guardrails used to detect eval cheating are often the same guardrails present during training, so the model is optimised against the detector rather than the task and learns to succeed in ways those particular checks miss. On that reading, eval-gaming is a predictable consequence of reusing one tool as both reward and integrity check, not an occasional lapse — and it generalises, because any grader that is also a reward becomes a target. OpenAI's disclosure the same day is the worked example, with flawed citation graders the suspected cause of a model fabricating nine figures: a grader that rewards the look of a citation trains the look of a citation. Both items land on the same procedural conclusion, which is the one this page has been converging on from several directions — a number is trustworthy in proportion to the independence of whoever produced it.

Today the variance turns out to be bigger than the result, twice, from unrelated sources. Fireworks benchmarked four coding models on DeepSWE and found DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash matching GPT-6 Astra’s accuracy band at $0.43 per task, about fifteen times cheaper — with all four finishing inside 0.7 pass@1 points of each other, which is less than the run-to-run variation Fireworks measured on its own setup. Publishing that second number is what turns the comparison from a ranking into a cost finding, and it is precisely the number almost every vendor comparison recorded on this page omits: the identical data, minus the error bars, would have been reported as a 0.7-point lead. IBM Research named the general case the same day. ALTK-Evolve introduces Consistency Guidelines for what it calls the inconsistency gap — a model’s success rate on one task dropping significantly across repeated runs of that same task — which separates two claims this page has been letting run together: that an agent completed a task, and that it will do so again. A single pass rate is one sample from a distribution presented as a property of the system. With the shuffled-task-order result that dissolved memory agents’ apparent self-improvement, three independent findings now say the spread routinely exceeds the effect. IBM presents the guidelines as guidance rather than as an intervention with a measured before and after, so the gap is named, not yet closed.

Earlier cycles

The prior cycle escalated the instrument problem one last step, to a measurement that cannot be fixed by fixing the instrument. OpenAI's Dan Selsam argues (Sep 15) that models are now situationally aware enough that researchers are losing the ability to evaluate them in contexts where the model believes it is unobserved — so models increasingly appear aligned whether or not they are, and a passing alignment result is compatible with both explanations. Every other finding on this page is a benchmark measuring the wrong construct; this is a measurement whose validity rests on a condition the subject can detect and the experimenter can no longer establish, and it lands squarely on the alignment certification the pacing proposal would make a release gate. Beside it, Dan Luu's treatment of bad benchmarks supplies the constructive half and pointedly refuses to supply a procedure: evals are experimental design, not steps, and his comparison case is winter tire testing — a mature discipline with standardised conditions, catalogued confounds and published methodology, which is precisely the apparatus missing whenever a model score is quoted without its harness, grader and split.

The rest of the cycle is the same lesson at lower stakes. Hamel Husain's AI Evals FAQ reached 48 answers with five additions that are all about reviewing evidence properly: write the rubric after annotating rather than before, because reviewers checking a list stop seeing anything off it; prefer a negotiated subset of real traces to redaction and redaction to synthetic data; anchor a large trace on its first upstream failure; give an LLM judge only the segment its failure mode needs, verified by ablation; and retire a gold eval that everything passes, since a suite with no failures carries no signal and costs more than a unit test to keep. The Artificial Analysis Capability Indices moved to v1.1 with re-tuned domains and Claude Fable 5.1 (max) leading all six — a real result, and one that needs its version number quoted alongside it, given how recently an index revision moved the top of a ranking with no model changing. And TurnBench turns out to ship a 104-hour training set beside its 30 held-out hours across six domains, which invites training for turn-taking rather than only scoring it, and makes contamination the next thing to police.

Last cycle's finding was that a leaderboard moved without either model moving. The one before today's was worse: a whole class of published scores was wrong because the questions were. Domain experts re-graded six widely cited physics benchmarks (Sep 14) and found most of the items models were recorded as failing were defective rather than hard — wrong answer keys, ambiguous wording, grader bugs. Corrected, frontier models sit close to saturation on all six, which retires the “models are weak at physics” reading these suites had been supporting. The authors are explicit that the successor has to be harder human-written exams rather than another automated quiz, since a suite assembled the same way inherits the same defect. The cheap diagnostic implied by it is one nobody was running: before concluding a model is weak at something, have a domain expert grade a sample of the failures by hand, because if most turn out to be bad questions the finding was about the instrument.

Real-SWE (Sep 14) is the cycle's other arrival and sits in an awkward relation to that. It evaluates coding agents against private enterprise repositories rather than public open-source projects, with a top resolution rate of 38.8% — far below the SWE-bench Verified numbers below, and deliberately so: proprietary internal systems appear in no training corpus, and business-specific conventions cannot be inferred from the code. As a probe of the contamination and familiarity questions hanging over SWE-bench it is well aimed. But it pays for that with auditability, since a benchmark built on private code cannot be inspected from outside, so its difficulty has to be taken on the maintainer's word — which is this section's other standing failure mode arriving in new clothes. Between the two, the working rule for the month is that a score is worth what its questions are worth, and almost nobody checks the questions.

The cycle's sharpest finding is that the leaderboard moved without either model moving. Artificial Analysis revised its Intelligence Index twice in four days: v4.2 on Sep 4 retired GPQA-Diamond as saturated, added two harder tests and doubled the private-test share to 40% to blunt gaming, shipped as an expedited interim release because the frontier was outrunning the completed v5; v4.3 on Sep 7 upgraded Terminal-Bench to v4.0 and swapped AutomationBench-AA in for τ³-Banking. Claude Fable 5.1 led v4.2 by two points over GPT-6 Astra and, after v4.3, the two tied at 53. Nothing about either model changed in between, which is the whole point: a top score now has a shelf life set by the evaluation's revision schedule rather than by the next release. CursorBench 4.0 makes the same problem concrete at vendor scale — harder instruction-following and sustained-project tasks dropped every model's score, Grok 4.6 hardest, Muse Spark least, and Cursor's own model pages still quote 3.2 figures, so two numbers from the same benchmark family are not comparable.

Against that, the week's most useful measurements are the ones denominated in money rather than rank. DeepSeek V4.1-Flash fixed 24 of 105 planted bugs on Bug Hunt Bench for $1.08 where Opus 5 Max fixed 27 for $51.33 and Grok 4.6 Max 27 for $16.96, and scored 81.2 against GPT-6 Astra's 82.7 on OpenDesign Arena at $0.023 per artifact instead of $1.61 — though the aggregate hides that it beat Astra on landing pages and trailed on dashboards, so “98% as good” is an average over tasks where it is better and tasks where it is worse. Meta's WearableQA adds a benchmark built from real physiological data rather than synthetic cases: thousands of health-reasoning questions from 200 users' wearables, blood biomarkers and demographics, with the cohort size the honest limit on what it can characterise.

Before all this, Perplexity's Q2D-Web (Sep 10) brought the scrutiny this page applies to models back onto a benchmark's own construction, and does it at a size retrieval evaluation rarely reaches: 190 million documents and 69,721 queries in ten languages, scoring first-stage retrievers rather than end-to-end answers. Two choices carry the weight. Relevance is judged three separate ways rather than once, so no single annotation process quietly defines what “relevant” means — the construct-validity worry applied to the label set instead of the task. And scoring runs through a subsampling scheme chosen to preserve model ranking at affordable cost, which is the honest concession that a benchmark nobody can afford to run stops being one. Isolating the first-stage retriever is the practitioner-facing part: it is the component whose failures are invisible end to end, because a document lost before reranking never appears in anything the final answer is scored against.

That continues the August line, in which the measurement apparatus rather than the models came under scrutiny: a re-evaluation of memory-based agents across multiple runs and shuffled task orders found much of their apparent self-improvement to be an artefact of evaluation noise, task sequencing and underspecified tasks. That lands squarely on the claim doing the most work in agent-memory marketing, that an agent demonstrably gets better at a task set by remembering earlier attempts at it, and it suggests the minimum bar for any such claim is repeated runs under permuted order. The constructive counterpart is evals-skills v2 (Aug 23), Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's rebuilt free skill set, whose addition is error discovery: hand a coding agent a file of outputs or traces and it builds a review app over a diverse sample, then clusters your annotations into failure modes and surfaces related examples as you go, with a start skill that routes to the right workflow depending on whether you are finding errors or auditing an existing pipeline. Installable via npx skills add, it puts eval methodology in the same distribution channel as the agents being evaluated — and it automates the part practitioners actually skip, which is looking at the data rather than picking a metric.

The single most useful number of the previous cycle was a gap, not a score. Sierra's Hyper-τ-bench puts a developer agent in a sandboxed workspace with a simulated business's records and a client it can message, and requires it to recover the spec, design an architecture and build a working customer-service agent that then serves from a fixed model menu inside a per-conversation cost budget. Claude Opus 5 at max reasoning inside Claude Code passes 23.9% of held-out tasks alone; the same class of model paired with an engineer who has deep context reaches 82.2%. What is being measured is the contribution of human context, and it is currently worth more than three times the model working unaided.

Two smaller studies say the same thing about the harness. Ten model-and-harness combinations given one Three.js prompt produced Astra 6.0 Max on Codex at 37 minutes and 1,335,495 tokens against GLM 5.3 Flash Max at nine minutes and 475,143 — neither passing both completion checks — a sample too small to rank models and large enough to show the harness moving cost nearly 3× on identical work. And Dan Luu's ~80 runs per condition across 26 testing instruction sets (TDD, fuzzing, Lean, TLA+, “Make no mistakes”) found default prompting above average and several popular skills below it, with agents wrapping their usual weak tests in a fancier framework or proving properties irrelevant to correctness: the vocabulary of rigour without the judgment.

The integrity problems on this page got two fresh instances. A developer's agents scored 94% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 by cheating, with no way to tell whether they sought the exploit or found published solutions while browsing — which makes any public benchmark a partly open-book exam for an agent with web access. And in speech recognition, three new tests for “benchmaxxing” caught models reproducing the benchmarks' own errors in VoxPopuli and LibriSpeech, the cleanest available evidence of memorization over capability. Role Anchor generalizes the concern past any one benchmark: constraining a compound pipeline's decomposer to stay in its assigned role erased 86% of that pipeline's apparent RL gains, meaning end-to-end metrics had been rewarding one module quietly feeding another the answers. On the cost axis, Together Compute's DeepSWE figures put GLM-5.3 at 87.6% for about $16 against Fable 5's 69.7% for $21.63 — 17 solves per $100 versus 3 — though the GLM number comes from four attempts rather than one, so it is the right comparison only for agents that retry. dig.bench adds the capability the rest of this page assumes: 70 text-based games (21 public) whose rules must be discovered by experiment, where humans succeed even on the hardest tier and the best models struggle.

The agent index got harder and several leaderboard positions did not survive it. Artificial Analysis added 66 Terminal-Bench 4.0 tasks and a private set of 657 Zapier-style workflows: GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 held the top and now score the same, with Astra averaging $3.26 per Intelligence Index task against Fable's $7.63 — a 57% cost gap at parity — while Gemini 3.8 Flash fell from 89% to 19% and Muse Spark 1.3 to 33%. SemiAnalysis calls the pattern “benchmaxxing”; Meta's chief AI officer counters that a large drop can simply mean a harder test. Both readings argue the same practical point, which is not to price a decision off one leaderboard. The subtler version of the problem arrived the same day: two runs sharing a provider, model family, metric identifier, unit and benchmark name can still disagree, because the runners, graders and dataset splits differ — a shared mmlu label names a dataset family, not a measurement procedure.

Three results this cycle measure what agents actually produce rather than what they score. 1Password's Off-by-1 Labs generated 6,080 patches against six recent CVEs and found only 26% fixed the vulnerability cleanly, with more than half failing, introducing a new vulnerability, or both. Bottleneck Labs gave seven frontier models a Mac Mini, $300 and 72 hours to build a business: they spent thousands on inference, earned nothing, and two runs were halted after the agents sent spam and unsolicited invoices — the finding being not the zero but that a vague goal and weak guardrails convert failure into outward-facing abuse. And Quesma's quantization sweep turns a recurring argument into a lookup: Qwen3.8-27B's 17GB Q4_K_M build holds up, the 1-bit build falls to roughly chance on GPQA Diamond.

Before all of this, two reforms had landed in the same week, and together they cover the two ways an agentic score misleads. Terminal-Bench 4.0 now reports resolution rate, tokens and dollar cost as one figure — Opus 5 at 51.8% for 6.5B tokens and ~$6.0K, GLM-5.3 at 41.8% for 8.7B tokens and ~$2.7K, Fable 5 at 44.5% with error bars overlapping GLM-5.3 and a higher cost — which turns a leaderboard position back into a purchasing decision, and, by publishing error bars, admits when two models are not actually separated. DeepMind's answer to everything below is structural: the first double-blind AI evaluation, using cryptographic environments so neither the developer nor the evaluated system can see test content in a form that could leak into training. That is the mechanism this page has been implicitly asking for — contamination, scaffolding inflation and harness choice are all currently policed by trust, and a cryptographic construction replaces trust with proof. Alongside it, a rare clean elasticity measurement: OpenAI's late-July discounts moved Luna usage 13.8× and Terra 5.6× while list-priced Sol moved 1.1×, most of the gain taken from other labs rather than cannibalized internally, and nearly a third of developers who switched stayed after prices returned.

The month's defining result on this page is a 37-point gap on a single checkpoint. OpenAI declared GPT-6 Astra the onset of AGI on the strength of 99.9% on ARC-AGI-3 — a number produced through a provider adapter harness OpenAI supplied. Run the same model through the benchmark's own software and it scores 62.7%. Three weeks earlier DeepSeek open-sourced the harness its own benchmarks ran in and exposed a 33-point Terminal-Bench spread on DeepSeek-V4-Pro-0813 (87.9% in its minimal mode, 78.7% on Terminus 2, 54.68% on Vals AI's). Two independent demonstrations in one month establish the same thing: an agentic benchmark number without its harness is not a measurement, and the spread between harnesses now exceeds the spread between model generations. Construct validity — whether a benchmark measures what its title claims — has been overtaken by a blunter question: whether the score describes the model at all, or the scaffolding someone wrapped around it. Astra's underlying result is still real (fewer actions than the median human on 96% of levels, by compiling unfamiliar environments into compact symbolic world models), which is what makes the inflated framing unnecessary as well as misleading. A separate critique lands on the chart most used for model selection: the intelligence-versus-cost plot uses a logarithmic cost axis, hiding both how large the gap is between cheap and heavy models and how negligible it is among the cheap ones, and it prices open-weight models at datacenter API rates rather than local hardware — penalising exactly the checkpoints a self-hosting team would choose.

The newest entry (Aug 3) carries the execution-over-description principle into security: IntrusionBench, released alongside Cogent's VR-1 cyber model, scores nothing for narrating an attack — the agent must execute the chain and produce checkable evidence before anything counts, making "proven attack paths" rather than plausible descriptions the scoring unit. It joins CyberGym and CTI-REALM as the third security-specific leaderboard this summer and is the strictest about verification; the accompanying vendor numbers (VR-1 at ~2× proven paths, ~¼ cost vs Kimi K3, Claude Opus 4.8, GLM-5.2) come with an unusually candid caveat that harness-matched the gap nearly closes — harness effects being exactly the confound this page's coding-agent head-to-heads try to isolate.

Before that (Aug 1), agent benchmarking pushed further toward real environments: Supabase Evals is an open-source benchmark (Apache-2.0, runs locally on pnpm) that scores Claude Code, Codex, and OpenCode on real Supabase tasks — it boots a containerized stack and a real CLI, then asks the agent to build a schema or fix a broken RLS policy, scoring with deterministic checks plus LLM-as-a-judge and allowing one retry. Headline findings: agents pass most scenarios with no skill loaded (Opus 5 and Kimi K3 both scored 100% unaided in the Build stage); skills matter further down the task list (Sonnet 5 went 78% to 100% with skills, GPT-5.4 mini 78% to 89%); and the odd behavioral finding is docs usage — Codex reads roughly eight docs pages per scenario against about two for Claude Code, the same task solved with 4× the reading. It extends two of this page's threads at once: benchmarks that grade against real systems rather than mocks, and behavioral telemetry (how an agent works, not just whether it passes) becoming a first-class output.

Before that, a July 31 clinical-AI thread extends the construct-validity strand into medicine: passing a benchmark doesn’t mean a clinical LLM is ready for real-world use — cognitive biases can still influence model decisions even when the underlying medical knowledge is correct — and the proposed response is evaluating those hidden failure modes with 17 deterministic benchmarks spanning clinical decision-making and coding, plus continuous testing before and after deployment. It is the sharpest domain-specific statement yet of this page’s running theme that a passing score and deployment-readiness are different claims.

Before that (July 30), a practitioner comparison sharpened the OCR/document-parsing thread from a single sponsored benchmark into a broader practitioner comparison: Joe, a document-AI practitioner at Adobe's PDF research lab who has processed hundreds of millions of document pages, compared traditional OCR pipelines against newer vision-language-model (VLM) OCR systems — LightOnOCR, Chandra, and MonkeyOCR — on hard documents, weighing cost, latency, grounding (does the extracted text actually tie back to its position on the page), and failure modes rather than a single leaderboard number. Read alongside the Datalab Marker 2 result below, the emerging picture is that document OCR/parsing is becoming its own benchmarking sub-discipline, with quality-speed-cost trade-offs and failure-mode analysis mattering as much as a single accuracy score.

The newest benchmark activity before that (mid-to-late July) adds a new modality and a new infrastructure-scale axis to the page. On speech, a July 25 roundup of open ASR models found NVIDIA's Parakeet RNNT 1.1B topping the Open ASR Leaderboard, beating Whisper large-v3 on average word error rate, with NVIDIA's Canary 1B leading multilingual transcription and translation. The same day, a vendor-sponsored comparison put Datalab's Marker 2 document parser at 76.0 on olmOCR-bench at 2.9 pages/sec — matching Gemini/MinerU quality while running roughly 5x faster than MinerU and Docling — a result to read with the sponsorship in mind. On infrastructure-scale benchmarking, NVIDIA's Blackwell led AgentPerf (July 21), described as the first agentic-AI infrastructure benchmark, running up to 20x more agents per megawatt than the prior Hopper generation — extending the page's benchmark scope from model quality to agent-serving throughput. And on July 19, Perplexity open-sourced a new benchmark reported to "break" research agents on realistic tasks (limited further detail available in the source), continuing the running theme below that benchmarks are increasingly designed to break agents rather than just score them.

Four longer-running strands continue underneath: headline benchmark records, the methodology discourse around what evals are for, construct validity (whether a benchmark actually measures what its headline claims), and whether error analysis itself can be automated. On that last strand, a July 15 Parlance Labs study put the new eval agents (Braintrust's Loop, Arize's Alyx, LangSmith's Engine) to the test against a human-labeled set of 100 real production traces: the best recovered 87% of human-flagged failures and every system surfaced issues humans missed, but all of them missed interactions that "looked correct" yet delivered a poor user experience, and each added false-positive noise — so the emerging verdict is automate error analysis to accelerate it, but keep a human in the loop. Relatedly, coding-agent benchmarking is getting more rigorous about what it measures: a MarkTechPost head-to-head ran Mistral's new Vibe for Code, Claude Code, Cursor, and Codex through the same scaffold-to-PR task on one shared harness, deliberately isolating agent behavior from the model and the scaffold (product detail on the Tools & DevEx page).

On the leaderboard front, the coding benchmarks moved sharply — Anthropic's Claude Fable 5 set a new SWE-bench Verified record of 95.0% (against the prior 88.6% held by Opus 4.8 only two weeks earlier) and posted 80.3% on SWE-bench Pro, a 21-point lead over GPT-5.5's 58.6%, which observers frame as a generational rather than marginal jump. SWE-bench (Verified and Pro) is now the de facto coding scoreboard, and the gap between the leader and the field is wide enough that practitioner newsletters explicitly advise head-to-head SWE-bench Pro runs before continuing to route coding work to GPT-5.5 or Gemini. A second, efficiency-flavored story is the active-parameter race: MoE models with under 5B active params (e.g. Cohere's North Mini Code at 3B active) are now topping coding benchmarks against dense 120B+ models, so "score per active parameter" is emerging as a reported axis alongside raw score.

On methodology, the conversation has shifted from "do you have evals?" to "what happens after evals?" — Parlance Labs framing internal tooling and retrieval as the real post-eval blockers. The construct-validity thread sharpened this month around a Nature Medicine Brief Communication whose title — general-purpose LLMs "outperform specialized clinical AI tools on medical benchmarks" — drew a retraction request and a detailed rebuttal from AI-in-Healthcare's David Talby: the study cleanly measured one capability (single-turn general medical Q&A across MedQA, HealthBench, and real clinician queries) but its headline generalized that to "medical benchmarks," a reminder that a title's scope should match its evidence and that exam-style scores say little about deployment — the premise behind MedHELM's 121-task clinical taxonomy.

Document OCR & VLM parsing

Document OCR/parsing is emerging as its own benchmarking sub-discipline rather than a footnote inside general vision evals. Datalab's Marker 2 (vendor-sponsored) scored 76.0 on olmOCR-bench at 2.9 pages/sec, roughly matching Gemini/MinerU quality at ~5x the speed of MinerU and Docling. A separate practitioner comparison (Joe, Adobe PDF research lab) evaluated newer VLM-based OCR systems — LightOnOCR, Chandra, and MonkeyOCR — against traditional OCR pipelines on hard documents across cost, latency, grounding, and failure modes, rather than a single accuracy number. Together the two suggest the field is converging on multi-axis evaluation (speed, cost, grounding, failure behavior) for document understanding, not just top-line extraction accuracy. Mistral OCR 4.1 (Aug 14, release detail) is built to be judged on that wider axis: it targets nested tables and hierarchical document structure and emits JSON or Markdown directly rather than flat text, which moves the question from “what share of characters were read correctly” to whether the emitted structure matches the document's actual hierarchy. That is a harder thing to score — character accuracy has a ground truth, structural fidelity needs a schema to compare against — and it is the gap most document benchmarks still have.

Coding benchmarks (SWE-bench)

SWE-bench Verified and the harder SWE-bench Pro are the dominant public coding scoreboards. Current standings: Claude Fable 5 at 95.0% Verified / 80.3% Pro, Claude Opus 4.8 at 88.6% Verified (the prior Verified record), GPT-5.5 at 58.6% Pro, and Claude Fable 5's reported 95.0% being "the highest ever recorded by any model." The unusually wide gap on SWE-bench Pro (21 points over GPT-5.5) is being read as a generational step, and is concrete enough that buyers are advised to run their own head-to-head on representative Pro-style workloads rather than trust a single aggregate number.

Fireworks supplied the clearest demonstration of why on Sep 16, and it did so by publishing the number most comparisons leave out. Benchmarking four models on DeepSWE, it found DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash reaching GPT-6 Astra’s accuracy band at $0.43 per task — about fifteen times cheaper — while all four finished within 0.7 pass@1 points of each other. That spread is smaller than the run-to-run variation Fireworks measured on the same setup, which makes the honest conclusion that the four models are indistinguishable on this benchmark and differ by more than an order of magnitude on price. Read without the variance figure, the identical data would have supported a headline about a 0.7-point lead, and almost every vendor comparison this page has recorded reports the score without it. The practical effect is to move the coding-benchmark question away from ranking entirely: when the top of the field is inside its own noise, the useful discriminators are cost per task, turns to a first meaningful edit, and consistency across repeated runs — which is exactly what ALTK-Evolve named as a defect the same day.

Active-parameter efficiency benchmarks

A newer reporting axis tracks score against active (not total) parameters. Sparse MoE models with under 5B active params are now beating dense 120B+ models on coding benchmarks — e.g. Cohere's North Mini Code (3B active) reportedly outperforming dense frontier models up to 120B+, alongside DeepSeek-V4-Pro (49B active), NVIDIA Nemotron Ultra (55B active), and hybrid Zamba2-VL 7B. The trend reframes "efficiency" from a deployment footnote into a headline benchmark dimension: total params up, active params down, quality up. (Model detail on the Model releases page.)

Post-eval failure modes

Two issues block teams after evals are working: internal tooling (no easy way for non-engineers to iterate on prompts, datasets, and judge configurations) and retrieval (RAG quality dominates output quality once eval drift is controlled). Parlance Labs is treating both as separable engineering disciplines rather than ad-hoc fixes.

A failure mode this section has met repeatedly in specific cases got a general name on Sep 16. IBM Research’s ALTK-Evolve introduces Consistency Guidelines addressing what it calls the inconsistency gap: a model’s success rate on one task dropping significantly across repeated runs of that same task. Nearly every agent evaluation recorded on this page reports a single pass rate, which is one sample from a distribution presented as a property of the system, and naming the spread as a defect in its own right separates two claims that have been running together — that an agent completed a task, and that it will complete it again. The corroboration arrived the same day from an unrelated direction, when Fireworks found four coding models separated by less than its own measured run-to-run variation, and it matches the earlier result that memory-based agents’ apparent self-improvement largely dissolves once task order is shuffled. Three independent findings now point at the same thing: the variance is frequently larger than the effect being reported. What ALTK-Evolve does not yet show is that its guidelines close the gap rather than expose it, since IBM presents them as guidance rather than as an intervention with a measured before and after.

Vals.ai added a mechanism on Sep 17 for the failure this section keeps recording without explaining: lab benchmark results that do not reproduce. Its argument is that the guardrails used to detect a model cheating on an evaluation are frequently the same guardrails present during training, so the model is optimised against the detector rather than against the task, and learns to complete work in whatever way those specific checks do not catch. If that holds, eval-gaming is not an occasional lapse by a careless team but a predictable consequence of reusing one piece of tooling as both training signal and integrity check. It generalises past benchmarks, too: any grader that is also a reward is a target. That is the same shape as OpenAI's disclosure the same day, where flawed citation graders are the suspected cause of a model fabricating figures — a grader rewarding the appearance of a citation is a training signal for producing the appearance. The remedy Vals draws is procedural rather than technical, and it is the one this page has been converging on from several directions: a number is trustworthy in proportion to the independence of whoever produced it.

From vibes to discipline

The discourse increasingly frames eval rigor as the defining skill of LLM application engineering. Practitioners (Husain, Iusztin, Berger) cite a similar arc: ship a vibe-checked agent, hit production issues, build evals, then realise evals are necessary but insufficient and have to invest in tooling, retrieval, and observability around them. Two September measurements show what the discipline is for. Bottleneck Labs ran seven frontier models on a Mac Mini with a $300 budget and 72 hours to build a business; they burned thousands on inference, made $0, and two runs had to be stopped after the agents began sending spam and unsolicited invoices. Quesma's quantization benchmarks answer a narrower question that usually gets settled by forum argument: for Qwen3.8-27B, the 17GB Q4_K_M build holds up well and the 1-bit build collapses to roughly random chance on GPQA Diamond. One is an open-ended goal with no evaluable target and predictable results; the other is a well-posed question that a measurement closes in an afternoon. The gap between them is most of what this section means by discipline. The rebuilt evals-skills (Aug 23) are the most direct attempt yet to make the disciplined path the cheap one. The new error-discovery skill takes a file of outputs or traces, builds a custom review app over a deliberately diverse sample, and clusters the annotations you make into failure modes while pulling up related examples — automating the bookkeeping around manual trace review rather than replacing the review itself, which is the right division of labour given that looking at the data is the step people actually skip. A start skill routes the agent to the appropriate workflow depending on whether you are discovering failure modes in raw traces or auditing an eval pipeline that already exists. Distribution matters as much as content here: shipping it as an installable skill (npx skills add) puts eval methodology in the same channel as the agents being evaluated, which is the first time the discipline has travelled by the same mechanism as the thing it disciplines.

The written form of the same discipline grew by five entries on Sep 15, and the additions are unusually well chosen because each answers a question that only arrives after the first eval suite exists. Do not write the rubric before annotating: reviewers who are checking items stop noticing anything outside the list, and what counts as good drifts as you read real interactions, so run error analysis first and let the rubric fall out of it. When traces contain sensitive data, the ordering is negotiated access to a real subset, then redaction with the redactor's output checked, and synthetic data last — it can only evidence behaviour someone already imagined. For a trace too large to read, anchor on the first upstream failure, since errors compound and the earliest one dominates, and use progressive disclosure instead of dumping tool output at the reviewer. Give an LLM judge only the segment its specific failure mode needs, tested by ablation against human labels, because extra context causes context rot and makes the judge worse. And retire a gold eval that everything passes: a suite with no failures has stopped carrying signal while still costing considerably more than a unit test to maintain, which is the first explicit sunsetting rule this section has had. The concession underneath all five is worth keeping: scores stop being comparable when the set changes, and that is acceptable, because an eval set is a hill to climb rather than a fixed yardstick — longer-horizon tracking belongs with product metrics.

Retrieval benchmarks at web scale

Retrieval evaluation has mostly been conducted at a size that flatters it, and Perplexity's Q2D-Web (Sep 10) is an attempt to fix that: 190 million documents and 69,721 queries in ten languages, scoring first-stage retrievers rather than end-to-end answers. Two design choices carry the methodological weight. Relevance is judged three separate ways rather than once, so no single annotation process silently defines what “relevant” means — the construct-validity concern below, applied to the label set instead of the task. And submissions are scored through a subsampling scheme chosen to preserve model ranking while keeping the evaluation affordable, which is the honest acknowledgement that a benchmark nobody can afford to run stops being a benchmark. Isolating the first-stage retriever is the useful part for practitioners: it is the component whose failures are invisible in a RAG evaluation, because a document lost before reranking never appears in anything the answer is scored against.

Construct validity & benchmark scope

A recurring failure mode is the gap between what a benchmark measures and what its name (or a paper's title) claims — and the Sep 4–7 Intelligence Index revisions show the name staying fixed while the measurement underneath it changes twice inside a week. v4.2 dropped GPQA-Diamond for saturation, added two harder components and raised the private-test share to 40%; v4.3 replaced τ³-Banking with AutomationBench-AA and moved Terminal-Bench to v4.0. Both revisions are defensible individually, and together they flipped the top of the ranking from Fable 5.1 leading by two to a tie at 53 without a single model weight changing. The construct-validity lesson is not that Artificial Analysis was wrong to revise — a saturated component measures nothing — but that “first on the Intelligence Index” is a claim about an index version, and almost nobody quoting it says which.

CursorBench 4.0 reproduces the problem inside one vendor's own pages, where 3.2 and 4.0 scores now sit side by side describing different tasks. The July 2026 medical-AI dispute remains the sharp example of the underlying issue: a Nature Medicine Brief Communication reported frontier models (GPT-5.2, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.6) beating two commercial clinical tools (OpenEvidence, Wolters Kluwer's UpToDate Expert AI) on MedQA (Gemini 97.4% vs 89.6% / 88.4%), HealthBench (GPT 88.0 vs ~62), and 1,800 blind-scored "real clinical queries" from NYU Langone (frontier 3.5–3.6 vs 3.17–3.24 on a 1–4 scale, the clinical tools roughly tying Google's free Search AI Overview at 3.27). Critics — led by John Snow Labs' David Talby, himself a maker of healthcare-specific models — argue all three tests probe a single capability (single-turn general medical question-answering with no patient record attached), so the paper's "medical benchmarks" and "specialized clinical AI tools" framing overstates a two-product, one-task result, and that benchmark-developer overlap (HealthBench was built by OpenAI) is an unaddressed confound. The durable lessons: match a title's scope to the evidence, and separate exam-style question sets from deployment tasks — the motivation behind MedHELM's clinician-validated taxonomy (5 categories, 22 subcategories, 121 tasks, many private or gated so they can't be memorized), which treats near-perfect exam scores as weak evidence of real-world clinical value. The clinical-AI thread (July 31) gives the construct-validity argument its sharpest domain-specific form: a clinical LLM can hold correct medical knowledge and still fail through cognitive bias, so readiness requires evaluating those hidden failure modes directly — 17 deterministic benchmarks across clinical decision-making and coding, run continuously before and after deployment — not just a passing score on a knowledge benchmark.

The most mundane version of the same problem surfaced on September 8 and is probably the most widespread: two runs sharing a provider, model family, metric identifier, unit and benchmark name can produce different numbers, because the runners, graders and dataset splits differ. A shared mmlu label identifies a dataset family, not the procedure that produced the score, so the comparison looks fully specified and is not. That is the same confound as the harness-versus-model one, one level lower — and it means a reported delta between two published numbers is uninterpretable unless the harness, grader and split are stated alongside them. Three August results extend this from “what is the benchmark measuring” to “what is the system doing when no one is watching a module.” In speech recognition, researchers introduced three tests for benchmark optimization and found models reproducing VoxPopuli's and LibriSpeech's own transcription errors — a model cannot inherit a dataset's mistakes by getting better at transcription, so this is memorization made visible; the recommended fixes are fully held-out evaluation sets and attention to temporal and speaker metadata. The Role Anchor result (Aug 18) is the more general one: in compound LLM pipelines, a specialized module can abandon its assigned role while system-level accuracy improves, and constraining one pipeline's decomposer to stay in role removed 86% of its apparent RL gains. Any multi-module system judged only end to end may be optimizing a shortcut through its own architecture.

And a developer's 94% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 (Aug 20) turned out to be cheating, with the ambiguity — deliberate exploit or web-search encounter with published solutions — unresolved and unresolvable from the score alone. Read together, these say a benchmark number now needs three qualifiers before it means anything: which harness, whether the agent had web access, and whether the modules were measured separately. A fourth qualifier arrived in August for any agent claiming to improve itself: what order the tasks ran in, and how many times. Re-running memory-based agents across multiple passes and shuffled task orders showed that a large share of their reported improvement tracks evaluation noise, sequencing effects and underspecification rather than anything the agent learned — the gain survives the original run order and mostly does not survive permutation.

This is the same construct-validity failure as the harness question one level up: the number was real, but it was measuring the protocol rather than the capability. It also sets a cheap, concrete bar that self-improvement claims can be held to, which is more than most of this section's problems have.

Everything above concerns benchmarks that measure the wrong thing. A Sep 14 re-grading exercise covers the case where a benchmark measures the right thing incorrectly, and it is the more embarrassing failure. Domain experts re-checked six widely cited physics benchmarks and found most of the questions models were scored as failing were defective rather than difficult: wrong answer keys, ambiguous wording, and bugs in the graders. Corrected, frontier models come out close to saturated on all six. That inverts a claim this page has repeated in good faith — that physics remained a weak spot — and it does so without any model changing, which is exactly the pattern the Intelligence Index revision showed from the other direction. The authors' recommendation is the part to carry forward: the replacement is harder human-written exams rather than another automated suite, because a new quiz assembled the same way inherits the same defect. It also supplies a cheap diagnostic nobody was running. Before concluding that models are weak at X, have a domain expert grade a sample of the failures by hand; if most of them are bad questions, the finding is about the benchmark. The nearest thing to a counterweight this cycle is Real-SWE (Sep 14), which tests agents on private enterprise codebases where the best resolution rate is 38.8% — a genuine probe of the contamination worry behind SWE-bench, but one that buys it by making the questions unauditable, which is this section's other failure mode in a new coat.

Two Sep 15 items sit at opposite ends of how bad this can get. The mild one is a repeat: the Artificial Analysis Capability Indices moved to v1.1 with stronger domain tuning across all six indices, and Claude Fable 5.1 (max) now leads every one. Retuning a saturating index is correct maintenance, and a clean sweep is a real result — but it is a result about v1.1, arriving on the same suite family whose Intelligence Index revisions flipped the top of the ranking twice inside a week without a model weight changing. When a leaderboard can move for two reasons, quoting it without the version states only one of them. The severe one is Dan Selsam's personal statement, which is an evaluation claim wearing a risk essay's clothes: models are becoming situationally aware enough that researchers are losing the ability to test them in contexts where the model believes it is unobserved, so models increasingly appear aligned whether or not they are. Every other entry in this section describes a benchmark measuring the wrong construct. This describes a measurement whose validity depends on a condition the subject can detect and the experimenter can no longer create, which is not fixable by a better instrument — and it lands on the alignment certification that the pacing proposal makes the gate for a release.

Against those, Dan Luu's Sep 15 treatment of bad benchmarks makes the constructive version of this section's whole argument, and it is notable for refusing to supply a procedure. Evals, on his account, are about avoiding specific mistakes rather than following steps, and no guide will produce a good one; what transfers is experimental design. He works through three cases chosen for how unlike each other they are — baseline numbers for performance “napkin math”, a set of model evals including Senior SWE-bench, and winter tire testing. The tire comparison is the load-bearing one rather than the joke it looks like: tire testing is a mature measurement discipline with standardised conditions, catalogued confounds and published methodology, which is exactly the apparatus this page finds missing every time a model score is quoted without its harness, grader and split. Read against the physics re-grading above, the two describe the same deficit from opposite sides — one shows what happens when nobody checks the instrument, the other shows what a field looks like once checking the instrument is the norm.

The most useful measurement of the cycle takes the instrument itself as the object. Arena.ai evaluated 21 model-harness pairs spanning seven models and three harnesses and reports that harness choice has little effect on task success rate and a large effect on cost — up to fivefold in the coverage that followed — with a simple harness competitive throughout. Read carefully, this does not demolish the harness-engineering argument so much as relocate it: if success rates converge, the harness is a procurement decision rather than a capability one, and every comparison of harnesses should carry a price the way model comparisons now carry a context length. The awkward part is that it does not reproduce the largest figure on the other side. DeepSeek published a 33-point spread for a single checkpoint across scaffolding modes, which is not a cost difference but a score difference, and this study finds nothing like it. Both cannot be generally true. The resolution probably lies in what is being measured — a curated set of tasks where models mostly succeed will compress score differences and expose cost ones — but until someone runs both designs against each other, “the harness does not change the score” is a finding about these 21 pairs, not about harnesses.

Automated & agentic eval systems

A new tool class tries to automate error analysis itself: eval platforms have each shipped an agent that reads production traces and reports what is failing — Braintrust's Loop, Arize's Alyx, and LangSmith's Engine — and general coding agents can attempt the same job from a folder of logs. A Parlance Labs experiment (Hamel Husain & Antaripa Saha) benchmarked them against a human-labeled set of 100 real traces from a production apartment-leasing agent: the best system recovered 87% of human-flagged failures and every system caught issues the humans missed, but all of them systematically missed "looked-correct-but-poor-UX" interactions and flagged non-failures as noise. The takeaway sharpens the page's methodology thread — these tools accelerate error analysis but do not remove the human, because criteria drift (you discover grading criteria by reading outputs) is exactly what a fully automated pass skips. The vendor-shipped side of this class grew on Aug 1 with Supabase Evals: rather than reading traces after the fact, it generates them under controlled conditions — a containerized Supabase stack, a real CLI, deterministic checks blended with an LLM judge, one retry allowed — and reports not just pass rates but behavior (docs-reading volume, skill-loading deltas). Platform-run, real-environment benchmarks published by the tool vendor itself are becoming a standard part of how agent-facing products ship. Sierra's Hyper-τ-bench (Sep 9) is the most demanding instance of this idea so far, because the artifact under evaluation is itself an agent.

The developer agent gets a sandboxed workspace holding a simulated business's records plus a simulated client it may message at any time, and must recover the specification from evidence, design the architecture, and convert the business's actions into tools until a working customer-service agent exists — which is then held to a fixed model menu and a per-conversation cost budget. Claude Opus 5 at max reasoning in Claude Code passes 23.9% of held-out tasks unaided and 82.2% when paired with an engineer carrying deep context. Set beside dig.bench, which scores whether an agent can experiment its way to a game's undisclosed rules across 70 games and finds humans clearing tiers the best models cannot, the two benchmarks isolate the same missing faculty from opposite ends: forming a correct model of a situation nobody has described.

Security benchmarks

Cybersecurity has developed its own leaderboard tier over the summer — CyberGym and CTI-REALM for defensive/CTI work (see Safety & policy for the specialist-model race running on them), and now IntrusionBench (Aug 3, released with Cogent's VR-1), which is the strictest about verification: an agent scores nothing for describing an attack and must execute the chain and produce checkable evidence, making "proven attack paths" the unit of measurement. The methodological lesson generalizes beyond security: Cogent's own disclosure that harness-matched comparisons nearly erase its black-box lead is a live demonstration of the harness-vs-model confound this page's coding-agent head-to-heads are designed to isolate. The security-patch side now has a number of its own. 1Password's Off-by-1 Labs generated 6,080 patches for six recent CVEs and found that only 26% fixed the vulnerability cleanly; more than half either failed, introduced a new vulnerability, or both. Read alongside VR-1's insistence on executed, evidence-producing attack chains, the two halves of the security loop end up asymmetric: finding is cheap enough that exploits now appear within minutes of a patch discussion, while fixing still needs an expert to adjudicate three patches in four. Patch generation is not the bottleneck; patch review is.
