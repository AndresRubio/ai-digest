Model releases & updates

Last updated: 2026-09-21

Current state

Today’s cycle is unusually honest about what compression and speed actually cost. PrismML’s Ternary Bonsai 2 27B (Sep 18) is the headline: Qwen3.8-27B at 1.76 effective bits per weight, 5.93GB against 53.80GB in FP16, Apache 2.0, 262K context, image input, 142.5 tokens/second on an RTX 5090, and an average of 83.9 across 20 benchmarks against the parent’s 85.4. PrismML leads with that 98.2% and then publishes the two figures that contradict it: long-horizon agent work keeps about 75%, and SWE-bench Verified falls to 60.8 from 80.6. That is the finding this thread has needed since the first Bonsai — sub-2-bit quantization is nearly free on single-turn knowledge questions and expensive on anything that must stay coherent across steps — which makes the headline average the wrong number to buy on. Stock llama.cpp cannot load it, and all results are the vendor’s.

The System One category stopped being one company’s claim. Jared Palmer’s kev (Sep 20) is an Apache-2.0 decision model on Qwen2.5-0.5B answering six questions in about 160ms on Apple Silicon behind a TypeSafe-compatible API — and its README states that Jev leads it by roughly 19 points out of domain, which is the first outside sizing of what the closed model buys. Underneath both sits a cheaper baseline: Goedecke’s point that batching single-token prompts with structured output turns any LLM into a System One model, leaving the architecture’s real advantage as latency and price rather than capability. Jev’s own numbers sharpened in the same window — 0.114s and $0.000081 against GPT-5.6 Terra’s 8.566s and $0.013880, on workflows TypeSafe wrote — and drew their first contrary datapoint, with Bryo AI’s CTO finding Gemini slightly more accurate on email triage at 10–20× the cost.

Alibaba shipped both ends of the multimodal tier in two days. Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash (Sep 18) is natively omnimodal across text, image, audio and video at a 1M-token context window, claimed close to Gemini 3.8 Flash on audio-visual and above it on audio; Qwen3.8-LiveTranslate (Sep 19) cuts interpretation lag from 2.8s to 2.3s across 60 languages understood and 29 spoken, with diarization and bilingual display at about $1.54 per hour of speech. SpaceXAI’s Grok Voice Transcribe 2.0 (Sep 18) competed on the same axis from the accuracy side, reporting 19-language short-phrase WER falling from 20.6% to 6.8% at $0.10 per audio hour. None of the three ships weights and every ranking is self-reported, so what is genuinely checkable this cycle is the pricing, which all three published — a change worth noting on its own.

Earlier cycles

Today's cycle has no frontier launch and three releases that are each interesting for the number they concede. The most-discussed has no vendor: Union Alpha appeared as a stealth listing on OpenRouter and became one of its most-used newcomers of the week, reported to rival GPT-6 Astra and Opus 5 on DeepSWE and to beat GPT-5.6 Sol on Terminal-Bench at roughly 18× lower cost per task, with 256K context and latency that spikes under load. The page has watched this close before — “Ox Alpha” ran anonymously for weeks and was GLM-5.3-Flash — so it is a lead, not a result. The genuinely new part is the venue: a model can now accumulate production traffic and a public price/performance reputation before anyone knows who trained it, which means the cost figure is checkable by anyone with an account and the provenance, training data and retention policy by nobody.

The two open-weights arrivals are narrow by design. Ant Group's Ling-3.0-flash-Fin, built with financial institutions for source checking, valuation spreadsheets and report writing, scores 23 on Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index and 24 on its Finance & Accounting Index — a one-point domain advantage on the index built for its own domain, which is worth more than the claim it was announced with. Knowledgator's GLiFormer is the sharper result: a 575M-parameter encoder emitting nested JSON extractions with no token generation at all, at 91.10 F1 against GPT-5.6-luna's 91.96 on the same 500 examples, Apache 2.0. If it holds, structured extraction does not need a generative model, and the cost gap between a 575M encoder and a frontier API for that job is large. Both caveats are the authors': the scores are self-reported, and the “up to 95.8× faster” headline is an analytical estimate rather than a measured run.

Today's three releases agree on something the frontier tier has spent the year contradicting: the way to win a workload is to stop competing for all of them. Jev (Sep 16) is the most literal case. TypeSafe AI, founded by ChatGPT co-creator Diogo Almeida on $40M, shipped a model that deliberately cannot write — you hand it program state and it returns a typed probabilistic value, trained by a method it calls RLCD. Removing the token stream is what buys the claimed 20–200× speed and 40–400× cost advantage, along with free output tokens and 70 ms responses, because independent decisions resolve in parallel instead of one sequence resolving faster. Its target is the unglamorous traffic before reasoning starts: routing, scoring, extraction, moderation, verification. Periodic Neon makes the same bet on data rather than architecture, beating GPT-6 Astra and Fable 5.1 on FrontierXRD at a lower cost per analysis after midtraining and RL on laboratory data, and it is already deployed in labs working on superconductors and magnets. Both claims are the vendor's, and both carry a specific soft spot: “cannot hallucinate” describes Jev's output format rather than its correctness, and a miscalibrated router is harder to notice than a fabricated sentence.

Gemini 3.8 Live and Live Extended Thinking are the exception that completes the cycle rather than contradicting it, and they close the audio thread StepAudio opened a day earlier. Google put transcription, speech translation, text-to-speech and music generation on one API across 97 languages, with Gemini 3.5 Transcribe alongside, and gave the Extended Thinking variant the ability to speak while it reasons, call tools in the background and be interrupted mid-utterance. Every voice release tracked here so far treats latency as something to minimise; this treats it as something to cover, which is a different escape from turn-taking than PolyAI's first-token turn signal or Meta's learned adaptive delay. Set against StepAudio 3 Gen (Sep 15) — one discrete autoregressive model over a shared RVQ token space generating speech, designed voices, vocals, effects, music and mixed audio — the two collapses have now met from opposite directions: StepFun fuses the audio kinds, Google fuses the stages, and the stitched ASR→LLM→TTS pipeline no longer describes any current release. Neither has published what the unification costs: StepFun's SOTA-plus-breadth figures are self-reported, and nobody has separated Gemini's accuracy while reasoning under a live stream from the same model answering at leisure.

The prior cycle added no new model, and instead sorted out what the two already on the page are actually good at. Zvi Mowshowitz's extended assessment of GPT-6 Astra (Sep 14) puts its raw capability at the top of the field while separating where the gain lands: the dramatic jumps are in 3D reasoning, games, computer use and subagent coordination, and coding is very good but explicitly not a quantum leap on GPT-5.6 Sol. That is the distribution to route work by, and it matches the architectural reading on the Research papers page, which names computer use as the capability a looped architecture should improve most. He also notes OpenAI has soft-announced an internal model a tier above Astra — the same one reported to have run the Navier–Stokes campaign, and still unreleased, which means the public frontier and the actual frontier are now separated by a gap nobody outside the lab can size.

Two entries gained the detail that decides whether they are usable. Sakana's Fugu Ultra v2 turns out to expose a full frontier API surface — configurable reasoning effort, function calling, structured outputs, image and PDF input, built-in web search — and can recursively call instances of itself alongside its fixed pool of open and specialist models. Its routing-beats-scale cost claim therefore costs nothing in integration, which is what makes it worth taking seriously rather than filing as a niche trade-off. And ChatGPT Images 2.5 is two models, not one: gpt-image-2.5-sunburst and gpt-image-2.5-flare, with OpenAI recommending Sunburst where editing precision matters and Flare for fast everyday generation — so the editing restraint that made the release interesting for pipeline work is Sunburst's specifically. OpenAI puts combined usage above 3 billion images. Both corrections point the same way: launch copy describes a release, and the model ID is what you actually deploy.

Five releases this cycle, and not one of them competes on raw capability. Cognition's SWE-2 (Sep 11) reaches 50.0% on FrontierCode 1.1 Main1 — within a point of Claude Fable 5.1, level with GPT-5.6 Sol — at 64% lower cost, and the gain over its own predecessor is behavioural rather than scalar: 58% fewer turns, a median of 18 steps to the first meaningful edit against SWE-1.7's 48. Sakana's Fugu Max and Fugu Ultra v2 make the same argument structurally, routing work across a swappable pool of open and specialist models instead of sending every problem to the largest one, claiming elite-adjacent performance at two to six times lower cost and, for Ultra v2, beating Opus 5 and Fable 5 on Sakana's own Chartography benchmark without invoking a frontier model at any point — a vendor benchmark carrying an architectural claim worth separating from it. Cohere's North Small Translate spends 218B parameters (25B active) entirely on machine translation across 50 languages, open-weights, at a moment when almost every other open release chases general capability. GPT-Live-1 stops being a ChatGPT feature and becomes a line item: $0.05 per minute in the API for the voice layer alone, with the model and harness billed separately, and 80% fewer interruptions than turn-based pipelines. And Meta's Muse Voice Transcribe completes a three-way speech-to-text field in which all entrants now sit under 4% word error rate — 3.1% streaming for Muse at $0.18 an audio hour, 2.6% non-streaming for Gemini 3.5 Transcribe, 2% for MAI-Transcribe-2 at $0.10 — which retires accuracy as the axis of competition and leaves latency and price. Meta's contribution is a mechanism: adaptive delay, in which the model decides chunk by chunk whether to emit a token or keep listening, trained with a reward for correctness against a penalty for taking too long.

Independent evaluation caught up with the two frontier models the same week, and it mostly deflates the launch copy. GPT-6 Astra ties Fable 5.1 for first on Artificial Analysis' v4.3 index at 53 and sits third on the Vals Index, but leads computer use outright and does it at $19.09 and 25 minutes a task against Fable 5.1's $28.92 and 76; of the three problems Anthropic said Fable 5.1 would fix, only the safeguards loosened, and cost per task went up about 20% despite the 75% cache-read cut. Behind them, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (Sep 10) turns out to be a 552B MoE with native vision, retiring both V4-Flash and the two-week-old V4-Flash-Vision-Exp the same day; the asymmetric-architecture pitch cashes out as a cache a quarter the HBM and an eighth the SSD of the previous generation, serving at $0.30 per million input tokens with another 50% off peak. That is a serving-economics release, and it is now the one with benchmarks behind it — see Evals for what it costs to get 89% of a frontier model's bug-fix score.

Behind it, the August releases filling in under the frontier were notable for where their advantage came from: Microsoft's MAI-Code-1.1-Flash claimed better code at 25% greater token efficiency and roughly a quarter of the June model's cost (+22% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 in GitHub Copilot CLI, +15% on .NET), attributed not to a larger base model but to optimising against hundreds of thousands of RL environments harvested from real Copilot sessions — the IDE-ownership advantage cashed out as a model, shipped alongside MAI-Thinking-1 and MAI-Image-2.6. Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B makes the opposite trade explicit: it keeps Qwen3.5's architecture unchanged and spends the difference on post-training, shipping 2.4 trillion parameters with 95B active, day-one SGLang and vLLM paths rather than an API-only preview, and reasoning depth exposed as a reasoning_effort setting instead of a separate SKU — the same knob-not-a-model direction Grok 4.6 and GPT-5.6 Sol took the same week. Mistral OCR 4.1 is the narrowest release and the clearest about its own scope: a vision-multimodal model built to parse nested tables and hierarchical document structure and emit JSON or Markdown directly, rather than a flat text dump a downstream model has to re-infer structure from, priced to make general multimodal input look expensive for the same job (evaluation detail).

The cycle before this one had already established that the interesting number on a launch is no longer a benchmark. Inception Labs' Mercury 2.5 is the largest diffusion language model trained to date, and it is sold entirely on throughput and price: 1,107 output tokens per second on ordinary Nvidia GPUs, a 260K context, and $0.04/$0.15 per million tokens under an 80% launch discount, at quality it positions against GPT-5.6 Luna (Low), Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and Haiku 4.5 rather than against the frontier. Its vendor-stated “40% more intelligent” means nothing; Augment's reported drop in context-compaction latency from about 150 seconds to 27 means a great deal, because it is a number from someone who had to live with the old one. OpenAI's ChatGPT Images 2.5 sells the same way — up to 50% lower latency than Images 2.0, and an edit that changes one product or line of copy without disturbing the rest of the frame, which is the property that decides whether an image model can sit inside a pipeline at all.

Around them the specialists keep arriving with narrower claims and more honest licences. Rumik OSS 1 puts 22 languages, code-switching and deliberately placed laughter and sighs into 3B parameters, beats Cartesia and ElevenLabs on its own emotion benchmark, stays far behind Gemini, and ships non-commercial. DeepSeek's V4-Flash-Vision-Exp adds image understanding at close to Opus 4.8 on agent tasks and speaks OpenAI's Chat Completions and Responses APIs plus Anthropic's Messages endpoint, so it enters an existing harness without a client rewrite — an experimental line, clearly labelled as one. DeepReinforce's Ornith-1.5 is the odd one out and the most interesting: a 397B MoE flagship, a 35B MoE activating 3B per token, and a 9B dense model with a quantized Mobile build, all produced by a loop that now generates its own training tasks as well as its scaffolds and rollouts — a claim with no independent verification attached to it yet. LittleLearner, a 5B model capped at fifth-grade knowledge and runnable in the browser, is not a product at all but the instrument the others lack: a model whose training corpus is bounded tightly enough that “when did this capability appear, and on what” is an answerable question.

Behind this cycle sit the specialists that led the previous one, and they made the same argument in different form: Google's TimesFM-3 (330M parameters, over a trillion time points, zero-shot multivariate forecasting with covariates) is small enough to serve where forecasting workloads actually live; Gemini 3.5 Transcribe calls other Gemini models mid-transcript, turning transcription into an agent loop; Tencent's ContextPilot-14B moves context management into the weights rather than the harness; and NVIDIA's DeepSeek-V4-Pro-0813-NVFP4 shows the now-routine cycle of open weights followed within weeks by a vendor's serving-grade quantization.

At the frontier the news is arithmetic. GPT-6 Astra's operating envelope is now better documented than its benchmark: OpenAI's own product lead says Astra on low beats GPT-5.6 Sol on high, so a reasoning setting carried across from Sol is quota burned for nothing, and a serving change reportedly draws 3–4× less usage on some long-tail workloads — while one 44-minute max-effort render consumed 6.73M tokens and 15% of a weekly allowance. Claude Fable 5.1 lists at $10/$50 per million tokens with cache reads cut 75%, and Mythos 5.1 goes only to vetted cybersecurity and life-sciences organizations with monitoring data retained on their own infrastructure. Gemini 3.8 Flash is $0.75/$3.75 introductory through the end of the year, with Cyber limited to trusted testers and governments. The cheap model that defined last month kept paying off in the meantime: GLM-5.3-Flash — the anonymous "Ox Alpha" that absorbed 26 trillion tokens across 327,000 users in its first four days on OpenCode before Z.ai claimed it — is the architecturally serious half of the GLM-5.3 pair: pretrained from scratch on 30T multimodal tokens with vision native from the start, 320B total and 18B active, hybrid linear-plus-sparse attention cutting attention compute to about a third of GLM-5.3's, and an IndexPool step holding the KV cache under a quarter of it at 1M context. It scores 57 on the Intelligence Index at $0.09 per task, against $2.03 for the Claude Opus 4.8 score it matches.

It is also verbose and slow (150M tokens to complete the Index against a 110M median; 45 tok/s against 69 typical) — and it was served, for the entire free preview, on Chinese-made chips, which reframes cost-effective serving as a memory-optimization problem rather than a supply-of-NVIDIA one. At the other end of the spectrum, Thomson Reuters spent $40M over three months turning Qwen3.5 into Thomson, a 397B/17B legal-and-finance model it licenses for customers to run on their own hardware — sovereign AI at corporate scale, and a direct answer to the retention question on Safety & policy.

September's releases are less about capability than about what labs will now ship openly. GPT-6 Astra is OpenAI's first model rated Critical on its own cybersecurity scale, and its system card concedes that the training which made it more robust to prompt injection also made it better at controlling its chain of thought — well enough to evade monitors under adversarial conditions. Its headline AGI claim does not survive the harness question (see Evals). Claude Fable 5.1 more than doubled Terminal-Bench-Science to 52.6%, and hands-on testing surfaced what no benchmark reports: its low and medium reasoning levels emit no reasoning at all on some prompts, while xhigh costs eighteen times more and takes eight minutes — a reasoning dial whose bottom half may be inert. Gemini 3.8 Flash arrived with a Flash Cyber variant behind a restricted defender program, making Google the third lab in a month to gate a security-capable model rather than publish it, and Muse Spark 1.3 shipped with its top reasoning mode withheld pending safety testing. The clearest price move is MAI-Transcribe-2: 60 languages with diarization and word-level timestamps at ten cents an audio hour, continuing Microsoft's pattern of replacing OpenAI-powered features with its own models one modality at a time.

The month since August 4 answered the question the Aug 3 cycle raised. Alibaba kept its promise: weights for Qwen3.8-Max and Qwen3.8-27B shipped within a week of the API launch — the first Max-tier Qwen ever downloadable — but only the 27B is unrestricted (full capabilities, Apache 2.0, third among open models at 52 on the Intelligence Index, and the most capable checkpoint that runs on a consumer machine). The open Max is text-only, loses the 1M context, and carries revenue-threshold conditions, so the pattern holds: the flagship is a rental and the deployable model is its smaller sibling.

Three more releases fill in the frontier. Z.ai's GLM-5.3 (Aug 14) ties Kimi K3 at 60 on the Intelligence Index purely by scaling GLM-5.2's fine-tuning recipe onto more RL environments — no new architecture, no fresh pretrain — and takes the top CyberGym score, which is why its weights were held for two weeks of safety evaluation (see Safety & policy). SpaceXAI's Grok 4.6 (Aug 10) ties for third at 61 and posts the highest GPQA Diamond score yet recorded (94.9%), but its real result is efficiency: it matched near-frontier work in about half the turns and a quarter of the input tokens of Claude Opus 5, which is the number that decides what an agent costs to run. And DeepSeek-V4-Pro-0813 (Aug 13) graduated from preview with Terminal-Bench 2.1 at 87.9% — while simultaneously publishing the harness those numbers were produced in, exposing how much of an agentic score is scaffolding rather than model (independent harnesses measured the same checkpoint at 54.68 and 78.7%). Two smaller releases sharpen the local tier: Meta Superintelligence Labs' first open model, Muse Glimmer (30B multimodal, built for agents running on the user's own machine), and NVIDIA's Nemotron 3.5 Lightning, whose companion NeMo Switchyard router cut agentic task cost to about a third of Opus 4.8 by sending each step to the cheapest model that can do it. The month's cautionary release is MiniMax H3: state-of-the-art video generation, weights free to download, and a licence naming the US, UK, EU and South Korea as "excluded territories" whose users must apply for permission — open in the sense that matters least to the people most likely to want it.

The Aug 3 cycle lands three releases at once, all circling one question: what can you actually run? Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max moved from preview (July 20) to shipping — the 2.4-trillion-parameter MoE flagship is live as a hosted, OpenAI-compatible endpoint with 1M-token context, priced at $2.00 input / $6.00 output per 1M tokens with implicit cache reads at $0.25 (cached input eight times cheaper than fresh, so prefix stability drives cost more than prompt length), scoring 86.6 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and lifting FrontierSWE from 40.7 to 73.5 over its predecessor. Open weights are promised for next week alongside an open Qwen3.8-27B — the checkpoint that actually fits ordinary on-premise hardware — while the activated-parameter count remains undisclosed, so open-weights serving cost cannot yet be modeled. Thinking Machines Lab answered the deployability question head-on with Inkling-Small: a 276B-total / 12B-active open-weights multimodal MoE (Apache 2.0), distilled in part from its own 975B Inkling, that comes out ahead of its teacher — SWE-bench Verified 80.2% vs 77.6%, Humanity's Last Exam 31.6% vs 29.7%, ARC-AGI-2 40.1% vs 36.5% — with native reasoning over text, images, and audio at 1M context, and an NVFP4 checkpoint that fits a single B300 at 180GB aggregated VRAM against 600GB for BF16. The honest trade: factual recall regressed, with SimpleQA Verified falling to 20.6% from the teacher's 43.9%. And AMD shipped Instella-MoE-16B-A3B, a fully open MoE (16B total, 2.8B active: 2 shared experts plus 6 routed from 64) trained from scratch on Instinct MI300X/MI325X, publishing weights from every training stage plus data mixtures, training configs, and inference code — the strongest fully open base at a 76.7 average, though the weights carry a research-only ResearchRAIL license, making the MIT-licensed training code the more reusable asset.

These land on top of the two threads the Aug 1–3 releases had already sharpened: post-training as the upgrade lever, and multimodal unification. DeepSeek shipped DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731, an upgrade whose model card says the architecture and size are unchanged — the gains come from re-post-training alone: Terminal Bench 2.1 moves 61.8 to 82.7, DeepSWE 7.3 to 54.4, beating DeepSeek's own V4-Pro (Preview) on every agentic benchmark it published, with output priced at $0.28 per 1M tokens against $0.87 for V4-Pro (MIT, ungated weights; ~110GB at 3-bit self-hosted; every figure vendor-reported on an unreleased harness). By August 3 it was live on Ollama's US cloud, one launch command from Claude Code, Hermes, or OpenClaw — the same distribution path GLM-5.2 and Kimi K3 used. On the multimodal side, MiniMax's H3 folds the traditional drawer of video expert models (text-to-video, image-to-video, first-and-last-frame, subject reference, editing) into one omni-modal pretraining paradigm generating 4–15-second 2K clips with native stereo audio; H3-VAE compression claims a 4× gain in effective sequence length (what makes native 2K economical), but open weights are promised rather than shipped — API-only today.

Before that, two July 30 releases extended the speech and retrieval tiers. PolyAI’s Dialog-RSN-1 is an audio-native dialog model fusing turn-taking, speech recognition, function calling, and response generation in one model: it listens to the audio itself rather than an ASR transcript (so tone, hesitation, and doubt survive), emits turn-taking as its first output token (EMPTY, ONGOING, or COMPLETE), and keeps TTS separate so deployers own the voice — PolyAI reports sub-300ms responses, +11% relative containment at a restaurant group, and −37% latency at an insurer (English only, no open weights or public API). Liquid AI’s LFM2.5-Encoder-230M and -350M target the invisible workhorse tier — the encoders under classifiers, intent routers, safety filters, and PII detectors — as bidirectional encoders that stay fast at 8K context on CPU: the 350M posts an 81.02 mean across 17 tasks (fourth of 14, every model above it far larger), and the 230M runs an 8K forward pass in ~28s on CPU versus 90s+ for ModernBERT-base (LFM Open License v1.0, 15 languages, Apache-2.0 eval harness).

Anthropic's newest frontier model, Claude Opus 5 (July 25), holds the line on Opus pricing ($5/$25 per million tokens) while topping SWE-bench Verified and adding tuning for agentic coding and computer use — capability gains at a stable price point rather than a new tier. The open-weight trillion-parameter race kept moving in both directions the same week: Alibaba's Qwen team previewed Qwen3.8-Max (July 20), a 2.4-trillion-parameter multimodal MoE processing text, images, video, and documents and tuned for coding and full-stack development, which Alibaba positions as "second only to Claude Fable 5," live on Alibaba Cloud from $6/month — arriving days after Moonshot's Kimi K3. Pulling the opposite direction on scale, a developer fine-tuned OpenBMB's MiniCPM5-1B on Claude Fable-5 reasoning traces to ship a 657MB, MIT-licensed local "thinking model" that keeps MiniCPM5's native thinking template and tool-calling with a 128K context window on a single GPU, no API key or cloud calls required — extending the on-device distillation thread below PrismML's sub-2-bit Bonsai 27B to under a gigabyte. Poolside also shipped an agentic-coding mixture-of-experts model (July 22), reported only as a headline item with no further benchmarked detail available.

The open-weight scale record jumped again on July 17: Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 is a 2.8-trillion-parameter open MoE — comfortably the largest open-weight model shipped to date, well past Thinking Machines Lab's 975B Inkling and Tencent's 295B Hy3 from the same week — with native multimodal reasoning, a 1M-token context window, and a new Kimi Delta Attention mechanism that delivers up to 6.3x faster decoding at million-token context, the piece that keeps a model this large and this long-context from being economically unusable. Retrieval also got a new open-weight leader the same day: NVIDIA's Nemotron-3-Embed-8B ranks #1 overall on RTEB (78.5), built on Ministral-3-8B and mapping text into 4096-dim vectors while lowering token spend versus rivals — a candidate new default embedding backbone for enterprise agent/RAG stacks (see Agents & agentic systems for the retrieval angle).

The frontier coding race gained a structurally new kind of entrant on July 9: Grok 4.5, a 1.5-trillion-parameter model from SpaceXAI and Cursor trained across tens of thousands of NVIDIA GB300 GPUs with Cursor's proprietary coding data folded into the training mix, shipping simultaneously in Grok Build, inside Cursor, and via API at an aggressive $2-input price aimed explicitly under Opus-class models. It is the first frontier model co-trained in direct partnership with a coding-tool company rather than trained in isolation and integrated afterward — if the Cursor-data edge holds up in independent benchmarks, leverage in the coding-agent stack shifts toward tool companies, not just model labs (business angle on the Industry & business page). Voice is the other frontier move: OpenAI's GPT-Live (GPT-Live-1 and GPT-Live-1 mini) brings full-duplex speech — listening and speaking simultaneously rather than strict turn-taking — to ChatGPT Voice, and the same brief teased a GPT-5.6 launch clearing government review (no detail yet).

The proprietary tier still sets the top of the coding leaderboard via Anthropic's June 9 Claude Fable 5 (95.0% SWE-bench Verified, the highest recorded; 80.3% SWE-bench Pro; $10/$50 per million tokens), but its availability remains the bigger story: a June 12 US export-control directive named Fable 5 and Mythos 5 specifically and Anthropic disabled both for all foreign nationals within hours — the first government-ordered mid-deployment withdrawal of a frontier model (see Safety & policy). Fable 5 / Mythos 5 remain two configurations of one base model (Fable 5 safeguarded and public; Mythos 5 same weights with safeguards lifted, institution-only), now with a jurisdiction axis layered on top. China's open-weight labs keep accelerating: Tencent's Hy3 (295B sparse MoE, only 21B active per token, a 3.8B multi-token-prediction layer, 256K context, Apache 2.0) lands frontier-scale reasoning at 21B-active serving cost, joining Z.ai's GLM-5.2 (June 14; usable 1M context, MIT, now US-hosted on Ollama's Blackwell cloud and launchable into Claude Code/Codex/Hermes/OpenClaw) in a run of open releases actively reshaping where enterprises route token spend.

A Western lab joined the open-frontier-challenger tier on July 16: Mira Murati's Thinking Machines Lab shipped its first open model, Inkling, a 975B total / 41B active parameter MoE with native reasoning over text, image, and audio, a 1M-token context, and "controllable thinking effort" (dialing reasoning depth against cost) — not the top scorer on every benchmark, but positioned as the strongest open, fine-tunable base yet to challenge closed frontier labs. The broader open tier is defined by a sparse-activation efficiency race — Cohere's North Mini Code (30B/3B-active), Xiaomi's 1.02T MiMo (42B active, >1,000 tok/s), NVIDIA's 550B/55B Nemotron 3 Ultra — where total params rise, active params fall, and quality holds. Multimodal and alternative-decoding entries continue: NVIDIA's Audex (Nemotron-Labs-Audex-30B-A3B) folds ASR, translation, TTS, and audio generation into one decoder without regressing the text backbone; Google's DiffusionGemma decodes 256-token blocks in parallel at 1,000+ tok/s; Google's Gemini 3.5 Live Translate does voice-preserving speech-to-speech across 70+ languages; and Moonshot's Kimi K2.7 Code reached Ollama's US cloud. Sovereign, language-specific open models are also becoming a real category: a European consortium released Soofi S 30B-A3B (July 16), a hybrid Mamba-Transformer MoE (3.2B of 31.6B active) purpose-built for German and English rather than English-first with a multilingual afterthought.

On-device deployment took a step from laptop-class to phone-class: PrismML's Bonsai 27B (July 16) compresses Qwen3.6-27B into sub-2-bit builds — a ternary build at 94.6% of FP16 quality (5.9GB) and a 1-bit build at 89.5% quality (3.9GB) — small enough to run offline on an iPhone 17 Pro with 262K context and vision intact. The embodied / robotics thread grew into a full open stack: Ant Group's Robbyant shipped four open models in five days — LingBot-Vision (1B dense-perception), LingBot-VLA 2.0 (6B, 20 embodiments), the LingBot-World-Infinity world model, and the LingBot-VA 2.0 video-action policy — now tracked on the dedicated Embodied AI & robotics page. The proprietary tier also gained a price-aggressive newcomer: Meta's Muse Spark 1.1 (July 10), its first paid model rather than an open Llama drop, launched undercutting OpenAI and Anthropic on price while rivaling their models on some benchmarks (though not their strongest tiers) — Meta's clearest move yet toward a paid-API business (see Industry & business). Retrieval also entered the release tier via Liquid AI's LFM2.5 embedding/ColBERT pair, and the open coding tier gained DeepReinforce's self-scaffolding Ornith-1.0 family (training angle on the Training & RL page).

The through-line: 1M context, agent-tuned post-training, and open weights are table stakes; the live differentiators are throughput-per-dollar, on-device deployability down to phone-class, multimodal unification, sovereign/language-specific foundation models, and co-training with the tools models actually run inside.

Frontier proprietary models

Anthropic's Claude line and OpenAI's GPT/Codex line set the pace for coding and agentic work. Anthropic's newest is Claude Fable 5 (June 9), positioned above Opus 4.8 and priced at $10/$50 per million input/output tokens; it emphasizes long-horizon autonomy, multi-stage planning, and self-verification, and ships with the Claude Managed Agents harness and an "advisor" mode in which faster worker models consult it mid-task. Reported benchmarks put it at 95.0% on SWE-bench Verified (vs. Opus 4.8's prior-record 88.6%) and 80.3% on SWE-bench Pro (vs. GPT-5.5's 58.6%). Notably, Fable 5 and Mythos 5 are two configurations of the same base model: Fable 5 is the safeguarded general release, while Mythos 5 carries the same weights with cyber/bio/chem safeguards lifted and is restricted to government and critical-infrastructure partners only. Opus 4.8 ($5/$25) remains the step below and is the built-in fallback target when Fable 5's cyber/bio safety classifiers block a request. Context windows of 1M tokens are now standard at the frontier. As of June 12, both Fable 5 and Mythos 5 are unavailable to foreign nationals: a US export-control directive named them specifically and Anthropic suspended access across every tier within hours — the first government-ordered mid-deployment withdrawal of a frontier model (see Safety & policy).

A new entrant joined this tier on July 9: Grok 4.5 from SpaceXAI and Cursor — a 1.5T-parameter coding model trained across tens of thousands of NVIDIA GB300 GPUs with Cursor's own coding data in the mix, shipping in Grok Build, inside Cursor, and via API at $2 input, positioned squarely under Opus-class pricing as a coding-first alternative. Meta joined this tier on July 10 with Muse Spark 1.1 — notably its first paid model rather than an open-weights Llama release — priced to undercut both OpenAI and Anthropic while matching rival models on some benchmarks (though not their strongest tiers), a signal Meta is now willing to compete as a paid-API vendor. (Pricing-strategy angle on the Industry & business page.) Microsoft's MAI line (Aug 12–13) is the tier below the frontier and is arguing a different case for how a proprietary model gets good. MAI-Code-1.1-Flash claims better code at 25% greater token efficiency and about a quarter of the previous model's cost, with a 22% improvement on Terminal-Bench 2.1 inside GitHub Copilot CLI and 15% on .NET work; the stated source of the gain is optimisation across hundreds of thousands of RL environments drawn from real Copilot sessions rather than a larger base model.

The most useful thing published about a frontier model this cycle is not a benchmark but a distribution. Zvi Mowshowitz's extended assessment of GPT-6 Astra (Sep 14) rates its raw capability the highest of any model while being precise about where that shows up: the large jumps are in 3D reasoning, anything involving games, computer use and subagent coordination, while coding is very good and a real improvement on GPT-5.6 Sol without being a step change. Anyone choosing between frontier models for a specific workload gets more from that split than from the composite indices, and it agrees with the architectural inference on the Research papers page, which predicts computer use as the capability a looped architecture improves most — two independent readings of the same model converging is about as much corroboration as a closed release allows. The assessment also records that OpenAI has soft-announced an internal model a tier above Astra, which is the one reported to have run the Navier–Stokes campaign and remains unreleased. This page tracks what ships; the gap between that and what exists is now a standing caveat on every frontier comparison here, and it is a gap nobody outside the lab can measure.

Where frontier releases compete on scale and architecture, this competes on owning the surface where the work happens and harvesting it — a moat that does not require winning the next pre-training run. MAI-Thinking-1 (mid-sized reasoning for cost-sensitive enterprise coding, math and knowledge tasks) and MAI-Image-2.6 (second on the Arena text-to-image leaderboard) round out the same positioning: not frontier, deliberately, and priced accordingly.

The sharpest challenge to this tier this month does not come from another general model. Periodic Neon (Sep 16) beats both GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 on FrontierXRD, an evaluation for scientific analysis, and does it at a lower cost per analysis — two axes at once, which is what makes it a Pareto claim rather than a trade. It was not scaled into that position: the gain comes from midtraining and reinforcement learning on laboratory data, and the model is already running inside labs analysing experiments for superconductors and magnets. Set beside Thomson Reuters' Thomson and Cohere's translation-only North Small Translate below, the shape is consistent. A frontier model is the best available answer when nobody has the domain's data; where someone does, a smaller model trained on it can sit above the frontier on the domain's own benchmark and below it on price. What that does not establish is generality, and the arithmetic to watch is the same one that applies to every vendor benchmark here: FrontierXRD is the evaluation the claim is staked on, and a cost stated per analysis rather than per token folds the model's efficiency together with how many steps the task took.

The week's most-discussed model has no vendor at all. Union Alpha appeared as a stealth listing on OpenRouter on Sep 17 and became one of its most-used newcomers within days, reported to rival GPT-6 Astra and Opus 5 on DeepSWE and to beat GPT-5.6 Sol on Terminal-Bench at roughly 18× lower cost per task, with a 256K context window, reliable API calling and a stated policy of not training on prompts. Latency spikes under load. This page has seen the pattern close before: “Ox Alpha” ran anonymously for weeks and turned out to be GLM-5.3-Flash, which is why an unattributed listing belongs here as a lead rather than as a result. What is genuinely new is the venue. An anonymous model can now accumulate real production traffic and a public price/performance reputation before anyone knows who trained it, which inverts the usual order in which a release earns trust — the cost figure is checkable by anyone with an account, and the provenance, training data and retention policy are checkable by nobody.

Open / efficient frontier challengers

Chinese and open-leaning labs are matching frontier context and integration while differentiating on positioning, and two Sep 11 releases show the challenge arriving from a third direction — neither scale nor post-training, but composition. Cognition's SWE-2 matches GPT-5.6 Sol and comes within a point of Fable 5.1 on FrontierCode 1.1 at 64% less cost, with the saving coming from turn count rather than token price: 58% fewer turns than SWE-1.7 and a median of 18 steps before its first meaningful edit against 48. Sakana's Fugu Max goes further and stops treating the model as the unit at all, routing across a swappable pool of open and specialist models for a claimed two-to-six-fold cost advantage, while Fugu Ultra v2 beats Opus 5 and Fable 5 on Sakana's Chartography benchmark without calling Fable 5, Fable 5.1 or GPT-6 Astra at any point — the benchmark is Sakana's own, and the interesting claim is the one underneath it, that the frontier is reachable by assembly.

Both sit alongside the tier's existing lesson, that the top of it can be reached by post-training alone. The clearest August datapoint for that: a fine-tune is now enough to reach the top of this tier — Z.ai's GLM-5.3 (Aug 14, 753B total / 40B active) hit 60 on Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index — level with Kimi K3, three points off Claude Opus 5 — by scaling GLM-5.2's post-training recipe onto a larger and more varied set of RL environments, with the architecture and pretraining untouched. The environments themselves were largely agent-built, graded by a separate agent that never saw the reference solution and was only trusted if it accepted a correct answer and rejected an unfinished one. Alongside it, the stealth "Ox Alpha" model that had been quietly climbing OpenRouter turned out to be GLM-5.3 Flash, whose 320B weights are MIT.

Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max answered from the other direction by finally releasing weights (Aug 16) — the first Max-tier Qwen to do so — though the downloadable checkpoint is text-only, drops the 1M context, and carries attribution and revenue thresholds, making the Apache-2.0 Qwen3.8-27B the release that actually changes what a self-hosting team can run. Z.ai's GLM-5.2 (June 14) leads this group: it advertises a usable 1M-token context window — explicitly framed against synthetic needle-in-haystack tests, with the pitch "benchmark the 1M context on your actual workload" — and two thinking-effort levels (High and Max, the latter for complex multi-step coding). It is MIT-licensed and ships native integration with Claude Code and OpenClaw. Most unusually, GLM-5.2 launched with no benchmarks at all, a deliberate repositioning away from leaderboard-driven launches toward real-workload evaluation. Usable (not retrieval-only) 1M context matters for long-horizon coding agents and document-heavy pipelines, and the two thinking modes give cost control without switching models. As of June 17 it is hosted on Ollama's US cloud (NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs, privacy policy and zero-data-retention) and launchable into Claude Code, Codex App, Hermes, and OpenClaw — the same US-hosted-cloud distribution path Z.ai's open peers (Kimi K2.7 Code, MiniMax M3) have used to reach Western coding workflows without self-hosting. A Western lab entered this group on July 16: Mira Murati's Thinking Machines Lab shipped its first model, Inkling, as open-weights rather than closed — a 975B-total / 41B-active MoE with native text/image/audio reasoning, a 1M-token context, and a headline "controllable thinking effort" dial (trading reasoning depth against cost).

It isn't #1 on every benchmark, but is positioned as the strongest open base built specifically to be fine-tuned, making it the most credible open challenger to closed frontier labs to date. On Aug 3 the lab followed with Inkling-Small (276B total / 12B active, Apache 2.0), distilled in part from Inkling and beating it on SWE-bench Verified (80.2% vs 77.6%), Humanity's Last Exam (31.6% vs 29.7%), and ARC-AGI-2 (40.1% vs 36.5%) at a quarter the size — the deployment story being the NVFP4 checkpoint at 180GB aggregated VRAM on a single B300 (vs 600GB BF16), with the caveat that factual recall regressed sharply (SimpleQA Verified 20.6% vs 43.9%). Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max also joined the shipped tier on Aug 3: the 2.4T flagship runs as a hosted, OpenAI-compatible endpoint today ($2.00/$6.00 per 1M tokens, $0.25 cached input, 1M context, 86.6 Terminal-Bench 2.1, FrontierSWE 40.7→73.5 over its predecessor), with open weights and an open Qwen3.8-27B promised next week — the 27B, not the 2.4T flagship, being the realistic on-premise path.

DeepSeek's V4-Flash-0731 (Aug 1) adds a different kind of datapoint to this group: an upgrade with no architecture change at all — same 284B design, new post-training curriculum — that lifts Terminal Bench 2.1 from 61.8 to 82.7 and DeepSWE from 7.3 to 54.4, beating V4-Pro (Preview) on every published agentic benchmark at roughly a third of its output price ($0.28 vs $0.87 per 1M tokens). Weights are MIT and ungated (~110GB at 3-bit if self-hosting), and by Aug 3 it was hosted on Ollama's US cloud with three reasoning-effort levels and launchable into Claude Code, Hermes, and OpenClaw. The caveat: every figure is vendor-reported on a harness that has not been released. If post-training alone can move an agentic benchmark twenty points, the upgrade cadence of open challengers decouples from the (much slower) pretraining cadence.

DeepSeek extended the same tier sideways on Aug 24 with V4-Flash-Vision-Exp, an experimental multimodal variant that adds image understanding — screenshots, diagrams, mixed formats — and comes close to Opus 4.8 on agent benchmarks. Its real differentiator is compatibility rather than score: it speaks OpenAI's Chat Completions and Responses APIs and Anthropic's Messages endpoint, so it can be swapped into an existing harness without touching client code. That is the same substitutability argument the open tier has been making all summer, applied to vision, and the “Exp” suffix is a genuine caveat rather than modesty. On Sep 10 DeepSeek collapsed that branch back into the main line: V4.1-Flash ships on the API with native multimodal support and both V4-Flash and V4-Flash-Vision-Exp are retired the same day, so the experimental vision variant lasted a little over two weeks as a separate SKU. The architecture claim is asymmetry — more capability per unit of cost — and the one concrete mechanism given is a smaller KV cache, which places the saving squarely on long contexts and long agent runs rather than on throughput at short prompts. No benchmarks accompanied it, which makes the cache footprint the checkable claim and the capability ordering the unsupported one.

Open-weights releases

The newest open release inverts the tier's usual bet on generality: Cohere Labs' North Small Translate (Sep 11) is a 218B-parameter model with 25B active, open-weights, spent entirely on machine translation across 50 languages. A sparse model of roughly frontier size dedicated to one task is a shape this section has not seen before, and it is a research release, so throughput and production terms are unstated. Recent open releases otherwise include Google DeepMind's Gemma 4 family (e2b/e4b, 12B, 26B, 31B; the 12B is encoder-free multimodal — vision and audio feed straight into the LLM backbone — and targets on-device agents at ~16GB RAM with reasoning approaching the 26B tier; Apache 2.0), NVIDIA Cosmos 3 (open world model unifying physical reasoning, world generation, and action; Nano 16B and Super 64B checkpoints under OpenMDW-1.1), Alibaba Qwen3.7-Plus (multimodal agent model on the Bailian/Model Studio platform), JetBrains Mellum2 (12B MoE with 2.5B active params, a "focal model" for routing/RAG/sub-agents; Apache 2.0), Miso Labs MisoTTS (8B emotive open-weights text-to-speech, ~110ms latency), MiniMax M3 (sparse-attention, multimodal, 1M context, tuned for coding/agentic), Cohere Command A+ (218B sparse MoE), Microsoft Fara1.5 (browser computer-use agents at 4B/9B/27B), ByteDance unified video, and Stability AI's open audio model.

A newer entrant targets language sovereignty rather than raw scale: a European consortium released Soofi S 30B-A3B (July 16), an open hybrid Mamba-Transformer MoE activating 3.2B of 31.6B parameters, purpose-built as a bilingual German/English base rather than English-first with translated coverage — a signal that sovereign, language-specific foundation models are becoming a distinct release category alongside general-purpose open weights. AMD's Instella-MoE-16B-A3B (Aug 3) stakes out the "fully open" end of the spectrum: not just a checkpoint but weights from every training stage, data mixtures, training configs, and inference code, trained from scratch on Instinct MI300X/MI325X GPUs — 16B total / 2.8B active (2 shared experts + 6 routed from 64), with gated MLA and FarSkip-Collective delivering a 12.7% pre-training speedup and up to 39.2% lower time-to-first-token under expert parallelism, and a 76.7 base-checkpoint average that AMD calls the strongest among fully open models. The catch: weights ship under a research-only ResearchRAIL license, so the MIT-licensed training code is the more reusable asset. A narrower kind of open release is now appearing alongside the general-purpose ones: Tencent's ContextPilot-14B (Aug 31) is a Qwen3-14B checkpoint post-trained for a single harness-level job — planning, holding long-term memory, and offloading less useful context mid-task while it continues to reason and call tools. It is the same problem the Self-GC pattern solves by asking a general model to garbage-collect its own context, moved into the weights; whether the trained version generalizes past the harness it was tuned against is the open question.

Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B (Aug 13) is the cycle's clearest statement that post-training is where the remaining headroom is: it inherits Qwen3.5's architecture outright and spends the entire delta on post-training, arriving at 2.4T total parameters with 95B active and targeting coding and long-horizon agent execution. Two release choices matter as much as the weights. It ships with SGLang and vLLM deployment paths from day one, so the open-weights claim is operational rather than a checkpoint someone else has to make servable; and reasoning depth is a reasoning_effort parameter rather than a separate model, which collapses the reasoning/non-reasoning SKU split that open releases had been copying from the closed labs. The same week's Grok 4.6 and GPT-5.6 Sol Ultrafast made the same move behind an API, so the convention is now shared across both sides of the weights line.

Two open-weights releases this cycle are narrow by design, and both are more interesting for what their own numbers concede than for what they claim. Ant Group's Ling-3.0-flash-Fin (Sep 17) was built with financial institutions for source checking, valuation spreadsheets and report writing, and Artificial Analysis scored it 23 on its Intelligence Index and 24 on its Finance & Accounting Index. A domain model that beats its own general score by one point on the index built for its domain has not demonstrated much domain advantage, and publishing both figures side by side is the useful thing here — most vertical releases quote only the vertical benchmark. Knowledgator's GLiFormer (Sep 16, Apache 2.0) is the sharper result: a 575M-parameter encoder that emits nested JSON extractions without generating tokens, scoring 91.10 F1 against 91.96 for GPT-5.6-luna on the same 500 examples. If that holds, structured extraction does not need a generative model, and the cost difference between a 575M encoder and a frontier API for the same job is not marginal. The caveats are the authors' own: every score is Knowledgator-reported, and the headline “up to 95.8× faster” is an analytical estimate rather than a measured run, which is exactly the number an adopter would want measured.

On-device & quantized weights

The open tier is increasingly competing on local deployability, not just capability. Gemma 4 ships quantization-aware-trained (QAT) weights for every model size, cutting memory footprint and speeding output without the quality loss of post-hoc quantization, and runs across Apple, AMD, Intel, NVIDIA, and Qualcomm hardware. See Infrastructure & compute for the inference-economics angle. The on-device frontier moved from laptop-class to phone-class on July 16: PrismML's Bonsai 27B compresses Qwen3.6-27B into sub-2-bit builds with no re-pretraining and no multi-GPU rig — a ternary build (1.71 bits/weight) holding 94.6% of FP16 quality at 5.9GB, and a 1-bit build holding 89.5% at 3.9GB — small enough to run entirely offline on an iPhone 17 Pro with 262K context and vision input intact. If sub-2-bit quantization keeps landing at ~90% quality, on-device becomes the default for privacy-sensitive and latency-critical apps rather than a fallback tier. At the server end of the same spectrum the quantization work has become vendor routine rather than community effort: NVIDIA published DeepSeek-V4-Pro-0813-NVFP4 (Aug 31), an NVFP4 build of the DeepSeek MoE produced with Model Optimizer and licensed for commercial use, aimed at reasoning, tool use and agentic workloads. Quesma's measurement of the other end of the ladder is the useful check on all of it — Qwen3.8-27B's 17GB Q4_K_M build holds up while the 1-bit build falls to chance on GPQA Diamond (see Evals & benchmarks) — so “quantized” remains a range, not a property.

The sequel arrived on Sep 18 and it is the first entry in this thread that publishes where compression hurts. Ternary Bonsai 2 27B compresses Qwen3.8-27B to 1.76 effective bits per weight and 5.93GB against 53.80GB in FP16, Apache 2.0, keeping the 262K context and image input, and decodes at 142.5 tokens/second on an RTX 5090. Averaged over 20 benchmarks it holds 83.9 against the parent’s 85.4 — 98.2%, and the number PrismML leads with. The two it also publishes are the ones that matter for anyone putting this behind an agent: long-horizon agent work keeps roughly 75%, and SWE-bench Verified drops to 60.8 from 80.6. That is the shape the earlier Bonsai entries hinted at and nobody had quantified: sub-2-bit quantization costs little on single-turn knowledge benchmarks and a great deal on tasks that have to stay coherent over many steps, so the aggregate figure is exactly the wrong one to procure on. Stock llama.cpp will not load it, which is a real adoption tax, and every result is PrismML’s own. The same week Inco AI and LM Studio took the opposite route on the same hardware — full precision, rewritten runtime, 74 tokens/second for Qwen3.8-27B on a 48GB M5 Pro — so the on-device choice is now legibly between paying in memory or paying in agent reliability.

Large-scale efficient MoEs

A parallel front to on-device is making very large open mixture-of-experts models cheap enough to serve. Xiaomi's MiMo-V2.5-Pro-UltraSpeed (1.02T total / 42B active, FP8, trained on 27T tokens, 1M context, MIT) reportedly clears 1,000 tokens/second on commodity GPUs via the TileRT runtime — reframing trillion-parameter inference as a deployment target. NVIDIA's Nemotron 3 Ultra (550B total / 55B active) pairs hybrid Mamba-Transformer layers with LatentMoE expert routing and NVFP4 quantization for up to 5× throughput, trained via multi-teacher on-policy distillation and tuned explicitly for long-running agentic workflows (1M context). The same sparse-activation logic now reaches small footprints: Cohere's North Mini Code (30B total / 3B active, Apache 2.0, AA coding-intelligence index 27.6) reportedly beats dense models up to 120B+ on coding, making sub-cent-per-call, edge-viable, sovereign coding agents practical. The common thread: sparsity, hybrid attention, and aggressive low-precision quantization are how the open frontier is decoupling parameter count from serving cost.

The newest open entrant in the class is Tencent's Hy3 (July 8): a 295B-total sparse MoE that activates just 21B parameters per token, with a 3.8B multi-token-prediction layer and a 256K context window, fully open under Apache 2.0 — 295B-class reasoning at 21B-active serving cost. That record was overtaken on July 17 by Moonshot AI's Kimi K3, a 2.8-trillion-parameter open MoE with native multimodal reasoning and a 1M-token context — now the largest open-weight model shipped to date. Its headline architecture, Kimi Delta Attention, is the reason it's usable rather than a benchmark curiosity: it delivers up to 6.3x faster decoding at million-token context, the mechanism that keeps trillion-scale, million-token inference economically viable rather than ruinously slow or expensive. The signal: open labs are no longer just matching frontier scale, they're solving the cost curve that keeps big-context models off real workloads. DeepReinforce's Ornith-1.5 (Aug 20) spans the whole range in one family — a 397B MoE flagship, a 35B MoE activating 3B per token, and a 9B dense model with a quantized Mobile build for iPhone and Android — but the release is notable for how it was produced rather than how it is served. Ornith-1.0 optimized scaffold construction and solution rollouts; 1.5 closes the loop by generating its own training tasks as well, so the system continually invents problems, discovers strategies for them and improves the policy by RL on the result. That is a strong claim about self-improvement with no independent verification behind it yet, and it should be read as a described pipeline rather than a demonstrated one. (Training-side context on the Training, post-training & RL page.)

Alternative decoding architectures

A distinct research line attacks the autoregressive decode bottleneck directly. Google's experimental DiffusionGemma (26B MoE, Apache 2.0) generates 256-token blocks in parallel using bidirectional attention plus iterative self-correction, shifting the bottleneck from memory bandwidth to compute and reaching 1,000+ tok/s on an H100 (700+ on an RTX 5090, up to 4× faster than autoregressive). Output quality currently trails standard Gemma 4 — it is a proof-of-concept research release, not a production replacement — but it is a credible signal that if quality closes over the next 6–12 months, autoregressive decoding becomes optional for latency-sensitive work. See Infrastructure & compute for the throughput angle. That 6–12 month question now has a partial answer. Inception Labs' Mercury 2.5 (Sep 9) is the largest diffusion language model trained to date and is being sold as a production tier rather than a proof of concept — 1,107 output tokens per second on widely available Nvidia GPUs, a 260K context, parallel tool calls, adjustable reasoning and schema-aligned JSON, at $0.04/$0.15 per million tokens during an 80% launch discount. The quality positioning is the honest part: it targets the cost-optimized tier (GPT-5.6 Luna Low, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, Haiku 4.5), not the frontier, which is exactly where DiffusionGemma suggested parallel decoding would land first. The datapoint worth keeping is Augment's, not Inception's: context compaction falling from roughly 150 seconds to 27. Diffusion decoding has stopped being interesting because of what it might become and started being interesting because of which latency budget it fits.

The category acquired a second entrant and its first outside measurement within four days. Jared Palmer’s kev (Sep 20) is an Apache-2.0 decision model on Qwen2.5-0.5B that runs on Apple Silicon behind a TypeSafe-compatible API and answers six questions in about 160ms per its README — and the same README states that Jev leads it by roughly 19 points out of domain. An open clone publishing its own deficit is how a category stops being one vendor’s claim. Sean Goedecke supplied the cheaper baseline underneath both: batch prompts that each generate one token, constrain them with structured output, and an ordinary LLM returns the same typed decisions in parallel — which makes the architecture’s advantage latency and price rather than a capability, since the batched version still pays full prefill per prompt and the direct-logit design does not. Meanwhile Jev’s own numbers got sharper and more specific: TypeSafe’s recorded demo has it finishing in 0.114s for $0.000081 against GPT-5.6 Terra’s 8.566s for $0.013880 — the 193.6× faster, 444.6× cheaper figure — on workflows its own team wrote, which TypeSafe says should sit at the high end of real use. Input is $0.042 per 1M tokens, access is a waitlisted API, and no weights are published. The first outside datapoint cuts the other way: Bryo AI’s CTO found Gemini slightly more accurate on email triage at 10–20× the cost.

Jev (Sep 16) goes further than either and abandons decoding altogether. TypeSafe AI, founded by ChatGPT co-creator Diogo Almeida on $40M, calls it a “System One Model”: it takes program state and returns a typed probabilistic value, trained by a method the lab names RLCD — Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions. Diffusion decoding parallelises within one token stream; Jev removes the stream, so many independent decisions resolve at once rather than one sequence faster. The claimed numbers follow from that: 20–200× faster, 40–400× cheaper, responses from 70 ms, and output tokens billed at zero because there are none to generate. The target workload is the unglamorous half of an agent run — routing, scoring, extraction, moderation, workflow branching, verifying another model's output — which is exactly the traffic the cache and routing work keeps trying to move off the expensive model. Two things need separating. “Cannot hallucinate” is a property of the output format, not of the answer: a typed value is well-formed by construction and can still be the wrong value, and a miscalibrated router is a harder failure to see than a fabricated sentence. And every figure is the vendor's, on a model in early access, with no independent measurement of calibration yet published.

Retrieval & embedding models

The release tier now extends past generative models to retrieval. Liquid AI's LFM2.5-Embedding-350M (dense bi-encoder) and LFM2.5-ColBERT-350M (late interaction) ship two distinct retrieval patterns in one release — bi-encoder for speed, ColBERT for precision — both at 350M parameters, both trained on 28T tokens, and both covering 11 languages, available on Hugging Face. The notable bit is how they were built: by patching LFM2.5-350M, a causal decoder, into a bidirectional encoder rather than training an encoder from scratch, reusing a large generative pretrain for retrieval and reportedly beating larger dedicated embedding models. Positioned as a drop-in upgrade for existing multilingual RAG pipelines and cheaper to run than the models they beat. (Architecture angle on the Research papers page.) NVIDIA's Nemotron-3-Embed (July 17) adds a multilingual embedding collection at 1B and 8B sizes; the 8B checkpoint, built on Ministral-3-8B and producing 4096-dim dense vectors, ranks #1 overall on RTEB (the Retrieval Embedding Benchmark) at 78.5 while lowering token spend versus rivals — open weights, and a strong candidate default retrieval backbone for enterprise agent/RAG stacks now that it tops the leaderboard and costs less to run. Liquid AI extended the line on July 30 with LFM2.5-Encoder-230M/350M: bidirectional encoders built to stay fast at 8K context on CPU for the classifier/router/safety-filter tier — the 350M ranks fourth of 14 on a 17-task mean (81.02) with only far larger models ahead, and the 230M runs an 8K forward pass in ~28s on CPU versus 90s+ for ModernBERT-base (LFM Open License v1.0, 15 languages).

Speech & audio models

The speech tier closed out a nine-day sprint on Sep 11 in which accuracy stopped being the argument. Google's Gemini 3.5 Transcribe (Aug 26), Meta's Muse Voice Transcribe (Sep 1) and Microsoft's MAI-Transcribe-2 (Sep 3) all now sit under 4% word error rate — Muse lowest among streaming models at 3.1%, MAI lowest non-streaming at 2% and leading multilingual FLEURS at 5.2% — so the competition has moved entirely to latency, price and language coverage, where the spread is wide: $0.10 an audio hour for MAI, $0.18 for Muse, roughly $0.30 for pre-recorded Gemini and $0.54 streaming. The one genuinely new mechanism is Meta's adaptive delay: audio is cut into 80-millisecond chunks, 12.5 a second, each compressed to a single soft token, and at every chunk the model chooses between emitting text and emitting a “next audio” token that buys it more context — trained with a reward for transcribing correctly against a penalty for taking too long, so the accuracy/latency trade is learned rather than configured.

Two API-only releases extended the tier on Sep 18–19, both competing on a number that is not accuracy. SpaceXAI’s Grok Voice Transcribe 2.0 reports short-phrase word error rate across 19 languages falling from 20.6% to 6.8% and rank 1 of 32 streaming models on Artificial Analysis, priced at $0.10 per audio hour batch and $0.20 streaming — which is the first time this section can put a transcription claim and its price in the same sentence from the vendor itself. Alibaba’s Qwen3.8-LiveTranslate competes on lag instead, cutting average LAAL from 2.8s to 2.3s while understanding 60 languages and speaking 29, with speaker diarization and synchronized bilingual display, at about $1.54 per hour of speech in and out. Half a second off interpretation lag is the difference between a translation you wait for and one you talk over, which is the same conversational-latency argument Gemini 3.8 Live made on the generation side. Neither ships weights, both report their own numbers, and the WER figure rests on four internal test sets — so the prices are the checkable part and the accuracy is not.

On the generation side GPT-Live-1 moved from a ChatGPT feature to an API product on Sep 11 at $0.05 per minute for the voice layer, with the backend model and harness billed separately, 12 voices, and tone and pacing directed from the system prompt; early tests report 80% fewer interruptions than turn-based systems. Read the three transcription models and GPT-Live together and the voice pipeline this section has described for a year — ASR, then an LLM, then TTS — is being collapsed from both ends at once. Before all that, speech-to-text saw two strong June 8 releases. Microsoft's MAI-Transcribe-1.5 leads the accuracy-speed Pareto frontier in its tier — 2.4% WER, 43 languages, an hour of audio transcribed in under 15 seconds, and best-in-class FLEURS accuracy — available on Azure AI Foundry.

NVIDIA's Nemotron 3.5 ASR takes the local-streaming angle: a single 600M-parameter cache-aware streaming checkpoint covering 40 language-locale pairs with native punctuation/capitalization and runtime language switching, running fully local via ONNX (on NGC, Hugging Face, and the Together AI API). Together with Miso Labs' MisoTTS on the generation side (see Applications), the open/efficient speech stack now spans both transcription and emotive synthesis. On the proprietary side, Google's Gemini 3.5 Live Translate (June 9) pushed real-time multilingual voice into a standard API call: streaming speech-to-speech across 70+ languages with auto language detection and near-real-time latency that preserves the speaker's original intonation, pacing, and pitch rather than substituting a robotic voice. It ships across the Gemini Live API, Google AI Studio, Google Meet, and Google Translate (Android + iOS). Two July additions widen the audio tier further: OpenAI's GPT-Live (GPT-Live-1 and GPT-Live-1 mini) brings full-duplex voice — models that listen and speak at the same time rather than strict turn-taking — to ChatGPT Voice, the missing piece for voice agents that can interrupt and backchannel naturally; and NVIDIA's Audex (Nemotron-Labs-Audex-30B-A3B, built on the Nemotron-Cascade-2-30B-A3B text MoE) folds speech recognition, translation, TTS, and audio generation into a single decoder while claiming to preserve the backbone's text-reasoning quality — sidestepping the usual accuracy tax of bolting audio onto a language model. PolyAI’s Dialog-RSN-1 (July 30) pushes the dialog stack itself audio-native: one model fuses turn-taking (emitted as the first output token: EMPTY, ONGOING, COMPLETE), speech recognition, function calling, and response generation, listening to raw audio so tone and hesitation survive instead of being discarded at the ASR boundary — sub-300ms responses, +11% relative containment and −37% latency in customer deployments, with TTS kept separate; English only and proprietary (no open weights or public API).

Google's Gemini 3.5 Transcribe (Aug 27) extends that same logic one step further: alongside the expected handling of noise, jargon, fillers, mid-sentence language switches, speaker attribution and word-level timestamps, it can invoke other Gemini models during a transcript to generate images or analyse a referenced file. Where Dialog-RSN-1 fuses the dialog stack so tone survives the ASR boundary, Transcribe makes the transcript itself an agent loop — and Microsoft's MAI-Transcribe-2 attacks the same tier from the price side at ten cents an audio hour. Transcription is no longer a commodity preprocessing step in any of the three designs. The open side of synthesis picked up a small entrant with an unusually specific pitch on Sep 9: Rumik's Rumik OSS 1 packs 22 languages, code-switching, four multilingual voices, delivery controls and precisely placed vocalizations — laughter, sighs — into 3B parameters. It beat Cartesia and ElevenLabs on Rumik's own emotion benchmark and remained far behind Gemini, a self-scored comparison whose ranking is worth much less than the capability it demonstrates. The non-commercial licence keeps it out of production regardless, so its value is as evidence that controllable paralinguistics fit in a model this size.

StepAudio 3 Gen (Sep 15) collapses the generation side along a different axis from everything above it. Where GPT-Live and Dialog-RSN-1 fuse the pipeline — ASR, LLM, TTS folded into one path so tone survives — StepFun fuses the audio kinds: one discrete autoregressive model over a shared RVQ token space producing speech, designed voices, vocals, sound effects, music and mixed audio, reporting state-of-the-art text-to-speech and voice design while keeping broad general-audio generation. The retention claim is the one to check, because unifying tasks this unlike usually costs the specialist capability, and a self-reported SOTA alongside a breadth claim is exactly the combination this page has learned to read with the construct-validity caveat attached. If it holds, the consequence is architectural rather than competitive: a shared tokenizer means a mixed soundtrack is one generation instead of a stitching problem, and the boundary between a speech model and an audio model stops being a product category. Read with Rumik OSS 1's paralinguistics at 3B, the open question for this tier is no longer whether one model can do several audio jobs but what the quality tax for doing so actually is — and nobody has published an independent measurement of it.

Google answered the pipeline half of that question a day later, and the answer is a product rather than a paper. Gemini 3.8 Live and Gemini 3.8 Live Extended Thinking (Sep 16) ship real-time voice with transcription, speech translation, text-to-speech and music generation on one API surface across 97 languages, with Gemini 3.5 Transcribe released alongside them. The mechanism that matters is in the Extended Thinking variant: it speaks while it reasons, calls tools and works multi-step requests in the background, and can be interrupted mid-utterance. Every voice design on this page so far has treated latency as something to minimise; this treats it as something to cover. A model that can acknowledge the speaker, keep talking and return to the slow thought does not need the thought to be fast, which is a different escape from the turn-taking problem than PolyAI's first-token turn signal or Meta's learned adaptive delay. Read against StepAudio 3 Gen directly above, the two collapses have now met: StepFun fuses the audio kinds, Google fuses the stages, and the stitched ASR→LLM→TTS stack that defined this section a year ago no longer describes any current release. What remains unmeasured on Google's side is the same thing unmeasured on StepFun's — whether parallel reasoning under a live audio stream costs accuracy against the same model answering at leisure, which no published evaluation separates.

Video & omni-modal generation

Video generation is consolidating the way text models did: from a drawer of expert models to one unified paradigm. MiniMax's H3 (Aug 1) folds text-to-video, image-to-video, first-and-last-frame, subject reference, and editing into a single omni-modal pretraining setup where the relationships between inputs are expressed in natural language. The licence, examined more closely in August, complicates the "open" framing: only the 768p base module is downloadable — the Context-IR prompt-reasoning module and the Regenerate-2K upscaler stay proprietary — distilling any model on H3 output is barred, and the United States, United Kingdom, European Union and South Korea are named "excluded territories" whose users must apply to MiniMax before using the weights on the same terms as everyone else. On capability the model is not in doubt: it debuted first on Artificial Analysis' video-editing leaderboard and within margin of error of first on text-to-video and image-to-video. Output is 2K at four to fifteen seconds (integer durations only) with native stereo audio; the H3-VAE's compression claims a 4× gain in effective sequence length — the piece that makes native 2K economical — and training throughput is reported up nearly 30%. Notably it is the exception to the current "leave the model alone" pattern: a genuinely rebuilt stack, and the one recent release you cannot download — open weights are promised in the coming days but only the API is available today. Earlier entries in this thread: ByteDance's unified video model and MiniMax's own M3 (multimodal, 1M context).

The omni tier gained a context length rather than a modality on Sep 18. Alibaba’s Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash is natively omnimodal across text, image, audio and video with a 1M-token context window, which is what lets a single model hold a long video and a long document at once instead of summarising one into the other. Alibaba reports audio-visual performance close to Gemini 3.8 Flash and overall audio performance above it. It is on the Qianwen AI Platform with no weights and no per-benchmark table, so the comparison to Gemini is entirely the vendor’s — the pattern this section has recorded for every omni release this quarter, where the modality list is verifiable and the ranking is not.

Time-series & forecasting models

A category that had stayed outside the release cycle entered it this cycle. Google's TimesFM-3 (Sep 1) is a 330M-parameter time-series foundation model pretrained on more than 1 trillion time points, adding zero-shot forecasting across multiple targets simultaneously and accepting both historical and known-future covariates without task-specific fine-tuning. The size is the point: a third of a billion parameters is servable on commodity hardware, which is where demand forecasting, capacity planning and anomaly detection actually run — not on the GPU fleet reserved for the frontier tier. It generalizes the same move the retrieval tier made when embeddings became a release category rather than a training exercise: a pretrained backbone displaces per-dataset model fitting, and the interesting question shifts from architecture to whether the zero-shot result beats the statistical baseline a team already has.
