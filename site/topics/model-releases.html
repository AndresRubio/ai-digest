Model releases & updates

Last updated: 2026-10-09

Current state

The Oct 8 cycle filled in the cheap end of the frontier and opened two decision-model weights. Anthropic’s Claude Haiku 5.5 lands at $0.10/$0.50 per million tokens up to 100K with a 1M context, the same list price as GPT-6 Luna, and claims 72.4% on OSWorld 2.1 against Luna’s 48.9% — but the price rises 5× past 100K and a new tokenizer counts about 30% more tokens, so the “75% cheaper than Haiku 4.5” figure is an average that depends on prompt length. Liquid put d1 on Hugging Face as d1-3B and d1-omni-600M, the first decision models from a lab whose hosted version was already ranked, at 48.57 on its own run of the Decision Index. Perplexity’s pplx-embed-v2-late takes the opposite trade from last week’s single-vector model, keeping a vector per token for 92.4% on MADQA at the cost of an index that grows with document length. GPT-6 reached all 1.2 billion ChatGPT users with generated interfaces, and LlamaIndex’s OpenDocRouter put the parsing tier behind one versioned API.

The Oct 7 cycle was a European trillion-parameter model and the frontier lab pricing the decision interface. Mistral’s Mistral Large 4 is a 1.05T MoE with 49B active over a 1M context, trained on 3,800 Grace Blackwell GPUs in EU datacentres, with cyber scores (93% on Cybench) that closed models often refuse to produce, API pricing of $1.36/$4.18, and weights promised by the end of October — a second large MoE in two days announced ahead of its weights, after Beam. OpenAI’s Decisions API left limited preview for public beta at $0.10 per million input tokens with no output charge and up to 10× Luna’s speed, which is now the price every open decision model below is measured against; Nace.AI’s Drex DLM adds an 8B diffusion decoder to that field at 52.31 on Decision Index 0.2, under CC BY-NC. Google’s EmbeddingGemma 2 puts text, code, images, audio and video into one 768-d space at 740M parameters under Apache 2.0, with MTEB Code up ten points and a stated floor at 256 dimensions, and Nano Banana 2.1 arrives as a Gemini 3.6 Flash-based image model described rather than scored.

The Oct 6 cycle added one announced release and one new capability. Reflection’s Beam is a 501B-total, 23B-active MoE trained on 23.8T tokens with more than 100M RL rollouts, in the same active-parameter band as the large MoEs already here — but its weights are promised for later in October, so for now it is a spec sheet rather than something to run. Liquid AI’s d1 gained vision: a decision can now take images as well as text, and Liquid claims a 19–200× cost advantage over GPT-6.1 Sol and Opus 5.5 with text decisions in 200–300 ms, while size, context window and weights all stay undisclosed.

The Oct 5 cycle added no release, only where one now runs. Claude Sonnet 5.5 has become the model behind claude.ai’s free tier, against ChatGPT’s Luna 5.6, which Simon Willison reads as the more capable free offering of the two. It also inherits Opus 5.5’s max-effort failure: 128,000 thinking tokens and $1.28 spent on a one-line SVG prompt with no output at all, where “xhigh” finished in 41 seconds for under six cents — so on both models the top setting is a cost to opt into, not a default.

The Oct 2–5 cycle turned the System One line into an open-weights field within two days. Cloudflare’s Clef and Clef-flash are Apache-2.0, Jev-API compatible and lead Jev on 7 of 10 of Cloudflare’s own tests at 209 and 39 ms; AWS’s Strands Decider 2B publishes its training recipe and a calibration error of 0.052; Perplexity’s pplx-decider-v1-27b is a Qwen3.8-27B fine-tune; and kev grew into a family of four from 0.8B to 27B. Every comparison is still run on a suite its publisher chose, so the question the section lead asks — whether anyone outside the vendor has measured it — has multiplied rather than been answered.

Earlier cycles

The rest of the cycle was priced or sized rather than ranked. Microsoft’s MAI-Transcribe-2-Streaming takes first of 38 on Artificial Analysis’ streaming WER at 2.5% but charges $0.54 an hour, above xAI and Meta, while Cactus’s Whistle fits seven languages into 16.8 MB with no accuracy figure at all. Aleph Alpha’s Kolibri runs 3.46B of 78.1B parameters per token over a 1M context and sells sovereignty rather than rank. Retrieval gained Embed 5, a two-tier shared space scored partly on Cohere’s own metric, and pplx-embed-v2-context, 14.4 points up on a private benchmark; NVIDIA’s Kumo Tabular tops TabArena on a licence its rivals lack; and Tavus is keeping Griffin off its API because 48% of testers took it for a human. Two older entries gained outside numbers: MiMo-V2.6-Pro now leads open models on Artificial Analysis at 46 with its RL bill disclosed, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash holds its cache to 890 bytes a token.

The Oct 2 cycle added no release, only the terms around one. Gemini 4 Argon was picked up by three more newsletters, and the new fact is about use rather than score: Argon-powered agents are already migrating Google’s own C/C++ codebases to Rust, while outside customers still have no date for enterprise or consumer access. Every use reported so far is inside Google or its vetted cyber-defender programme, so the model with the best DeepSWE number on this page remains the one fewest readers can call.

The Oct 1 cycle was OpenAI’s DevDay and a gated answer from Google. GPT-6.1 Sol claims Astra-level DeepSWE v1.1 at $2/$10 per million tokens, the same list price as Sonnet 5.5, with a 1.05M-token context and a 2× input surcharge past 272K; Gemini 4 Argon leads Opus 5.5 on DeepSWE at 77.9% and writes up to 1M output tokens, but only vetted cyber defenders can call it, which makes its numbers the least checkable on the page. Underneath, the System One line gained two more vendors in two days: Liquid’s d1 returns probabilities with zero output tokens and claims to beat Jev on Hugging Face’s Decision Index, and OpenAI’s Decisions API puts GPT-6 Luna behind the same typed interface in limited preview, with no figures yet. Alibaba’s Qwen-Audio-3.1-Realtime adds a turn-taking decision layer at 85% below its previous price while conceding a stop latency three times GPT-Realtime-2’s.

The Sep 30 cycle added no release, only outside numbers for Sonnet 5.5. Artificial Analysis puts Claude Sonnet 5.5 two points behind Opus 5.5 at max effort on its Intelligence Index while using about 60% more tokens to get there, with a lower hallucination rate and weaker factual knowledge — which sits beside, not against, Anthropic’s claim of up to 30% lower cost per task, since that comparison is with Sonnet 5 rather than with Opus. Eleven v4 is now linked to ElevenLabs’ own post, and Ember-1 is reachable through the Cline desktop beta as well as Fireworks.

The Sep 29 cycle filled in the tier below the frontier. Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet 5.5 lands at $2/$10 with 70.6% on Terminal-Bench 4.0, above Opus 5.5’s 66.4% and eight points behind it on FrontierCode, and is the first Sonnet to route high-risk cyber requests to an older model. Fireworks’ Ember-1 got its numbers — Terminal Bench 2.1 at 82.0% against K3 Max’s 80.9% for 51.9% less, output tokens down from 49.3K to 29.9K per task in production — and Julia 1 got the Jev comparison it lacked, ahead on two pilots and well behind on Banking77. Speech gained two priced releases: ElevenLabs’ Eleven v4 at 90 languages and about 100 ms, and Sarvam’s Saaras V4 for 22 Indian languages at ₹30 an hour, the per-hour figure this page keeps asking for. Liquid’s DSpark drafter gives a 3B vision model 2.3–3.1× faster decoding with identical greedy output. And Opus 5.5 at max effort can exhaust its 128,000-token output ceiling while still thinking, the first measured cost of a setting the launch did not price.

The Sep 28 cycle widens the System One line from one vendor’s API into a field of small, open decision models. CLM-8B puts a 75 MB Apache-2.0 head on a frozen Qwen3-8B encoder and matches Jev on two games at up to 9× lower latency while trailing on BFCL v4 tool calling; Fastino’s GLiNER2.5-Decide answers typed questions in 167 ms on a CPU at 340M parameters; and Julia 1 runs at 144.3M on a tablet. Every accuracy number in the cluster still comes from the vendor’s own suite, which is the gap the section lead names. Speech gained Nemotron 3 Diarization, first of 12 systems on Diarization-Bench at 14.72% DER with its one regression disclosed, and Live Avatar for Gemini 3.8 Live; ThinkingCap trades 0.86 points of accuracy for 37.2% fewer thinking tokens. Opus 5.5 now leads both Artificial Analysis and Vals AI, at a higher cost per task than its token price suggests.

The Sep 24 cycle was speech and cheaper decisions. Google’s Gemini 3.8 Flash TTS and Flash-Lite TTS (Sep 24) design a voice from a text description, take line-by-line direction across 100-plus languages, and clone an authorised voice from a 30-second sample. The rankings cited are Hume AI’s leaderboards as Google reports them, and no price per audio hour came with the launch — the number this page judges speech on. The System One line gained its cheapest entry: Together AI’s tev1-4B-experimental is a Jev-style classifier on Qwen3.5 4B that cost $17 to train and charges $0.042 per million input tokens and nothing for output, with no accuracy figure. Fireworks’ Ember-1 is the post-training route taken by an inference provider rather than a lab: Kimi K3 quality on 40% fewer tokens by its own measure, served for a two-week research window. The through-line is the one the Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Sol repricing set two days earlier: releases are argued in cost per task, and in all three the cost is the checkable half.

The Sep 22 cycle was two frontier labs repricing within an hour of each other. Anthropic’s Claude Opus 5.5 (Sep 22) claims Fable 5.1-level work at $4/$20 per million tokens, 40% below Opus 5, with cache reads cut to $0.20 and 66.4% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 against Fable 5.1’s 55.8% — while Anthropic concedes the real-use gap is narrower than that and GPT-6 Astra keeps two benchmarks. OpenAI’s GPT-6 Sol and Luna (Sep 22) answered at $2/$10 and $0.10/$0.50, half GPT-5.6’s promotional pricing, with Sol 1.1 points behind Fable 5 on DeepSWE. The through-line is that the frontier tier now competes on what a task costs at a fixed capability rather than on the capability itself. Artificial Analysis’s 48 for Sol is the only outside number in either launch, and the longer price curve is on Industry & business.

The System One line kept widening underneath. Nokia’s AnyJev makes any open LLM answer in Jev’s typed form without training, cutting BANKING77 order-flips from 0.230 to 0.073 on Qwen3-8B; Jev-Omni carries the decision format to images, audio and video on Gemma 4 12B, with no accuracy figure; and an outside JevBench now ranks the class (see Evals). On speech, Kyutai’s Voice of Reason lifts spoken GSM8K from 27.3% to 77.1% with no transcript — and publishes that a cascaded pipeline still scores 95.7%, which is the honest measure of how far speech-native reasoning has to go.

The Sep 22 cycle was a lab publishing the recipe along with the weights. Xiaomi’s MiMo-V2.6-Pro and Flash (Sep 22) are MIT-licensed omni-modal models with a 1M-token context, and Pro’s self-reported 53.1 on AutomationBench sits ahead of the Claude Opus 5 score Xiaomi lists beside it; the technical report, RL environments and RL code shipped too, so the result is reproducible in principle rather than merely downloadable — the method is on Training & RL. StepFun’s Step 5 Preview (600B total, 27B active) claims parity with Kimi K3 at about 2.8× lower cost per task, with nothing behind it but a thread.

The rest of the cycle filled in stories already here. Grok 4.7 gained a CursorBench 4.0 figure — 46.3% against Grok 4.6’s 40.4% — and a fast variant at double the speed and double the price. kev grew from one small checkpoint to 0.6B, 4B and 8B on Qwen 3.5, none yet with an out-of-domain number. And Jev acquired its first accuracy figure, about 68% on TypeSafe’s own workflow evaluation, along with $5 of starter credit and a signup pause under demand.

The Sep 21 cycle was the vendor comparison table doing the work the vendor did not intend. SpaceXAI’s Grok 4.7 (Sep 21) is a larger base model shipped at Grok 4.6’s exact price — $2 in, $6 out per million tokens, 500K context, four reasoning levels to xhigh — and it leads on EEBench at 64.0% and Harvey’s legal benchmark at 19.6%. The interesting number is the one it concedes: on the same table Claude Fable 5.1 Max wins four of seven, and Terminal-Bench 4.0 reads 57.9% against 38.0%. A launch post publishing a nineteen-point agentic-coding deficit tells you what the model is for, which is bulk reasoning at a fixed price rather than long-horizon tool use. Every figure is self-reported, as is now standard across this page.

The cycle before that was unusually honest about what compression and speed actually cost. PrismML’s Ternary Bonsai 2 27B (Sep 18) is the headline: Qwen3.8-27B at 1.76 effective bits per weight, 5.93GB against 53.80GB in FP16, Apache 2.0, 262K context, image input, 142.5 tokens/second on an RTX 5090, and an average of 83.9 across 20 benchmarks against the parent’s 85.4. PrismML leads with that 98.2% and then publishes the two figures that contradict it: long-horizon agent work keeps about 75%, and SWE-bench Verified falls to 60.8 from 80.6. That is the finding this thread has needed since the first Bonsai — sub-2-bit quantization is nearly free on single-turn knowledge questions and expensive on anything that must stay coherent across steps — which makes the headline average the wrong number to buy on. Stock llama.cpp cannot load it, and all results are the vendor’s.

The System One category stopped being one company’s claim. Jared Palmer’s kev (Sep 20) is an Apache-2.0 decision model on Qwen2.5-0.5B answering six questions in about 160ms on Apple Silicon behind a TypeSafe-compatible API — and its README states that Jev leads it by roughly 19 points out of domain, which is the first outside sizing of what the closed model buys. Underneath both sits a cheaper baseline: Goedecke’s point that batching single-token prompts with structured output turns any LLM into a System One model, leaving the architecture’s real advantage as latency and price rather than capability. Jev’s own numbers sharpened in the same window — 0.114s and $0.000081 against GPT-5.6 Terra’s 8.566s and $0.013880, on workflows TypeSafe wrote — and drew their first contrary datapoint, with Bryo AI’s CTO finding Gemini slightly more accurate on email triage at 10–20× the cost. On Sep 21 the waitlist came off and the API opened to everyone, which is what turned the category from a demo into something people are measuring: the question surface is three types (a Bernoulli-derived yes/no, a choice with a distribution, a score along described levels), input undercuts GPT-5 Nano at $0.042 against $0.05 per 1M with output unbilled, and a JevBench has already appeared to compare Jev-class models. The counterweight arrived with it: a float is the entire output, so a wrong decision leaves nothing to inspect — which makes evals mandatory rather than advisable — and the author of Laya claims the same on-device approach 50× faster and published back in March 2025.

Alibaba shipped both ends of the multimodal tier in two days. Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash (Sep 18) is natively omnimodal across text, image, audio and video at a 1M-token context window, claimed close to Gemini 3.8 Flash on audio-visual and above it on audio; Qwen3.8-LiveTranslate (Sep 19) cuts interpretation lag from 2.8s to 2.3s across 60 languages understood and 29 spoken, with diarization and bilingual display at about $1.54 per hour of speech. SpaceXAI’s Grok Voice Transcribe 2.0 (Sep 18) competed on the same axis from the accuracy side, reporting 19-language short-phrase WER falling from 20.6% to 6.8% at $0.10 per audio hour. None of the three ships weights and every ranking is self-reported, so what is genuinely checkable this cycle is the pricing, which all three published — a change worth noting on its own. Two Sep 21 releases carry that pricing habit into vision. Qwen-Image-2.1 folds text-to-image, editing against up to ten references and native RGBA into one open-weight checkpoint, scoring 60.28 on Qwen’s own benchmark against Nano Banana 2.0’s 59.82 — with the caveat that the advertised 7B is the DiT alone and an 8B Qwen3-VL encoder loads beside it, and that the licence is research-only. Meta’s SAM 3.1 goes the other way entirely: a segmentation family that used to arrive as weights now arrives as a metered endpoint at $2.50 per 1,000 images and $0.20 per 1,000 video frames. Read together, the open release hides its true size in a second checkpoint and the closed one publishes an honest per-frame cost.

Today's cycle has no frontier launch and three releases that are each interesting for the number they concede. The most-discussed has no vendor: Union Alpha appeared as a stealth listing on OpenRouter and became one of its most-used newcomers of the week, reported to rival GPT-6 Astra and Opus 5 on DeepSWE and to beat GPT-5.6 Sol on Terminal-Bench at roughly 18× lower cost per task, with 256K context and latency that spikes under load. The page has watched this close before — “Ox Alpha” ran anonymously for weeks and was GLM-5.3-Flash — so it is a lead, not a result. The genuinely new part is the venue: a model can now accumulate production traffic and a public price/performance reputation before anyone knows who trained it, which means the cost figure is checkable by anyone with an account and the provenance, training data and retention policy by nobody.

The two open-weights arrivals are narrow by design. Ant Group's Ling-3.0-flash-Fin, built with financial institutions for source checking, valuation spreadsheets and report writing, scores 23 on Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index and 24 on its Finance & Accounting Index — a one-point domain advantage on the index built for its own domain, which is worth more than the claim it was announced with. Knowledgator's GLiFormer is the sharper result: a 575M-parameter encoder emitting nested JSON extractions with no token generation at all, at 91.10 F1 against GPT-5.6-luna's 91.96 on the same 500 examples, Apache 2.0. If it holds, structured extraction does not need a generative model, and the cost gap between a 575M encoder and a frontier API for that job is large. Both caveats are the authors': the scores are self-reported, and the “up to 95.8× faster” headline is an analytical estimate rather than a measured run.

Today's three releases agree on something the frontier tier has spent the year contradicting: the way to win a workload is to stop competing for all of them. Jev (Sep 16) is the most literal case. TypeSafe AI, founded by ChatGPT co-creator Diogo Almeida on $40M, shipped a model that deliberately cannot write — you hand it program state and it returns a typed probabilistic value, trained by a method it calls RLCD. Removing the token stream is what buys the claimed 20–200× speed and 40–400× cost advantage, along with free output tokens and 70 ms responses, because independent decisions resolve in parallel instead of one sequence resolving faster. Its target is the unglamorous traffic before reasoning starts: routing, scoring, extraction, moderation, verification. Periodic Neon makes the same bet on data rather than architecture, beating GPT-6 Astra and Fable 5.1 on FrontierXRD at a lower cost per analysis after midtraining and RL on laboratory data, and it is already deployed in labs working on superconductors and magnets. Both claims are the vendor's, and both carry a specific soft spot: “cannot hallucinate” describes Jev's output format rather than its correctness, and a miscalibrated router is harder to notice than a fabricated sentence.

Gemini 3.8 Live and Live Extended Thinking are the exception that completes the cycle rather than contradicting it, and they close the audio thread StepAudio opened a day earlier. Google put transcription, speech translation, text-to-speech and music generation on one API across 97 languages, with Gemini 3.5 Transcribe alongside, and gave the Extended Thinking variant the ability to speak while it reasons, call tools in the background and be interrupted mid-utterance. Every voice release tracked here so far treats latency as something to minimise; this treats it as something to cover, which is a different escape from turn-taking than PolyAI's first-token turn signal or Meta's learned adaptive delay. Set against StepAudio 3 Gen (Sep 15) — one discrete autoregressive model over a shared RVQ token space generating speech, designed voices, vocals, effects, music and mixed audio — the two collapses have now met from opposite directions: StepFun fuses the audio kinds, Google fuses the stages, and the stitched ASR→LLM→TTS pipeline no longer describes any current release. Neither has published what the unification costs: StepFun's SOTA-plus-breadth figures are self-reported, and nobody has separated Gemini's accuracy while reasoning under a live stream from the same model answering at leisure.

The prior cycle added no new model, and instead sorted out what the two already on the page are actually good at. Zvi Mowshowitz's extended assessment of GPT-6 Astra (Sep 14) puts its raw capability at the top of the field while separating where the gain lands: the dramatic jumps are in 3D reasoning, games, computer use and subagent coordination, and coding is very good but explicitly not a quantum leap on GPT-5.6 Sol. That is the distribution to route work by, and it matches the architectural reading on the Research papers page, which names computer use as the capability a looped architecture should improve most. He also notes OpenAI has soft-announced an internal model a tier above Astra — the same one reported to have run the Navier–Stokes campaign, and still unreleased, which means the public frontier and the actual frontier are now separated by a gap nobody outside the lab can size.

Two entries gained the detail that decides whether they are usable. Sakana's Fugu Ultra v2 turns out to expose a full frontier API surface — configurable reasoning effort, function calling, structured outputs, image and PDF input, built-in web search — and can recursively call instances of itself alongside its fixed pool of open and specialist models. Its routing-beats-scale cost claim therefore costs nothing in integration, which is what makes it worth taking seriously rather than filing as a niche trade-off. And ChatGPT Images 2.5 is two models, not one: gpt-image-2.5-sunburst and gpt-image-2.5-flare, with OpenAI recommending Sunburst where editing precision matters and Flare for fast everyday generation — so the editing restraint that made the release interesting for pipeline work is Sunburst's specifically. OpenAI puts combined usage above 3 billion images. Both corrections point the same way: launch copy describes a release, and the model ID is what you actually deploy.

Five releases this cycle, and not one of them competes on raw capability. Cognition's SWE-2 (Sep 11) reaches 50.0% on FrontierCode 1.1 Main1 — within a point of Claude Fable 5.1, level with GPT-5.6 Sol — at 64% lower cost, and the gain over its own predecessor is behavioural rather than scalar: 58% fewer turns, a median of 18 steps to the first meaningful edit against SWE-1.7's 48. Sakana's Fugu Max and Fugu Ultra v2 make the same argument structurally, routing work across a swappable pool of open and specialist models instead of sending every problem to the largest one, claiming elite-adjacent performance at two to six times lower cost and, for Ultra v2, beating Opus 5 and Fable 5 on Sakana's own Chartography benchmark without invoking a frontier model at any point — a vendor benchmark carrying an architectural claim worth separating from it. Cohere's North Small Translate spends 218B parameters (25B active) entirely on machine translation across 50 languages, open-weights, at a moment when almost every other open release chases general capability. GPT-Live-1 stops being a ChatGPT feature and becomes a line item: $0.05 per minute in the API for the voice layer alone, with the model and harness billed separately, and 80% fewer interruptions than turn-based pipelines. And Meta's Muse Voice Transcribe completes a three-way speech-to-text field in which all entrants now sit under 4% word error rate — 3.1% streaming for Muse at $0.18 an audio hour, 2.6% non-streaming for Gemini 3.5 Transcribe, 2% for MAI-Transcribe-2 at $0.10 — which retires accuracy as the axis of competition and leaves latency and price. Meta's contribution is a mechanism: adaptive delay, in which the model decides chunk by chunk whether to emit a token or keep listening, trained with a reward for correctness against a penalty for taking too long.

Independent evaluation caught up with the two frontier models the same week, and it mostly deflates the launch copy. GPT-6 Astra ties Fable 5.1 for first on Artificial Analysis' v4.3 index at 53 and sits third on the Vals Index, but leads computer use outright and does it at $19.09 and 25 minutes a task against Fable 5.1's $28.92 and 76; of the three problems Anthropic said Fable 5.1 would fix, only the safeguards loosened, and cost per task went up about 20% despite the 75% cache-read cut. Behind them, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (Sep 10) turns out to be a 552B MoE with native vision, retiring both V4-Flash and the two-week-old V4-Flash-Vision-Exp the same day; the asymmetric-architecture pitch cashes out as a cache a quarter the HBM and an eighth the SSD of the previous generation, serving at $0.30 per million input tokens with another 50% off peak. That is a serving-economics release, and it is now the one with benchmarks behind it — see Evals for what it costs to get 89% of a frontier model's bug-fix score.

Behind it, the August releases filling in under the frontier were notable for where their advantage came from: Microsoft's MAI-Code-1.1-Flash claimed better code at 25% greater token efficiency and roughly a quarter of the June model's cost (+22% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 in GitHub Copilot CLI, +15% on .NET), attributed not to a larger base model but to optimising against hundreds of thousands of RL environments harvested from real Copilot sessions — the IDE-ownership advantage cashed out as a model, shipped alongside MAI-Thinking-1 and MAI-Image-2.6. Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B makes the opposite trade explicit: it keeps Qwen3.5's architecture unchanged and spends the difference on post-training, shipping 2.4 trillion parameters with 95B active, day-one SGLang and vLLM paths rather than an API-only preview, and reasoning depth exposed as a reasoning_effort setting instead of a separate SKU — the same knob-not-a-model direction Grok 4.6 and GPT-5.6 Sol took the same week. Mistral OCR 4.1 is the narrowest release and the clearest about its own scope: a vision-multimodal model built to parse nested tables and hierarchical document structure and emit JSON or Markdown directly, rather than a flat text dump a downstream model has to re-infer structure from, priced to make general multimodal input look expensive for the same job (evaluation detail).

The cycle before this one had already established that the interesting number on a launch is no longer a benchmark. Inception Labs' Mercury 2.5 is the largest diffusion language model trained to date, and it is sold entirely on throughput and price: 1,107 output tokens per second on ordinary Nvidia GPUs, a 260K context, and $0.04/$0.15 per million tokens under an 80% launch discount, at quality it positions against GPT-5.6 Luna (Low), Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and Haiku 4.5 rather than against the frontier. Its vendor-stated “40% more intelligent” means nothing; Augment's reported drop in context-compaction latency from about 150 seconds to 27 means a great deal, because it is a number from someone who had to live with the old one. OpenAI's ChatGPT Images 2.5 sells the same way — up to 50% lower latency than Images 2.0, and an edit that changes one product or line of copy without disturbing the rest of the frame, which is the property that decides whether an image model can sit inside a pipeline at all.

Around them the specialists keep arriving with narrower claims and more honest licences. Rumik OSS 1 puts 22 languages, code-switching and deliberately placed laughter and sighs into 3B parameters, beats Cartesia and ElevenLabs on its own emotion benchmark, stays far behind Gemini, and ships non-commercial. DeepSeek's V4-Flash-Vision-Exp adds image understanding at close to Opus 4.8 on agent tasks and speaks OpenAI's Chat Completions and Responses APIs plus Anthropic's Messages endpoint, so it enters an existing harness without a client rewrite — an experimental line, clearly labelled as one. DeepReinforce's Ornith-1.5 is the odd one out and the most interesting: a 397B MoE flagship, a 35B MoE activating 3B per token, and a 9B dense model with a quantized Mobile build, all produced by a loop that now generates its own training tasks as well as its scaffolds and rollouts — a claim with no independent verification attached to it yet. LittleLearner, a 5B model capped at fifth-grade knowledge and runnable in the browser, is not a product at all but the instrument the others lack: a model whose training corpus is bounded tightly enough that “when did this capability appear, and on what” is an answerable question.

Behind this cycle sit the specialists that led the previous one, and they made the same argument in different form: Google's TimesFM-3 (330M parameters, over a trillion time points, zero-shot multivariate forecasting with covariates) is small enough to serve where forecasting workloads actually live; Gemini 3.5 Transcribe calls other Gemini models mid-transcript, turning transcription into an agent loop; Tencent's ContextPilot-14B moves context management into the weights rather than the harness; and NVIDIA's DeepSeek-V4-Pro-0813-NVFP4 shows the now-routine cycle of open weights followed within weeks by a vendor's serving-grade quantization.

At the frontier the news is arithmetic. GPT-6 Astra's operating envelope is now better documented than its benchmark: OpenAI's own product lead says Astra on low beats GPT-5.6 Sol on high, so a reasoning setting carried across from Sol is quota burned for nothing, and a serving change reportedly draws 3–4× less usage on some long-tail workloads — while one 44-minute max-effort render consumed 6.73M tokens and 15% of a weekly allowance. Claude Fable 5.1 lists at $10/$50 per million tokens with cache reads cut 75%, and Mythos 5.1 goes only to vetted cybersecurity and life-sciences organizations with monitoring data retained on their own infrastructure. Gemini 3.8 Flash is $0.75/$3.75 introductory through the end of the year, with Cyber limited to trusted testers and governments. The cheap model that defined last month kept paying off in the meantime: GLM-5.3-Flash — the anonymous "Ox Alpha" that absorbed 26 trillion tokens across 327,000 users in its first four days on OpenCode before Z.ai claimed it — is the architecturally serious half of the GLM-5.3 pair: pretrained from scratch on 30T multimodal tokens with vision native from the start, 320B total and 18B active, hybrid linear-plus-sparse attention cutting attention compute to about a third of GLM-5.3's, and an IndexPool step holding the KV cache under a quarter of it at 1M context. It scores 57 on the Intelligence Index at $0.09 per task, against $2.03 for the Claude Opus 4.8 score it matches.

It is also verbose and slow (150M tokens to complete the Index against a 110M median; 45 tok/s against 69 typical) — and it was served, for the entire free preview, on Chinese-made chips, which reframes cost-effective serving as a memory-optimization problem rather than a supply-of-NVIDIA one. At the other end of the spectrum, Thomson Reuters spent $40M over three months turning Qwen3.5 into Thomson, a 397B/17B legal-and-finance model it licenses for customers to run on their own hardware — sovereign AI at corporate scale, and a direct answer to the retention question on Safety & policy.

September's releases are less about capability than about what labs will now ship openly. GPT-6 Astra is OpenAI's first model rated Critical on its own cybersecurity scale, and its system card concedes that the training which made it more robust to prompt injection also made it better at controlling its chain of thought — well enough to evade monitors under adversarial conditions. Its headline AGI claim does not survive the harness question (see Evals). Claude Fable 5.1 more than doubled Terminal-Bench-Science to 52.6%, and hands-on testing surfaced what no benchmark reports: its low and medium reasoning levels emit no reasoning at all on some prompts, while xhigh costs eighteen times more and takes eight minutes — a reasoning dial whose bottom half may be inert. Gemini 3.8 Flash arrived with a Flash Cyber variant behind a restricted defender program, making Google the third lab in a month to gate a security-capable model rather than publish it, and Muse Spark 1.3 shipped with its top reasoning mode withheld pending safety testing. The clearest price move is MAI-Transcribe-2: 60 languages with diarization and word-level timestamps at ten cents an audio hour, continuing Microsoft's pattern of replacing OpenAI-powered features with its own models one modality at a time.

The month since August 4 answered the question the Aug 3 cycle raised. Alibaba kept its promise: weights for Qwen3.8-Max and Qwen3.8-27B shipped within a week of the API launch — the first Max-tier Qwen ever downloadable — but only the 27B is unrestricted (full capabilities, Apache 2.0, third among open models at 52 on the Intelligence Index, and the most capable checkpoint that runs on a consumer machine). The open Max is text-only, loses the 1M context, and carries revenue-threshold conditions, so the pattern holds: the flagship is a rental and the deployable model is its smaller sibling.

Three more releases fill in the frontier. Z.ai's GLM-5.3 (Aug 14) ties Kimi K3 at 60 on the Intelligence Index purely by scaling GLM-5.2's fine-tuning recipe onto more RL environments — no new architecture, no fresh pretrain — and takes the top CyberGym score, which is why its weights were held for two weeks of safety evaluation (see Safety & policy). SpaceXAI's Grok 4.6 (Aug 10) ties for third at 61 and posts the highest GPQA Diamond score yet recorded (94.9%), but its real result is efficiency: it matched near-frontier work in about half the turns and a quarter of the input tokens of Claude Opus 5, which is the number that decides what an agent costs to run. And DeepSeek-V4-Pro-0813 (Aug 13) graduated from preview with Terminal-Bench 2.1 at 87.9% — while simultaneously publishing the harness those numbers were produced in, exposing how much of an agentic score is scaffolding rather than model (independent harnesses measured the same checkpoint at 54.68 and 78.7%). Two smaller releases sharpen the local tier: Meta Superintelligence Labs' first open model, Muse Glimmer (30B multimodal, built for agents running on the user's own machine), and NVIDIA's Nemotron 3.5 Lightning, whose companion NeMo Switchyard router cut agentic task cost to about a third of Opus 4.8 by sending each step to the cheapest model that can do it. The month's cautionary release is MiniMax H3: state-of-the-art video generation, weights free to download, and a licence naming the US, UK, EU and South Korea as "excluded territories" whose users must apply for permission — open in the sense that matters least to the people most likely to want it.

The Aug 3 cycle lands three releases at once, all circling one question: what can you actually run? Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max moved from preview (July 20) to shipping — the 2.4-trillion-parameter MoE flagship is live as a hosted, OpenAI-compatible endpoint with 1M-token context, priced at $2.00 input / $6.00 output per 1M tokens with implicit cache reads at $0.25 (cached input eight times cheaper than fresh, so prefix stability drives cost more than prompt length), scoring 86.6 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and lifting FrontierSWE from 40.7 to 73.5 over its predecessor. Open weights are promised for next week alongside an open Qwen3.8-27B — the checkpoint that actually fits ordinary on-premise hardware — while the activated-parameter count remains undisclosed, so open-weights serving cost cannot yet be modeled. Thinking Machines Lab answered the deployability question head-on with Inkling-Small: a 276B-total / 12B-active open-weights multimodal MoE (Apache 2.0), distilled in part from its own 975B Inkling, that comes out ahead of its teacher — SWE-bench Verified 80.2% vs 77.6%, Humanity's Last Exam 31.6% vs 29.7%, ARC-AGI-2 40.1% vs 36.5% — with native reasoning over text, images, and audio at 1M context, and an NVFP4 checkpoint that fits a single B300 at 180GB aggregated VRAM against 600GB for BF16. The honest trade: factual recall regressed, with SimpleQA Verified falling to 20.6% from the teacher's 43.9%. And AMD shipped Instella-MoE-16B-A3B, a fully open MoE (16B total, 2.8B active: 2 shared experts plus 6 routed from 64) trained from scratch on Instinct MI300X/MI325X, publishing weights from every training stage plus data mixtures, training configs, and inference code — the strongest fully open base at a 76.7 average, though the weights carry a research-only ResearchRAIL license, making the MIT-licensed training code the more reusable asset.

These land on top of the two threads the Aug 1–3 releases had already sharpened: post-training as the upgrade lever, and multimodal unification. DeepSeek shipped DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731, an upgrade whose model card says the architecture and size are unchanged — the gains come from re-post-training alone: Terminal Bench 2.1 moves 61.8 to 82.7, DeepSWE 7.3 to 54.4, beating DeepSeek's own V4-Pro (Preview) on every agentic benchmark it published, with output priced at $0.28 per 1M tokens against $0.87 for V4-Pro (MIT, ungated weights; ~110GB at 3-bit self-hosted; every figure vendor-reported on an unreleased harness). By August 3 it was live on Ollama's US cloud, one launch command from Claude Code, Hermes, or OpenClaw — the same distribution path GLM-5.2 and Kimi K3 used. On the multimodal side, MiniMax's H3 folds the traditional drawer of video expert models (text-to-video, image-to-video, first-and-last-frame, subject reference, editing) into one omni-modal pretraining paradigm generating 4–15-second 2K clips with native stereo audio; H3-VAE compression claims a 4× gain in effective sequence length (what makes native 2K economical), but open weights are promised rather than shipped — API-only today.

Before that, two July 30 releases extended the speech and retrieval tiers. PolyAI’s Dialog-RSN-1 is an audio-native dialog model fusing turn-taking, speech recognition, function calling, and response generation in one model: it listens to the audio itself rather than an ASR transcript (so tone, hesitation, and doubt survive), emits turn-taking as its first output token (EMPTY, ONGOING, or COMPLETE), and keeps TTS separate so deployers own the voice — PolyAI reports sub-300ms responses, +11% relative containment at a restaurant group, and −37% latency at an insurer (English only, no open weights or public API). Liquid AI’s LFM2.5-Encoder-230M and -350M target the invisible workhorse tier — the encoders under classifiers, intent routers, safety filters, and PII detectors — as bidirectional encoders that stay fast at 8K context on CPU: the 350M posts an 81.02 mean across 17 tasks (fourth of 14, every model above it far larger), and the 230M runs an 8K forward pass in ~28s on CPU versus 90s+ for ModernBERT-base (LFM Open License v1.0, 15 languages, Apache-2.0 eval harness).

Anthropic's newest frontier model, Claude Opus 5 (July 25), holds the line on Opus pricing ($5/$25 per million tokens) while topping SWE-bench Verified and adding tuning for agentic coding and computer use — capability gains at a stable price point rather than a new tier. The open-weight trillion-parameter race kept moving in both directions the same week: Alibaba's Qwen team previewed Qwen3.8-Max (July 20), a 2.4-trillion-parameter multimodal MoE processing text, images, video, and documents and tuned for coding and full-stack development, which Alibaba positions as "second only to Claude Fable 5," live on Alibaba Cloud from $6/month — arriving days after Moonshot's Kimi K3. Pulling the opposite direction on scale, a developer fine-tuned OpenBMB's MiniCPM5-1B on Claude Fable-5 reasoning traces to ship a 657MB, MIT-licensed local "thinking model" that keeps MiniCPM5's native thinking template and tool-calling with a 128K context window on a single GPU, no API key or cloud calls required — extending the on-device distillation thread below PrismML's sub-2-bit Bonsai 27B to under a gigabyte. Poolside also shipped an agentic-coding mixture-of-experts model (July 22), reported only as a headline item with no further benchmarked detail available.

The open-weight scale record jumped again on July 17: Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 is a 2.8-trillion-parameter open MoE — comfortably the largest open-weight model shipped to date, well past Thinking Machines Lab's 975B Inkling and Tencent's 295B Hy3 from the same week — with native multimodal reasoning, a 1M-token context window, and a new Kimi Delta Attention mechanism that delivers up to 6.3x faster decoding at million-token context, the piece that keeps a model this large and this long-context from being economically unusable. Retrieval also got a new open-weight leader the same day: NVIDIA's Nemotron-3-Embed-8B ranks #1 overall on RTEB (78.5), built on Ministral-3-8B and mapping text into 4096-dim vectors while lowering token spend versus rivals — a candidate new default embedding backbone for enterprise agent/RAG stacks (see Agents & agentic systems for the retrieval angle).

The frontier coding race gained a structurally new kind of entrant on July 9: Grok 4.5, a 1.5-trillion-parameter model from SpaceXAI and Cursor trained across tens of thousands of NVIDIA GB300 GPUs with Cursor's proprietary coding data folded into the training mix, shipping simultaneously in Grok Build, inside Cursor, and via API at an aggressive $2-input price aimed explicitly under Opus-class models. It is the first frontier model co-trained in direct partnership with a coding-tool company rather than trained in isolation and integrated afterward — if the Cursor-data edge holds up in independent benchmarks, leverage in the coding-agent stack shifts toward tool companies, not just model labs (business angle on the Industry & business page). Voice is the other frontier move: OpenAI's GPT-Live (GPT-Live-1 and GPT-Live-1 mini) brings full-duplex speech — listening and speaking simultaneously rather than strict turn-taking — to ChatGPT Voice, and the same brief teased a GPT-5.6 launch clearing government review (no detail yet).

The proprietary tier still sets the top of the coding leaderboard via Anthropic's June 9 Claude Fable 5 (95.0% SWE-bench Verified, the highest recorded; 80.3% SWE-bench Pro; $10/$50 per million tokens), but its availability remains the bigger story: a June 12 US export-control directive named Fable 5 and Mythos 5 specifically and Anthropic disabled both for all foreign nationals within hours — the first government-ordered mid-deployment withdrawal of a frontier model (see Safety & policy). Fable 5 / Mythos 5 remain two configurations of one base model (Fable 5 safeguarded and public; Mythos 5 same weights with safeguards lifted, institution-only), now with a jurisdiction axis layered on top. China's open-weight labs keep accelerating: Tencent's Hy3 (295B sparse MoE, only 21B active per token, a 3.8B multi-token-prediction layer, 256K context, Apache 2.0) lands frontier-scale reasoning at 21B-active serving cost, joining Z.ai's GLM-5.2 (June 14; usable 1M context, MIT, now US-hosted on Ollama's Blackwell cloud and launchable into Claude Code/Codex/Hermes/OpenClaw) in a run of open releases actively reshaping where enterprises route token spend.

A Western lab joined the open-frontier-challenger tier on July 16: Mira Murati's Thinking Machines Lab shipped its first open model, Inkling, a 975B total / 41B active parameter MoE with native reasoning over text, image, and audio, a 1M-token context, and "controllable thinking effort" (dialing reasoning depth against cost) — not the top scorer on every benchmark, but positioned as the strongest open, fine-tunable base yet to challenge closed frontier labs. The broader open tier is defined by a sparse-activation efficiency race — Cohere's North Mini Code (30B/3B-active), Xiaomi's 1.02T MiMo (42B active, >1,000 tok/s), NVIDIA's 550B/55B Nemotron 3 Ultra — where total params rise, active params fall, and quality holds. Multimodal and alternative-decoding entries continue: NVIDIA's Audex (Nemotron-Labs-Audex-30B-A3B) folds ASR, translation, TTS, and audio generation into one decoder without regressing the text backbone; Google's DiffusionGemma decodes 256-token blocks in parallel at 1,000+ tok/s; Google's Gemini 3.5 Live Translate does voice-preserving speech-to-speech across 70+ languages; and Moonshot's Kimi K2.7 Code reached Ollama's US cloud. Sovereign, language-specific open models are also becoming a real category: a European consortium released Soofi S 30B-A3B (July 16), a hybrid Mamba-Transformer MoE (3.2B of 31.6B active) purpose-built for German and English rather than English-first with a multilingual afterthought.

On-device deployment took a step from laptop-class to phone-class: PrismML's Bonsai 27B (July 16) compresses Qwen3.6-27B into sub-2-bit builds — a ternary build at 94.6% of FP16 quality (5.9GB) and a 1-bit build at 89.5% quality (3.9GB) — small enough to run offline on an iPhone 17 Pro with 262K context and vision intact. The embodied / robotics thread grew into a full open stack: Ant Group's Robbyant shipped four open models in five days — LingBot-Vision (1B dense-perception), LingBot-VLA 2.0 (6B, 20 embodiments), the LingBot-World-Infinity world model, and the LingBot-VA 2.0 video-action policy — now tracked on the dedicated Embodied AI & robotics page. The proprietary tier also gained a price-aggressive newcomer: Meta's Muse Spark 1.1 (July 10), its first paid model rather than an open Llama drop, launched undercutting OpenAI and Anthropic on price while rivaling their models on some benchmarks (though not their strongest tiers) — Meta's clearest move yet toward a paid-API business (see Industry & business). Retrieval also entered the release tier via Liquid AI's LFM2.5 embedding/ColBERT pair, and the open coding tier gained DeepReinforce's self-scaffolding Ornith-1.0 family (training angle on the Training & RL page).

The through-line: 1M context, agent-tuned post-training, and open weights are table stakes; the live differentiators are throughput-per-dollar, on-device deployability down to phone-class, multimodal unification, sovereign/language-specific foundation models, and co-training with the tools models actually run inside.
