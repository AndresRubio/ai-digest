Infrastructure & compute

Last updated: 2026-10-26

Current state

The Oct 22 to 25 cycle was latency and residency rather than throughput. Liquid AI’s RIPL serves LFM2.5-1.2B at 34.6 ms p95 under 1,000 requests a second on one B200 against 498 ms for a smaller model on vLLM, a closed engine aimed at search ranking and fraud checks. Cerebras explained how it splits trillion-parameter models across WSE-3T wafers that each hold 44 GB on chip, and Cohere’s Model Vault now runs on confidential H100s on Azure so the operator cannot read the prompt.

Retrieval and compression both got cheaper per useful token. Rerank 5’s Context Focus returns passages instead of whole documents and cut a search agent’s tokens 35% in Cohere’s test, at a median 11.7 seconds per 96-document request on Pro. ISTA-DASLab’s 2-bit DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash keeps 100% of the base model’s average on three benchmarks, and the same lab’s quantization turns out to be the parent file of Saluki 27B, which MarkTechPost found re-tuned in 146 of 851 tensors.

The Oct 22 addition is the kernel layer under the formats. vLLM’s Humming is a general-purpose kernel library for quantized inference, one architecture across devices and quantization formats with execution parameters exposed for tuning, announced without a throughput figure in the summary; it goes in the quantization section as the thing the fewer-bits lever runs on, and the speedups are in the post rather than here.

The Oct 21 cycle was the PyTorch Conference giving the serving stack away. Google and Meta’s TorchTPU makes Cloud TPU a native PyTorch backend and replaces PyTorch/XLA, with the efficiency gap stated by the vendor: up to 52% MFU training Qwen 3 8B on Ironwood and a fine-tuning step cut from 11.49 s to 2.78 s, but only after tuning and torch.compile, since eager execution out of the box is slow. With TorchNeuron the same day, now carrying Deepgram’s 60% more audio seconds per dollar, PyTorch gained two non-NVIDIA native backends in one conference. Google Cloud’s llm-d 1.0 is the first stable release of a Kubernetes-native inference stack that pools vLLM or SGLang replicas and routes by cached prefix, with KV tiers from HBM to SSD and a disaggregated prefill-decode set, reported at 17,000 nodes and with no throughput number. And DeepSeek open-sourced DeepInfer, the engine behind its API, with the fleet figures attached, 155 trillion tokens a day per cluster and $38 per GPU-hour, and the cache, disaggregation and load-balancing layers that make the money kept back.

The Oct 20 cycle was three operators describing their own bills and plants. Cloudflare cut the inference cost of its automated code review from $2.43 to $0.77 a review, 68%, mostly by checking what reached the provider on a session’s first request and ordering messages so the cached prefix stayed stable across commits and repositories: the cache section’s argument that the hit is something to verify, done by a caller rather than asserted by the provider. Meta published the design of Prometheus, the gigawatt-scale supercluster under Muse, with closed-loop liquid cooling and a plan toward about one million GPUs, a target without a utilisation or cost figure. AWS’s TorchNeuron puts a native PyTorch backend on Trainium in public beta under Apache 2.0, the accelerator-portability bet still owing its efficiency gap, and vLLM’s push-based NIXL connector gained AWS’s own write-up, still with no number in the summary.

Earlier cycles

The Oct 19 addition is the shape of what NVIDIA gave away. Run:ai turns out to be one Apache-2.0 monorepo of about 50 Go services, control plane, cluster services, installer, CLI and an MCP server, with four pieces usable on their own: KAI Scheduler, now a CNCF Sandbox project, does gang scheduling, fair-share and topology awareness, and the GPU Fractioning Operator gives CUDA 13.4-native fractions with a hard memory cap per workload. The utilisation figure the Kubernetes section has been waiting for is still absent; what changed is that the queue is something an estate can read and fork rather than license.

The Oct 16 cycle was the desk and the failure path. Tom’s Hardware, cited by The Batch, put local machines on one agent benchmark: on AgentPerf-Local with a quantized Qwen 3.8-27B the NVIDIA-based Surface Laptop Ultra finished in 18.9 minutes against an M5 Max MacBook Pro’s 24.74 and AMD’s AI Ryzen Halo’s 42.1, though NVIDIA’s chips stop at 128 GB where an $18,299 Mac Studio reaches 256 GB — the quantization section’s trade of speed against residency, on named hardware. vLLM’s push-based NIXL connector takes KV-transfer setup off the critical path between prefill and decode workers, and Netflix described the detection and auto-remediation that keeps distributed training alive through hardware faults; neither summary carried a number, so both are designs to read rather than results to compare.

The Oct 15 cycle was mostly the rack and the box. NVIDIA contributed Vera Rubin rack designs to OCP with a 5,000A busbar and up to 250kW a rack, and AMD’s Helion blueprint packs up to 32,768 EPYC cores into a rack for agent workloads, partner systems due in November — one power figure and one bet that agent loops are CPU-bound, neither with a workload measurement. NVIDIA also open-sourced Run:ai, KAI Scheduler and GPU fractioning included, putting a vendor’s GPU queue into the Kubernetes section with no utilisation number of its own. At the desk end, AMD’s Ryzen AI Halo lets the GPU address 160GB of 192GB unified memory — more memory in the machine, with no tokens-per-second figure yet.

The Oct 14 entry is a chip measured in the unit this page cares about. Microsoft’s Maia 300 is a third-generation in-house inference accelerator claimed at 40% more tokens per megawatt than leading GPU systems, deploying from 2027 at multiple gigawatts — power rather than FLOPs as the denominator, which is the compute section’s point that the constraint has moved off the die. Microsoft does not plan to rent the chips directly, so for anyone outside Microsoft it is a cost curve behind its services rather than hardware to target.

The Oct 9 entry is two answers to running a 27B model on less. Conway Research’s Saluki 27B takes Qwen3.8-27B to 2 bits and 7.89 GB with a pass tuned for tool calling, beats its parent on its own 120-task tool benchmark and gives up 17 points on AIME — the quantization section’s point that compression is measured best where it was tuned, made by a single model card. LithosAI’s lithos-metal attacks the compute side with generated Metal megakernels and claims over 200 tokens a second for the same model on an M5 Max, a figure that arrives without batch size, precision or baseline.

The Oct 7 entry opens a section below scheduling. Meta open-sourced Rebalancer, the C++ assignment solver that has placed objects into bins under constraints in its production for over nine years, about 40 million problems a day: the formulation is separate from the backend, so one spec runs on local search or on Xpress, Gurobi or HiGHS, and the local-search path solves 265,000 objects into 3,200 bins at a p99 of 12 seconds where MIP gives up past a million objects. It is not a model and not Kubernetes; it is the layer that decides where the fleets in the sections above actually put their work, now installable with pip, with every figure Meta’s own.

The Oct 6 entry is a box rather than a benchmark. Ghost’s Core is a $3,499 screenless machine for running local models around the clock, with swappable Hugging Face models, harnesses such as OpenCode, and encryption the company says locks even itself out; the first batch sold out. What it does not publish is the figure this page judges local hardware on — memory size and tokens per second — so it cannot yet be placed against DGX Spark 64GB’s 273 GB/s or Splash Engine’s 74 tokens per second on a 48GB M5 Pro. Added by hand the same day, Google Research’s March TurboQuant attacks the same memory wall from the software side: a rotation, a polar-coordinate quantizer and one bit of residual correction take the KV cache to 3 bits with no retraining and, on Google’s own long-context tests, no accuracy loss — up to 8× faster attention logits on an H100, though against a JAX baseline rather than a serving kernel.

The Oct 2–5 cycle was serving and scheduling at fleet scale, with every figure the operator’s own. Prime Intellect opened Prime Inference after serving about a trillion tokens a day to itself; GLM-5.3 runs on GB200 NVL72, where splitting prefill from decode cut p90 inter-token latency 40% and NVFP4 lifted cached tokens per decoder from 1.09M to 1.63M. AI21 put a 10,000-GPU fleet behind Kueue and reports high-priority jobs starting 83% sooner, on a figure from the cloud it runs on. NVIDIA’s DGX Spark 64GB halves the desk box’s memory at 273 GB/s, aimed at 30–35B models and long-input work rather than at serving a chat endpoint.

Two entries sit upstream of serving. Ai2’s OLMo-core 3 is an open framework for training MoEs into the trillion-parameter range, and Vx puts topology and memory placement into a type system so placement bugs fail at compile time instead of deep into a run — neither with a throughput number yet. The cache thread gained the specification behind a claim already recorded: DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash stores 890 bytes of cache per token by having 4 of its 40 layers compute it and the rest borrow it, detailed on Model releases.

The Oct 2 cycle brought one reference and one specification. Modal’s GPU Glossary opens a new section for reference material: an interlinked, MIT-licensed guide from streaming multiprocessors and the warp scheduler up to nvcc flags, the vocabulary every serving and kernel entry here assumes. Separately, OpenAI’s Jalapeño, an older entry first reported in August with throughput-per-kilowatt figures on a single model, now has a published configuration — 216 GiB of HBM4 beside a compute die and an I/O chiplet — from a More Than Moore deep-dive that is now its headline link. The memory figure is the one this page’s cache and long-context entries can be read against; the power and benchmark detail is the analyst’s account, not an OpenAI disclosure.

The Oct 1 cycle was one retrieval engine rewritten for its tail. Perplexity’s Photon replaces a forked open-source engine with Rust and takes p99 for retrieval and ranking from about 800 ms to 65 ms on 20% fewer machines while storing 2.5× more per document; its Fast Search mode sells the result at $1 per 1,000 requests and matched the default mode on Perplexity’s own agent eval at about a third of the estimated cost. The trade is stated rather than buried — long-tail DCG falls from 2.45 to 2.21 — and, like CobbleDB before it, the engine is not open source, so every number is Perplexity’s.

The Sep 30 cycle was routing per turn, and inference with no server at all. Fireworks’ FireRouter chooses between Opus 5.5, GLM 5.3 and GLM 5.3 Flash on every turn and reports 98.1% of Opus-only accuracy for $6.63 a coding session against $15.36, calling itself cache-aware without saying how a mid-session switch is priced — the second per-turn router in a week after jev-router, and the first with a dollar figure for a whole session. At the other end, MicroLLM Lab runs seven 4-bit models of 26M to 360M parameters in a browser tab over WebGPU, a playground by its own description. The per-agent machines that boot in under 10ms are on Tools & DevEx.

The Sep 29 cycle was where the agent-serving bill actually lands. Modal’s Quail passes a billion tokens a minute on one H100, more than 10× its vLLM baseline at under $0.06 per billion tokens, a throughput figure that still needs its model and batch size beside it. An estimate of what serving Muse costs puts 100 million daily users at 1–2 gigawatts with only about 0.1 GW of it on GPUs and the rest in per-user sandboxes — and Anthropic’s $11.6 billion Akamai commitment, on Industry & business, buys CPU capacity for exactly that layer. SpaceXAI reaches 1.44 million GPUs at Colossus and is building a 1.2 GW plant to switch them on, the grid binding before the silicon. jev-router got its number, 82% more agent tasks solved than OpenRouter’s Auto Router, and one dissenting test.

The Sep 28 cycle is kernels and orbits. Inferact’s TPU v7 megakernels push Kimi K3 past 700 tokens per second with speculative decoding and claim 1.4–2× a GB200 at batch sizes 1 to 8, carrying the megakernel argument Cohere made on GPUs to a second accelerator, on the vendor’s own comparison. Google’s Suncatcher launches four TPUs on a kilowatt of solar on October 1 — the far end of this page’s claim that the binding constraint keeps moving off the die, now to power and heat in orbit. And jev-router on OpenRouter lets a decision model choose the model and effort before generation, a routing layer that acts on the cache economics below without yet publishing what it saves.

The Sep 23 cycle was three changes below the model. vLLM’s hardware-agnostic layers reach 96.6% of native efficiency on H100 while staying torch-compilable, and open a new Serving across accelerators section: portability is now priced at a few percent on the hardware the native kernels were written for. A new paper bounds MoE training memory — expert dispatch, vocabulary projection, checkpointing and optimizer state — by scheduling alone, without approximating the computation. And djev on Cloud Run serves a Jev-compatible decision model for about $3 an hour at 35–60ms per decision, the self-host alternative to calling the closed API.

The Sep 22 arrival was silicon, reported in exactly the way this page keeps warning about. Alibaba’s Zhenwu V900 accelerator is said to triple its predecessor’s performance and to drive 20GW of Alibaba data centres by 2032 — a comparison against its own last chip, with no memory or interconnect figure attached, which on this page’s running argument are the numbers that actually bind.

The Sep 21 cycle pointed the agents at the serving stack itself. Z.ai says a GLM-5.3-powered Infra Agent built the production serving infrastructure for GLM-5.3-Flash across more than 100,000 Chinese accelerators in under two weeks, working from dense feedback on kernel fixes and system-level optimisation, and tripled throughput in the process. Z.ai frames it as a step toward recursive self-improvement; the sober reading is that serving is the part of the stack with the tightest feedback loop and the most mechanical search space, which is what makes it the first place an agent could plausibly do this. The baseline it tripled is unpublished and every figure is the vendor’s, so what is solid here is the shape of the workload, not the multiple.

At the other end of the scale, local inference crossed a usability line rather than a capability one. Inco AI and LM Studio’s Splash Engine decodes Qwen3.8-27B at 74 tokens per second on a 48GB M5 Pro, with a stated floor of an M3, macOS 26.4+ and 36GB of unified memory — which is really a statement about which laptops are inference hardware now. Set beside PrismML’s Bonsai 2 27B, arriving the same week at 5.93GB against 53.80GB in FP16, the two answers to the same machine separate cleanly: compress the weights and pay for it in long-horizon agent work, or rewrite the runtime and keep full precision but need the memory. Both now exist for the same model family, which is the first time this section can put the choice that plainly.

The siting question moved too. Crusoe, which built OpenAI’s Abilene supercomputer, is now factory-manufacturing small data centres, loading them onto flatbed trucks and dropping them wherever power is available, on the reasoning that inference runs on far fewer chips than training and that construction schedules are the slower constraint. That is the training/inference split turning into a real-estate decision, and it points the same way as the latency work this section tracks: serving capacity wants to sit near the power and the users, not near the training cluster. No unit economics have been published for the small builds, so the comparison to Abilene remains the company’s own.

An earlier cycle was Kubernetes quietly winning the layer under agents. Google put Agent Substrate on GKE — an open-source, secure-by-default agent execution runtime holding millions of sandboxes at roughly ten times standard container density, with sub-500ms resume, over 500 suspend/resume activations per second and zero-trust kernel and network isolation as a native property. Microsoft open-sourced TauGrid the same week, an MIT-licensed Helm install bundling the tau CLI, Kueue, KubeRay, GPU health monitoring and observability, with telemetry off by default. Neither claims novelty in the components; both put the agent sandbox and the GPU queue inside the cluster manager the rest of the estate already runs. Density and resume latency are the numbers to hold onto, because they decide whether an idle agent session can be parked rather than kept warm — the difference between paying for concurrency and paying for occupancy, and the cheapest available answer to a $19,000 swarm refactor.

Two measurements land beside them, both notable for publishing the unflattering half. Meta's ZGateway fronts ZippyDB with a stateless proxy tier at over a billion operations per second, carrying about 40% of traffic at roughly 6% overhead — not deployable outside Meta, so the artifact is the 6% rather than the code, and it is the figure this pattern usually leaves unstated. Nunchux AI's VC-Attention is a training-free low-bit attention kernel for video diffusion transformers: Wan2.2 attention runs 1.59× faster on a B200 at 8-bit and 3.58× on an RTX 5090 at 4-bit, but end to end those become 1.19× and 1.70×. Quoting both numbers is the honest move and a rare one; a kernel speedup reported alone routinely implies several times the gain a user would actually see. No kernel has been released publicly yet, so the figures remain the authors' own.

Today the retrieval layer gets its cost model rewritten twice, once by a vendor and once by a customer who stopped being one. Pinecone now meters reads on egress as well as on queries, with monthly allowances of 1 GB on Starter, 10 GB on Builder and 100 GB on Standard and Enterprise; past the allowance the upper tiers keep serving and bill at the published rate, while Starter and Builder block reads that return record data until the next period — an overage that becomes an outage. What it prices is not how many queries you run but how much record data each one hands back, which makes the ordinary fetch-then-rerank pipeline expensive by design and turns returning identifiers and hydrating from cheaper storage into a cost decision. Perplexity answered the same arithmetic by building: CobbleDB, its own key-value store for serving prepared web content, took median batch reads from 31.4ms to 5.60ms and p99 from 123ms to 24.2ms against DynamoDB at an estimated 20%+ lower cost, with open-sourcing planned. Batch reads decide search latency because a query fans out and the p99 sets the answer, so the five-fold tail cut is the bigger result. Both are the same judgement from opposite sides of the invoice — a managed service is right until the access pattern is narrow and enormous, at which point its generality is the thing being paid for.

Pinecone also documented the schema change under the full-text search it shipped last week: a Documents API on a schema-based index where text fields, dense vectors and sparse vectors are declared on one document rather than flattened into a single field or parked in unrankable metadata. Hybrid retrieval was always assembled rather than built, and the missing piece was never BM25 or cosine similarity but a record shape holding both as first-class. PlanetScale reached the same conclusion from the opposite side the same day with TIN, a GA Postgres full-text extension reporting 25× ParadeDB’s QPS on mixed ranked queries and 541× GIN on conjunction and phrase queries by using ctid values as document identifiers so postings operations vectorize — for anyone on pgvector that removes the managed service rather than improving it, at 50.7 GB of index and on PlanetScale’s own benchmarks. Back at Pinecone, three ceilings moved alongside it (1,000,000 namespaces per serverless index on Enterprise, pod-to-serverless migration doubled to 50 million records, Marketplace retired), each removing a workaround rather than adding a capability. The crawler bill this page quantified at git.kernel.org got its first two answers the same day, and they are a price and a permission. A developer charged agents a penny a page over the x402 protocol and watched an agent pay it — no real revenue yet, but the flow works, and charging is the only response available to publishers with no licensing leverage. Cloudflare shipped the other half, a setting that keeps a site indexed for search while refusing training use, splitting a permission that had been one lever even though the two uses have opposite value to a publisher. Both are voluntary against a cost that is not, which is robots.txt’s unresolved problem restated. And the hardware assumption under every local-inference result here weakened: RTX 5090 supply has emptied from US online retail, with third-party listings at $6,400 to $9,500 against roughly $4,299 in store, because its 32 GB of GDDR7 made it local AI infrastructure and the market is pricing the memory.

The prior cycle produced the first published routing result to put a number on the alternative to compression. Spotify's Portal (Sep 14) cut routine Claude Code token spend by roughly 90% after finding the agent was burning most of its tokens reading files rather than reasoning: a cheap-model reader ingests large files and returns a summary so raw files never reach the frontier model's context, a writer generates tests and config from a spec plus an example straight to disk, and a plugin intercepts large reads and routes them automatically. What makes it usable is that the failures are stated. In one benchmark the cheaper model missed a subtle thread-safety bug the frontier model caught instantly, so debugging and safety-critical logic stay on the flagship; summaries lose exact line numbers, so direct edits do too; and routing small files costs more overhead than it saves. Gartner's projection that per-developer token spend passes the average developer salary by 2028 is the pressure behind it. Routing and compression fail differently — compression rewrites the prefix and breaks the cache, routing leaves the prefix alone but introduces a judgement boundary, and the benchmark miss is what being wrong about that boundary looks like.

An objection landed the same week that applies to every cache figure on this page. “A cache hit is not proof that you skipped the work” (Sep 12) points out that the hit is asserted by the system whose bill it reduces, and lists what would make it evidence: an independent oracle expecting the prefix, an engine attesting to it, a prompt path demonstrably skipping it, byte-identical output, a passing evaluator, and a verifier binding all of it to a specific public bundle. Nothing meets that bar today, so the residue is a caution rather than a method — cache-hit rate is vendor-reported, unaudited, and the metric with the most definitional latitude. The same problem reappears one layer out: Mohamed Moustafa's catalogue of OpenRouter routing (Sep 11) finds one model ID served by providers running different inference software and settings, some providers of a vision model lacking vision entirely, and reasoning-effort read inconsistently between them. provider.only pins the deployment and /endpoints lists the choices. A model ID names a family of deployments, and any reproducible measurement has to say which one.

On hardware, Positron AI (Sep 14) raised a round led by SemiAnalysis Capital for a chip built around memory rather than FLOPs: 288 GB to 2.3 TB per Asimov chip, commodity LPDDR5X on an organic substrate, air-cooled. Each of those choices is a bet against the constraints this page has been tracking — serving is memory-bound, and what makes frontier inference hardware scarce and site-dependent is HBM supply plus liquid cooling. Commodity memory on air cooling claims a large share of inference needs neither, and can go where a liquid-cooled rack cannot. Bandwidth per dollar is the number that would settle it and was not given. Meanwhile a cost nobody budgets for got quantified: git.kernel.org has about 14 CPU cores across five nodes doing nothing but rendering Git commits as HTML for crawlers — more than all legitimate access including clones. That is the training-data pipeline's infrastructure bill arriving on someone else's invoice, and the exposure scales with how useful your content is to index.

The cycle's two additions attack the same bill from opposite ends of the stack: one makes a cost visible, the other removes it. Pi (Sep 11) now detects prompt-cache misses — triggered by switching models, editing an earlier message, or simply letting a session sit idle — tells the developer one has happened, and estimates what rereading that context cost. Given that cached input runs a quarter to a tenth of fresh, a prefix that quietly stops being cached multiplies the rest of a session's spend while changing nothing the user can observe, which is exactly the class of leak this section keeps describing and nobody could previously instrument. At the other end, Edge0 serves a 35B sparse MoE on Apple Silicon by treating the SSD as the weight store and streaming experts in on demand: 23 GB on disk, roughly 2.9 GB of live memory, 14.9–17.7 tokens per second on an M4 Pro Mac mini. It works because sparsity means only a fraction of the weights are touched per token, so it does not transfer to dense models, and SSD bandwidth rather than compute sets the ceiling — but it moves the on-device constraint off RAM capacity, which is the one that has been binding.

Before them, three items said the same thing about where an agent's bill actually comes from, and none of them is the per-token rate on the model card. “The cache is the price” (Sep 8) puts numbers on it: GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 advertise broadly similar rates, and Fable's cache reads cost about a quarter of Astra's — noise on a chat completion, several-fold on an agent replaying a large stable prefix every step. Google's Headroom cookbook (Sep 10) attacks the same total from the other side, compressing tool output before it enters the prompt for 76% fewer prompt tokens at no measured accuracy loss across Gemini 3.8 Flash, Google ADK and OpenCode — targeting tool results, the bulkiest and least information-dense part of a long run, rather than the conversation history whose loss users notice. The two pull against each other in a way worth holding onto: compression rewrites the prefix, and a rewritten prefix is a cache miss. Pinecone's full-text search GA (Sep 10) is the same economy applied to retrieval, folding BM25 ranking, Lucene syntax and stemming in 18 languages into the vector index and removing the second engine most RAG stacks were running beside it — a synchronisation problem and a duplicate latency budget deleted, which for most teams outweighs any per-query gain on either half.

All three are the fine-grained version of the invoices August produced. A 5,000-developer engineering team watched its AI bill rise fivefold in a month once autonomous coding agents began burning usage-based credits in background debug loops — a failure mode with no equivalent in the request/response era, because nothing previously retried on its own initiative. The structural consequence is that under usage-based pricing every context load, tool call and retry carries direct cost, so model choice and context size stop being tuning details and become infrastructure decisions. The examined alternatives each fail differently: self-hosting cuts per-query cost for predictable high-volume work but GPU capacity, cluster operations and specialist engineering time erase much of the saving, while local inference avoids API charges and buys memory limits, prefill latency, maintenance and the loss of centralised telemetry instead. The conclusion is workload routing rather than a winner — the logic NeMo Switchyard applies inside one agent run, applied to the estate. The second invoice is one unauthenticated request that consumed 1,302 tokens of GPU time on a model server with no record of who sent it. Putting an AI-aware gateway (agentgateway) in front of Ollama restores authentication, stable model names, token accounting and a consistent endpoint without touching the models, and token limits stop a single agent loop from consuming shared capacity — though the standalone setup shares one budget rather than metering per key, and under health-based routing the first failed request still reaches the client before the unhealthy target is evicted. The companion piece on governed MCP platforms adds the sharper lesson: authorization has to reach the tool-call boundary rather than stopping at server access, and runtime testing caught misplaced policies that static configuration review had passed. Pricing moved in the same direction from the vendor side, with Gemini 3.7 Flash arriving three weeks after 3.6 at a 50% introductory cut — $0.75 per million input tokens and $3.75 output through the end of the year.

The most instructive infrastructure result of the previous cycle was also an invoice. Between February and August, agent requests at Uber rose 9.4× and users 7×, while AI spend stayed flat after April and cost per session for one model fell 52% from its June peak. The method generalizes: decompose spend into adoption, workload and token price; accept that only workload is yours; then route every job through one harness that picks the model balancing cost against quality, hand simpler subtasks to cheaper subagents (their single biggest saver), and send less context — hour-long caches matched to how engineers actually work, and a “code-mode” bundling many tool calls into one script that cut tokens per query by more than half. Ramp's Router productizes the first of those, matching each request to the lowest-cost model that clears its performance bar while reacting to live latency and failure rates, and claims a 40% average saving.

Two vendor-independent numbers back the same conclusion from the kernel and the card. Cohere's decode megakernel for North Mini Code reaches 292 tokens per second at batch size 1 — 62% of speed-of-light, 1.58× vLLM — and holds that margin across batch sizes and out to 256K context with no measurable accuracy loss, while supporting continuous batching, paged attention and ragged sequences behind an OpenAI-compatible endpoint; the achievement is surviving the features a real server needs, which is where earlier fusion results stopped. FreeToken attacks the other end, continuously remapping experts, model state and CPU/GPU work to whatever bandwidth a personal machine has, with reported support for 20+ MoE models from 35B on an 8 GB laptop GPU up to a 753B GLM on one workstation GPU; corroborating figures put GLM-5.2 753B at 14.9 tok/s on 96 GB, DeepSeek-V4-Flash 284B at 22 tok/s on 32 GB and Qwen3.6-35B at 39.3 tok/s on 8 GB.

Against all of that efficiency, the price of the hardware is going the other way. Nvidia told major customers that Vera Rubin and Grace Blackwell servers shipping early next year will cost more than 15% more, driven by memory rather than logic — treating HBM as a small share of the accelerator price protects margin now and becomes untenable in FY28 as memory content per part rises. Nvidia also cut its financial backstop for OpenAI's Ohio campus from a planned $250 billion to under $120 billion for a first phase of roughly five gigawatts, moving financing risk back to OpenAI. And Cerebras says its CS-4 is several times faster than the CS-3, sampling now and widely available in Q3 — an unmeasured claim that matters because Cerebras now runs every competitive speed offering OpenAI sells.

Two supply questions now sit on either side of the same trade. Google began selling TPUv7 Ironwood outright as well as renting it, claiming up to 50% better performance per dollar than NVIDIA's B200/B300 — the first generation Google competes for inference workloads it does not run itself, with the real product being whether decades of internal TPU tooling can be externalized (the Accelerator Agents release is the first instalment). NVIDIA's answer at the latency end is Groq 3 LPX, now in full production as racks extending Vera Rubin, claiming a 4× response-time improvement over the nearest platform and agentic tasks finishing in minutes rather than hours — a vendor number, but a separate accelerator line tuned for latency rather than throughput is a real structural bet. Underneath both, a forecast puts 2027 AI compute production ahead of data-centre capacity that can actually be energized, leaving roughly 15GW of IT load delayed, concentrated in North America, and bottlenecked on interconnections, transformers, cooling, permitting and turbines rather than on chips. That is a lead time measured in years, and it is the number to hold next to Anthropic's 14.8GW of signed leases. Prices moved in the meantime, in both directions: OpenAI cut GPT-5.6 Luna by 80% to $0.20/$1.20 per million tokens and Terra by 20% to $2/$12, which changes routing arithmetic for agent loops more than a capability bump at the top would; and Cheaper Inference began reselling unused provider capacity through one OpenAI-compatible API at 30–60% off list — Astra at 30%, GLM-5.3 and DeepSeek V4 Flash at 45%, GPT-5.6 Luna and GLM-5.3 Flash at 60% — the first thing resembling a spot market for inference rather than a rate card.

Behind all of it, the month's most instructive systems artifact still serves no traffic at all. kimi-k3-in-c runs Kimi K3's full 2.78 trillion parameters on a single CPU from 176 KB of portable C99 — no BLAS, no framework, no GPU — against a 1.56 TB on-disk checkpoint, at 26.5 seconds per token with 8 GB of RAM, 19.8 at 64 GB, and 5.6 once 128 GB holds the working set. Speed arrived as a first-class purchasing axis in late August, alongside cost and accuracy. Inference economics otherwise remain the headline story in infrastructure. With 1M-context models becoming standard, KV cache size has gone from "a concern" to "the dominant cost driver" for long-running agents. The newest hard number on the architecture-level lever is MiniMax Sparse Attention (MSA), now detailed with specifics: a 28.4× reduction in per-token attention compute at 1M context versus dense GQA with on-par quality, translating to 14.2× faster prefill and 7.6× faster decode on an H800 — and, because MSA is GQA-native, droppable into existing serving stacks without a redesign. Since long-context inference is currently the biggest cost driver in production LLM serving, a near-30× compute cut at 1M context with no quality loss is the kind of result that tends to land in most long-context serving stacks within a year. It composes with the serving-layer levers already in play: Together AI's 8× KV cache reduction and EAGLE 3.1 speculative-decoding improvements target cost-per-token at long contexts from a different layer of the stack, joined by NVIDIA's DFlash speculative decoding (up to 15× throughput on Blackwell) and a further, lightly-detailed ~2× serving-throughput gain NVIDIA flagged in the July 10 weekly brief (no published specifics). The flat-memory idea is also reaching document AI: Baidu's Unlimited OCR keeps attention-cache memory constant regardless of document length via Recurrent Sliding Window Attention (R-SWA), attacking the KV-cache explosion that forces long-document OCR pipelines to chunk or run out of memory. The throughput-per-dollar story for very large models keeps sharpening: Xiaomi's MiMo + TileRT runtime pushed a 1.02-trillion-parameter MoE past 1,000 tokens/second on commodity GPUs (42B active, FP8), NVIDIA's Nemotron 3 Ultra used hybrid Mamba-Transformer layers plus NVFP4 quantization for up to 5× throughput, and the same sparse-activation logic now scales down to Cohere's 3B-active North Mini Code — evidence that runtime engineering, sparsity, and low-precision formats are decoupling serving cost from parameter count.

A newer lever attacks the decode step itself: Google's experimental DiffusionGemma generates text in parallel 256-token blocks (bidirectional attention + iterative self-correction) at 1,000+ tok/s on an H100 and 700+ on an RTX 5090, shifting the bottleneck from memory bandwidth to compute and pointing at a future where autoregressive decode is optional for latency-sensitive work — if quality closes.

A parallel front is running capable models locally: Gemma 4's quantization-aware-trained (QAT) weights and a 12B model tuned for a 16GB laptop, joined by streaming ASR (Nemotron 3.5 ASR) running fully local via ONNX, PrismML's sub-2-bit Bonsai 27B running on an iPhone (see Model releases), and now the browser itself as a deployment target: Google's LiteRT.js (July 16) is a JavaScript binding that runs .tflite models directly inside Chrome/Edge 113+ and Safari 17.4+, accelerated by WebGPU, with inference staying entirely client-side — zero server cost, zero inference backend, and (per Google) improved privacy and latency. It collapses the deployment gap for web ML: no Python backend, no inference server, no per-call cost, meaning any web developer can now ship a real model as a static asset, widening who can build ML features well beyond teams that can stand up serving infrastructure.

At the data-center end, NVIDIA's GTC Taipei framing of "the age of the AI factory" (Vera CPU for agents, Vera Rubin in full production, RTX Spark / DGX Station for the desk) continues to define the hardware narrative.

A newer cost lever sits one step before serving — the data pipeline that builds the indexes agents retrieve from: Flash-KMeans, an IO-aware exact K-Means using Triton GPU kernels, runs ~200× faster than FAISS and 30× faster than cuML (17.9× end-to-end on an H200) by redesigning clustering around GPU memory bottlenecks the way FlashAttention redesigned attention, directly cutting the cost and time of building and refreshing vector indexes for RAG at scale. The data-store layer beneath agents is getting the same treatment: Netflix cut wide-partition read latency on its Cassandra-backed TimeSeries store from seconds to low double-digit milliseconds using an asynchronous, metadata-driven engine that dynamically detects and splits oversized partitions per ID without a full migration — a downtime-free pattern directly reusable by the vector and metadata stores behind production AI. Self-hosted sandboxes and private-network MCP tunnels (Anthropic) round out a picture focused on the production economics of serving — and increasingly self-hosting — agentic workloads.

On the hosted-serving side, capacity is now a moving target too: Ollama reports its cloud serving of GLM-5.2 has been accelerated by additional NVIDIA Blackwell capacity in the US and Europe, a reminder that for managed-inference providers, geographic hardware supply is as much a lever on throughput and latency as the runtime optimizations above. The self-hosting-vs-API economics now have a concrete worked example from healthcare: a published cost model shows that for high-volume, full-document workloads, per-token API pricing (linear in data volume) diverges sharply from per-server local deployment (sublinear in volume), reaching ~18–29× cost differences at population scale — a reminder that beyond a certain throughput the deployment model, not the serving-layer optimization, dominates total cost.
