Infrastructure & compute

Last updated: 2026-09-18

Current state

Today's cycle is Kubernetes quietly winning the layer under agents. Google put Agent Substrate on GKE — an open-source, secure-by-default agent execution runtime holding millions of sandboxes at roughly ten times standard container density, with sub-500ms resume, over 500 suspend/resume activations per second and zero-trust kernel and network isolation as a native property. Microsoft open-sourced TauGrid the same week, an MIT-licensed Helm install bundling the tau CLI, Kueue, KubeRay, GPU health monitoring and observability, with telemetry off by default. Neither claims novelty in the components; both put the agent sandbox and the GPU queue inside the cluster manager the rest of the estate already runs. Density and resume latency are the numbers to hold onto, because they decide whether an idle agent session can be parked rather than kept warm — the difference between paying for concurrency and paying for occupancy, and the cheapest available answer to a $19,000 swarm refactor.

Two measurements land beside them, both notable for publishing the unflattering half. Meta's ZGateway fronts ZippyDB with a stateless proxy tier at over a billion operations per second, carrying about 40% of traffic at roughly 6% overhead — not deployable outside Meta, so the artifact is the 6% rather than the code, and it is the figure this pattern usually leaves unstated. Nunchux AI's VC-Attention is a training-free low-bit attention kernel for video diffusion transformers: Wan2.2 attention runs 1.59× faster on a B200 at 8-bit and 3.58× on an RTX 5090 at 4-bit, but end to end those become 1.19× and 1.70×. Quoting both numbers is the honest move and a rare one; a kernel speedup reported alone routinely implies several times the gain a user would actually see. No kernel has been released publicly yet, so the figures remain the authors' own.

Today the retrieval layer gets its cost model rewritten twice, once by a vendor and once by a customer who stopped being one. Pinecone now meters reads on egress as well as on queries, with monthly allowances of 1 GB on Starter, 10 GB on Builder and 100 GB on Standard and Enterprise; past the allowance the upper tiers keep serving and bill at the published rate, while Starter and Builder block reads that return record data until the next period — an overage that becomes an outage. What it prices is not how many queries you run but how much record data each one hands back, which makes the ordinary fetch-then-rerank pipeline expensive by design and turns returning identifiers and hydrating from cheaper storage into a cost decision. Perplexity answered the same arithmetic by building: CobbleDB, its own key-value store for serving prepared web content, took median batch reads from 31.4ms to 5.60ms and p99 from 123ms to 24.2ms against DynamoDB at an estimated 20%+ lower cost, with open-sourcing planned. Batch reads decide search latency because a query fans out and the p99 sets the answer, so the five-fold tail cut is the bigger result. Both are the same judgement from opposite sides of the invoice — a managed service is right until the access pattern is narrow and enormous, at which point its generality is the thing being paid for.

Earlier cycles

Pinecone also documented the schema change under the full-text search it shipped last week: a Documents API on a schema-based index where text fields, dense vectors and sparse vectors are declared on one document rather than flattened into a single field or parked in unrankable metadata. Hybrid retrieval was always assembled rather than built, and the missing piece was never BM25 or cosine similarity but a record shape holding both as first-class. PlanetScale reached the same conclusion from the opposite side the same day with TIN, a GA Postgres full-text extension reporting 25× ParadeDB’s QPS on mixed ranked queries and 541× GIN on conjunction and phrase queries by using ctid values as document identifiers so postings operations vectorize — for anyone on pgvector that removes the managed service rather than improving it, at 50.7 GB of index and on PlanetScale’s own benchmarks. Back at Pinecone, three ceilings moved alongside it (1,000,000 namespaces per serverless index on Enterprise, pod-to-serverless migration doubled to 50 million records, Marketplace retired), each removing a workaround rather than adding a capability. The crawler bill this page quantified at git.kernel.org got its first two answers the same day, and they are a price and a permission. A developer charged agents a penny a page over the x402 protocol and watched an agent pay it — no real revenue yet, but the flow works, and charging is the only response available to publishers with no licensing leverage. Cloudflare shipped the other half, a setting that keeps a site indexed for search while refusing training use, splitting a permission that had been one lever even though the two uses have opposite value to a publisher. Both are voluntary against a cost that is not, which is robots.txt’s unresolved problem restated. And the hardware assumption under every local-inference result here weakened: RTX 5090 supply has emptied from US online retail, with third-party listings at $6,400 to $9,500 against roughly $4,299 in store, because its 32 GB of GDDR7 made it local AI infrastructure and the market is pricing the memory.

The prior cycle produced the first published routing result to put a number on the alternative to compression. Spotify's Portal (Sep 14) cut routine Claude Code token spend by roughly 90% after finding the agent was burning most of its tokens reading files rather than reasoning: a cheap-model reader ingests large files and returns a summary so raw files never reach the frontier model's context, a writer generates tests and config from a spec plus an example straight to disk, and a plugin intercepts large reads and routes them automatically. What makes it usable is that the failures are stated. In one benchmark the cheaper model missed a subtle thread-safety bug the frontier model caught instantly, so debugging and safety-critical logic stay on the flagship; summaries lose exact line numbers, so direct edits do too; and routing small files costs more overhead than it saves. Gartner's projection that per-developer token spend passes the average developer salary by 2028 is the pressure behind it. Routing and compression fail differently — compression rewrites the prefix and breaks the cache, routing leaves the prefix alone but introduces a judgement boundary, and the benchmark miss is what being wrong about that boundary looks like.

An objection landed the same week that applies to every cache figure on this page. “A cache hit is not proof that you skipped the work” (Sep 12) points out that the hit is asserted by the system whose bill it reduces, and lists what would make it evidence: an independent oracle expecting the prefix, an engine attesting to it, a prompt path demonstrably skipping it, byte-identical output, a passing evaluator, and a verifier binding all of it to a specific public bundle. Nothing meets that bar today, so the residue is a caution rather than a method — cache-hit rate is vendor-reported, unaudited, and the metric with the most definitional latitude. The same problem reappears one layer out: Mohamed Moustafa's catalogue of OpenRouter routing (Sep 11) finds one model ID served by providers running different inference software and settings, some providers of a vision model lacking vision entirely, and reasoning-effort read inconsistently between them. provider.only pins the deployment and /endpoints lists the choices. A model ID names a family of deployments, and any reproducible measurement has to say which one.

On hardware, Positron AI (Sep 14) raised a round led by SemiAnalysis Capital for a chip built around memory rather than FLOPs: 288 GB to 2.3 TB per Asimov chip, commodity LPDDR5X on an organic substrate, air-cooled. Each of those choices is a bet against the constraints this page has been tracking — serving is memory-bound, and what makes frontier inference hardware scarce and site-dependent is HBM supply plus liquid cooling. Commodity memory on air cooling claims a large share of inference needs neither, and can go where a liquid-cooled rack cannot. Bandwidth per dollar is the number that would settle it and was not given. Meanwhile a cost nobody budgets for got quantified: git.kernel.org has about 14 CPU cores across five nodes doing nothing but rendering Git commits as HTML for crawlers — more than all legitimate access including clones. That is the training-data pipeline's infrastructure bill arriving on someone else's invoice, and the exposure scales with how useful your content is to index.

The cycle's two additions attack the same bill from opposite ends of the stack: one makes a cost visible, the other removes it. Pi (Sep 11) now detects prompt-cache misses — triggered by switching models, editing an earlier message, or simply letting a session sit idle — tells the developer one has happened, and estimates what rereading that context cost. Given that cached input runs a quarter to a tenth of fresh, a prefix that quietly stops being cached multiplies the rest of a session's spend while changing nothing the user can observe, which is exactly the class of leak this section keeps describing and nobody could previously instrument. At the other end, Edge0 serves a 35B sparse MoE on Apple Silicon by treating the SSD as the weight store and streaming experts in on demand: 23 GB on disk, roughly 2.9 GB of live memory, 14.9–17.7 tokens per second on an M4 Pro Mac mini. It works because sparsity means only a fraction of the weights are touched per token, so it does not transfer to dense models, and SSD bandwidth rather than compute sets the ceiling — but it moves the on-device constraint off RAM capacity, which is the one that has been binding.

Before them, three items said the same thing about where an agent's bill actually comes from, and none of them is the per-token rate on the model card. “The cache is the price” (Sep 8) puts numbers on it: GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 advertise broadly similar rates, and Fable's cache reads cost about a quarter of Astra's — noise on a chat completion, several-fold on an agent replaying a large stable prefix every step. Google's Headroom cookbook (Sep 10) attacks the same total from the other side, compressing tool output before it enters the prompt for 76% fewer prompt tokens at no measured accuracy loss across Gemini 3.8 Flash, Google ADK and OpenCode — targeting tool results, the bulkiest and least information-dense part of a long run, rather than the conversation history whose loss users notice. The two pull against each other in a way worth holding onto: compression rewrites the prefix, and a rewritten prefix is a cache miss. Pinecone's full-text search GA (Sep 10) is the same economy applied to retrieval, folding BM25 ranking, Lucene syntax and stemming in 18 languages into the vector index and removing the second engine most RAG stacks were running beside it — a synchronisation problem and a duplicate latency budget deleted, which for most teams outweighs any per-query gain on either half.

All three are the fine-grained version of the invoices August produced. A 5,000-developer engineering team watched its AI bill rise fivefold in a month once autonomous coding agents began burning usage-based credits in background debug loops — a failure mode with no equivalent in the request/response era, because nothing previously retried on its own initiative. The structural consequence is that under usage-based pricing every context load, tool call and retry carries direct cost, so model choice and context size stop being tuning details and become infrastructure decisions. The examined alternatives each fail differently: self-hosting cuts per-query cost for predictable high-volume work but GPU capacity, cluster operations and specialist engineering time erase much of the saving, while local inference avoids API charges and buys memory limits, prefill latency, maintenance and the loss of centralised telemetry instead. The conclusion is workload routing rather than a winner — the logic NeMo Switchyard applies inside one agent run, applied to the estate. The second invoice is one unauthenticated request that consumed 1,302 tokens of GPU time on a model server with no record of who sent it. Putting an AI-aware gateway (agentgateway) in front of Ollama restores authentication, stable model names, token accounting and a consistent endpoint without touching the models, and token limits stop a single agent loop from consuming shared capacity — though the standalone setup shares one budget rather than metering per key, and under health-based routing the first failed request still reaches the client before the unhealthy target is evicted. The companion piece on governed MCP platforms adds the sharper lesson: authorization has to reach the tool-call boundary rather than stopping at server access, and runtime testing caught misplaced policies that static configuration review had passed. Pricing moved in the same direction from the vendor side, with Gemini 3.7 Flash arriving three weeks after 3.6 at a 50% introductory cut — $0.75 per million input tokens and $3.75 output through the end of the year.

The most instructive infrastructure result of the previous cycle was also an invoice. Between February and August, agent requests at Uber rose 9.4× and users 7×, while AI spend stayed flat after April and cost per session for one model fell 52% from its June peak. The method generalizes: decompose spend into adoption, workload and token price; accept that only workload is yours; then route every job through one harness that picks the model balancing cost against quality, hand simpler subtasks to cheaper subagents (their single biggest saver), and send less context — hour-long caches matched to how engineers actually work, and a “code-mode” bundling many tool calls into one script that cut tokens per query by more than half. Ramp's Router productizes the first of those, matching each request to the lowest-cost model that clears its performance bar while reacting to live latency and failure rates, and claims a 40% average saving.

Two vendor-independent numbers back the same conclusion from the kernel and the card. Cohere's decode megakernel for North Mini Code reaches 292 tokens per second at batch size 1 — 62% of speed-of-light, 1.58× vLLM — and holds that margin across batch sizes and out to 256K context with no measurable accuracy loss, while supporting continuous batching, paged attention and ragged sequences behind an OpenAI-compatible endpoint; the achievement is surviving the features a real server needs, which is where earlier fusion results stopped. FreeToken attacks the other end, continuously remapping experts, model state and CPU/GPU work to whatever bandwidth a personal machine has, with reported support for 20+ MoE models from 35B on an 8 GB laptop GPU up to a 753B GLM on one workstation GPU; corroborating figures put GLM-5.2 753B at 14.9 tok/s on 96 GB, DeepSeek-V4-Flash 284B at 22 tok/s on 32 GB and Qwen3.6-35B at 39.3 tok/s on 8 GB.

Against all of that efficiency, the price of the hardware is going the other way. Nvidia told major customers that Vera Rubin and Grace Blackwell servers shipping early next year will cost more than 15% more, driven by memory rather than logic — treating HBM as a small share of the accelerator price protects margin now and becomes untenable in FY28 as memory content per part rises. Nvidia also cut its financial backstop for OpenAI's Ohio campus from a planned $250 billion to under $120 billion for a first phase of roughly five gigawatts, moving financing risk back to OpenAI. And Cerebras says its CS-4 is several times faster than the CS-3, sampling now and widely available in Q3 — an unmeasured claim that matters because Cerebras now runs every competitive speed offering OpenAI sells.

Two supply questions now sit on either side of the same trade. Google began selling TPUv7 Ironwood outright as well as renting it, claiming up to 50% better performance per dollar than NVIDIA's B200/B300 — the first generation Google competes for inference workloads it does not run itself, with the real product being whether decades of internal TPU tooling can be externalized (the Accelerator Agents release is the first instalment). NVIDIA's answer at the latency end is Groq 3 LPX, now in full production as racks extending Vera Rubin, claiming a 4× response-time improvement over the nearest platform and agentic tasks finishing in minutes rather than hours — a vendor number, but a separate accelerator line tuned for latency rather than throughput is a real structural bet. Underneath both, a forecast puts 2027 AI compute production ahead of data-centre capacity that can actually be energized, leaving roughly 15GW of IT load delayed, concentrated in North America, and bottlenecked on interconnections, transformers, cooling, permitting and turbines rather than on chips. That is a lead time measured in years, and it is the number to hold next to Anthropic's 14.8GW of signed leases. Prices moved in the meantime, in both directions: OpenAI cut GPT-5.6 Luna by 80% to $0.20/$1.20 per million tokens and Terra by 20% to $2/$12, which changes routing arithmetic for agent loops more than a capability bump at the top would; and Cheaper Inference began reselling unused provider capacity through one OpenAI-compatible API at 30–60% off list — Astra at 30%, GLM-5.3 and DeepSeek V4 Flash at 45%, GPT-5.6 Luna and GLM-5.3 Flash at 60% — the first thing resembling a spot market for inference rather than a rate card.

Behind all of it, the month's most instructive systems artifact still serves no traffic at all. kimi-k3-in-c runs Kimi K3's full 2.78 trillion parameters on a single CPU from 176 KB of portable C99 — no BLAS, no framework, no GPU — against a 1.56 TB on-disk checkpoint, at 26.5 seconds per token with 8 GB of RAM, 19.8 at 64 GB, and 5.6 once 128 GB holds the working set. That curve is the value: it restates "you need a cluster" as a resident-set question with a measurable latency price, which is the same memory-locality argument the Cerebras and Jalapeño results below make from the other end of the budget.

Speed arrived as a first-class purchasing axis in late August, alongside cost and accuracy. OpenAI and Cerebras previewed Ultrafast, serving GPT-5.6 Sol on Cerebras hardware at up to 750 output tokens per second against 65 on OpenAI's own API — about 11× — and 83 seconds per GDPval task against 7.7 minutes. The headline demonstration is a throughput result, not an intelligence one: all 2,500 Humanity's Last Exam questions answered in 11 hours 11 minutes against 78 hours 27 minutes for Claude Fable 5. The mechanism belongs on this page because it is a memory argument rather than a compute one — Cerebras holds the weights in 44GB of on-chip SRAM, eliminating the external-memory round trips that bottleneck GPU inference, which is the same bandwidth constraint the KV-cache and quantization work below attacks from the software side. Gemini 3.7 Flash (330 tok/s) and Nemotron 3.5 Lightning (302 tok/s, plus the NeMo Switchyard router that cut agentic task cost to a third of Opus 4.8 by dispatching each step to the cheapest adequate model) shipped the same week on the same pitch, and OpenAI published first results for Jalapeño, its Broadcom-built inference chip. The caveat worth carrying forward: latency and throughput are distinct goods that these announcements conflate — GPT-5.6 Sol takes 97.2 seconds to its first token at max reasoning against 14.5 for GPT-5.5, and no latency figure for Ultrafast has been published at all.

The newest signal (July 31) is sovereign rather than technical: NVIDIA's newsletter reports Japan has launched what it calls the world's first national AI infrastructure, backed by METI and anchored by Noetra — a state-level buildout of the compute layer this page tracks, and the clearest instance yet of the sovereign-AI thread (previously visible in language-specific models like Soofi S) extending down from models into national compute. Vendor-reported via NVIDIA, so read the "first" claim accordingly.

Below it, two July 30 open-source drops extend both the serving and training ends of this page. Tencent’s AngelSpec is a unified training framework for speculative decoding on its Hy3 models that ships two drafters instead of one compromise — autoregressive MTP for open-ended chat, block-parallel DFly for code and math — on the observation that real traffic is not an average: DFly reaches 4.79 mean accepted length on Hy3-A21B (vs. 3.69 for DFlash and 3.00 for MTP) and a 1.98–2.40× end-to-end speedup on HY3-295B-A21B at TP=8 across concurrency 4–64, with seven checkpoints and six draft architectures behind one config-driven pipeline. Moonshot AI’s MoonEP (MIT, shipped on Kimi K3 Open Day alongside FlashKDA and AgentEnv) attacks MoE training’s slowest-rank problem with a hard guarantee: every expert-parallel rank receives exactly S×K tokens no matter how skewed the router is, via redundant experts planned online on-GPU and prefetched before expert compute, with static shapes eliminating the per-layer host sync.

An earlier signal (July 25) attacks a pipeline stage upstream of serving: tokenization. Marcel Røed's GigaToken (July 25) is an open-source Rust BPE tokenizer that encodes text at 24.53 GB/s on a 144-core AMD EPYC 9565 — a claimed up to 989x speedup over HuggingFace tokenizers (about 681x over tiktoken) — built as a drop-in replacement (66% Rust / 33% Python), meaning any pipeline currently bottlenecked on tokenization throughput before it even reaches the model gets a near-three-orders-of-magnitude lever with no model-side change. On the training side, NVIDIA's Transformer Engine showed up to 3.48x GEMM speedup using NVFP4 versus BF16 on NVIDIA Blackwell (July 21), aimed at faster training of frontier-scale transformer models — a training-throughput data point alongside the inference-economics levers this page otherwise tracks.

Inference economics otherwise remain the headline story in infrastructure. With 1M-context models becoming standard, KV cache size has gone from "a concern" to "the dominant cost driver" for long-running agents. The newest hard number on the architecture-level lever is MiniMax Sparse Attention (MSA), now detailed with specifics: a 28.4× reduction in per-token attention compute at 1M context versus dense GQA with on-par quality, translating to 14.2× faster prefill and 7.6× faster decode on an H800 — and, because MSA is GQA-native, droppable into existing serving stacks without a redesign. Since long-context inference is currently the biggest cost driver in production LLM serving, a near-30× compute cut at 1M context with no quality loss is the kind of result that tends to land in most long-context serving stacks within a year. It composes with the serving-layer levers already in play: Together AI's 8× KV cache reduction and EAGLE 3.1 speculative-decoding improvements target cost-per-token at long contexts from a different layer of the stack, joined by NVIDIA's DFlash speculative decoding (up to 15× throughput on Blackwell) and a further, lightly-detailed ~2× serving-throughput gain NVIDIA flagged in the July 10 weekly brief (no published specifics). The flat-memory idea is also reaching document AI: Baidu's Unlimited OCR keeps attention-cache memory constant regardless of document length via Recurrent Sliding Window Attention (R-SWA), attacking the KV-cache explosion that forces long-document OCR pipelines to chunk or run out of memory. The throughput-per-dollar story for very large models keeps sharpening: Xiaomi's MiMo + TileRT runtime pushed a 1.02-trillion-parameter MoE past 1,000 tokens/second on commodity GPUs (42B active, FP8), NVIDIA's Nemotron 3 Ultra used hybrid Mamba-Transformer layers plus NVFP4 quantization for up to 5× throughput, and the same sparse-activation logic now scales down to Cohere's 3B-active North Mini Code — evidence that runtime engineering, sparsity, and low-precision formats are decoupling serving cost from parameter count.

A newer lever attacks the decode step itself: Google's experimental DiffusionGemma generates text in parallel 256-token blocks (bidirectional attention + iterative self-correction) at 1,000+ tok/s on an H100 and 700+ on an RTX 5090, shifting the bottleneck from memory bandwidth to compute and pointing at a future where autoregressive decode is optional for latency-sensitive work — if quality closes.

A parallel front is running capable models locally: Gemma 4's quantization-aware-trained (QAT) weights and a 12B model tuned for a 16GB laptop, joined by streaming ASR (Nemotron 3.5 ASR) running fully local via ONNX, PrismML's sub-2-bit Bonsai 27B running on an iPhone (see Model releases), and now the browser itself as a deployment target: Google's LiteRT.js (July 16) is a JavaScript binding that runs .tflite models directly inside Chrome/Edge 113+ and Safari 17.4+, accelerated by WebGPU, with inference staying entirely client-side — zero server cost, zero inference backend, and (per Google) improved privacy and latency. It collapses the deployment gap for web ML: no Python backend, no inference server, no per-call cost, meaning any web developer can now ship a real model as a static asset, widening who can build ML features well beyond teams that can stand up serving infrastructure.

At the data-center end, NVIDIA's GTC Taipei framing of "the age of the AI factory" (Vera CPU for agents, Vera Rubin in full production, RTX Spark / DGX Station for the desk) continues to define the hardware narrative.

A newer cost lever sits one step before serving — the data pipeline that builds the indexes agents retrieve from: Flash-KMeans, an IO-aware exact K-Means using Triton GPU kernels, runs ~200× faster than FAISS and 30× faster than cuML (17.9× end-to-end on an H200) by redesigning clustering around GPU memory bottlenecks the way FlashAttention redesigned attention, directly cutting the cost and time of building and refreshing vector indexes for RAG at scale. The data-store layer beneath agents is getting the same treatment: Netflix cut wide-partition read latency on its Cassandra-backed TimeSeries store from seconds to low double-digit milliseconds using an asynchronous, metadata-driven engine that dynamically detects and splits oversized partitions per ID without a full migration — a downtime-free pattern directly reusable by the vector and metadata stores behind production AI. Self-hosted sandboxes and private-network MCP tunnels (Anthropic) round out a picture focused on the production economics of serving — and increasingly self-hosting — agentic workloads.

On the hosted-serving side, capacity is now a moving target too: Ollama reports its cloud serving of GLM-5.2 has been accelerated by additional NVIDIA Blackwell capacity in the US and Europe, a reminder that for managed-inference providers, geographic hardware supply is as much a lever on throughput and latency as the runtime optimizations above. The self-hosting-vs-API economics now have a concrete worked example from healthcare: a published cost model shows that for high-volume, full-document workloads, per-token API pricing (linear in data volume) diverges sharply from per-server local deployment (sublinear in volume), reaching ~18–29× cost differences at population scale — a reminder that beyond a certain throughput the deployment model, not the serving-layer optimization, dominates total cost.

Long-context serving

Three approaches are converging on the long-context cost problem: serving-layer KV cache compression (Together AI claims 8×), speculative decoding with reduced attention drift (EAGLE 3.1), and architecture-level sparse attention (MiniMax's MSA). Each attacks a different layer of the stack and they compose. MSA is now the most concretely quantified of the three: its two-branch design (local + global) cuts per-token attention compute at 1M context by 28.4× versus dense GQA at equal quality, measured at 14.2× faster prefill and 7.6× faster decode on an H800. The key serving property is that it is GQA-native — it slots into the attention block of an existing GQA model without architectural changes — which lowers the adoption cost from "retrain on a new architecture" to "swap the attention implementation." (Method angle on the Research papers page.) Tencent’s AngelSpec (July 30) adds the training side of speculative decoding to this layer: rather than hunting one drafter that wins the average benchmark, it trains workload-specific drafters (MTP for chat, block-parallel DFly for code/math) under one open, config-driven framework — DFly reaching 4.79 mean accepted length and 1.98–2.40× serving speedups on Hy3 models.

Quantization & on-device inference

Edge0 (Sep 11) adds a second axis to local deployment that is not about precision at all. Rather than shrinking weights to fit RAM, it leaves a 35B sparse MoE on the SSD at 23 GB and streams experts in as they are routed to, holding roughly 2.9 GB of active memory and sustaining 14.9–17.7 tokens per second on an M4 Pro Mac mini. Sparsity is the enabling property — only a small share of parameters is touched per token — so the trick does not carry to dense models, and the bottleneck moves from memory capacity to SSD read bandwidth. Read against the quantization line below, the two are complementary compressions: QAT reduces the bytes per parameter, Edge0 reduces the parameters resident at once, and a model quantized and streamed is the obvious next combination nobody has published numbers for. Quantization-aware training (QAT) is itself the preferred path to local deployment: unlike post-hoc quantization, it folds the quantization into training so the compressed weights keep nearly full quality. Gemma 4's QAT weights span every size and accelerate output across Apple, AMD, Intel, NVIDIA, and Qualcomm silicon — pushing capable agents onto laptops (the 12B targets ~16GB RAM) rather than rented GPUs. PrismML's July 16 Bonsai 27B pushes the same idea to sub-2-bit precision with no re-pretraining and no multi-GPU rig, holding ~90-95% of FP16 quality at under 6GB — small enough for an iPhone 17 Pro. See Model releases for the model-lineup angle.

Low-bit work has mostly been aimed at weights, and Nunchux AI's VC-Attention (Sep 16) moves it to the attention kernel in video diffusion transformers, where the cost concentrates. It is training-free, which is the property that makes it adoptable: Wan2.2 attention runs 1.59× faster on a B200 at 8-bit and 3.58× faster on an RTX 5090 at 4-bit with no retraining. The end-to-end figures are 1.19× and 1.70×, and publishing both is the honest part — attention is a large share of a diffusion step but never all of it, and a kernel benchmark quoted alone routinely implies four times the speedup a user would see. The 4-bit consumer-GPU result is the interesting one, since it puts the gain where the hardware budget is tightest. No kernel has shipped publicly yet, so all of it remains the authors' own measurement.

Browser & edge ML deployment

A distinct deployment target from laptop/phone on-device inference is the browser itself, where the model runs inside the user's tab rather than on the user's OS. Google's LiteRT.js (July 16) is a JavaScript binding of LiteRT that runs .tflite models directly in Chrome/Edge 113+ and Safari 17.4+, accelerated by WebGPU rather than falling back to CPU/WASM. Because inference stays local to the page, there is no inference server to run and no per-call cost — a web developer can ship a real ML feature as a static asset with no backend at all. This is the same "collapse the serving layer" move as on-device quantization, applied to the web-app deployment surface instead of the OS.

High-throughput serving of large MoEs

A distinct lever from on-device deployment is making very large open MoEs cheap to serve at scale. Xiaomi's MiMo-V2.5-Pro-UltraSpeed clears 1,000 tokens/second on a 1.02T-param MoE (42B active, FP8) via the TileRT runtime — a runtime/kernel-engineering result as much as a model one. NVIDIA's Nemotron 3 Ultra combines hybrid Mamba-Transformer layers (cheaper long-context attention), LatentMoE expert routing, and NVFP4 (a 4-bit float format) for up to 5× higher throughput across GPU architectures. The pattern: sparsity + hybrid attention + aggressive low-precision quantization are converging to break the link between parameter count and per-token serving cost. See Model releases for the model lineup. Two August/September results push this in opposite directions and are best read together. Cohere's decode megakernel optimizes the datacentre case to 292 tokens/sec at batch 1 (62% of speed-of-light, 1.58× vLLM) while keeping continuous batching, paged attention, ragged sequence lengths and tool calling — the combination, not the peak figure, is the contribution. FreeToken optimizes the opposite case: rather than assuming a fixed topology, it continuously remaps experts, model state, CPU/GPU work and agent state reuse onto whatever bandwidth and memory a personal machine currently has, reporting 20+ supported MoE models spanning a 35B on an 8 GB laptop GPU to a 753B GLM on one workstation GPU. Independent figures circulated alongside it — GLM-5.2 753B at 14.9 tok/s on 96 GB, DeepSeek-V4-Flash 284B at 22 tok/s on 32 GB, Qwen3.6-35B at 39.3 tok/s on 8 GB — are slow but not unusable, and the 8 GB number is the one most readers can check themselves.

Parallel & diffusion decoding

Distinct from compressing the KV cache or shrinking weights, a third lever targets the autoregressive decode loop itself, where tokens are produced one at a time and throughput is memory-bandwidth-bound. Google's DiffusionGemma (26B MoE) generates 256-token blocks in parallel using bidirectional attention with iterative self-correction, moving the bottleneck to compute and reaching 1,000+ tok/s on an H100 and 700+ on a consumer RTX 5090 — up to 4× faster than autoregressive generation. The trade-off today is output quality (below standard Gemma 4) and it is an experimental release, but at this throughput on consumer hardware it is a meaningful proof-of-concept; if quality closes over the next 6–12 months, parallel/diffusion decoding becomes a serious option for latency-sensitive serving. Compute & AI factories

At the hardware layer, NVIDIA is positioning compute as the revenue-bearing product: agentic and RL workloads are CPU-intensive (orchestration, data processing, trajectory replay), which it argues motivates a purpose-built "CPU for agents" (Vera) alongside the Vera Rubin accelerator platform now in full production. The same keynote pushed frontier inference toward the desk (RTX Spark, DGX Station for Windows), reinforcing the on-device deployment trend visible in the open-model tier. The AI-factory concept is also going sovereign: Japan launched what NVIDIA describes as the world's first national AI infrastructure (July 31), backed by METI and anchored by Noetra — state-backed compute as national infrastructure rather than a private-cloud purchase, with national models and vision-AI agents built on top. Two developments now bracket the factory question. On supply, Google's TPUv7 Ironwood (Sep 8) is the first TPU generation sold outright as well as rented, claiming up to 50% better performance per dollar than NVIDIA's B200/B300 and competing directly for inference Google does not run itself; NVIDIA's Groq 3 LPX (Aug 25) entered full production the same fortnight as a latency-tuned line inside Vera Rubin, claiming 4× better response times than the nearest alternative. On what can actually be plugged in, a 2027 forecast puts compute production ahead of energizable capacity by roughly 15GW, delayed not by fabs but by interconnections, transformers, cooling, networking, permitting and turbine availability — which is why Anthropic's $517B and 14.8GW of leases and SpaceX bringing turbine-blade manufacturing in-house are the same story told from the demand and the supply side. The AI factory's binding constraint has moved from silicon to substation.

Two Sep 14 items pull against that framing from below. Positron AI raised a round led by SemiAnalysis Capital — Dylan Patel and Atreides Management's Gavin Baker joining the board — for an inference chip designed around memory capacity and bandwidth instead of peak FLOPs: a single Asimov chip carries 288 GB to 2.3 TB of memory, on commodity LPDDR5X mounted on an organic substrate, air-cooled. Every part of that specification is a bet against the constraints described above. Long-context and large-MoE serving is memory-bound, and what makes frontier inference hardware scarce and site-dependent is HBM supply plus liquid cooling; commodity memory on air cooling says a large share of inference needs neither, and can therefore go in buildings that cannot host a liquid-cooled rack. Whether it holds depends on bandwidth per dollar rather than capacity, which the announcement does not give. Patel is simultaneously acquiring Citrini Research, which puts the two most-read independent newsletters covering this market under one owner who now also invests in it — worth noting when the specifications on this page are increasingly sourced from analysts with positions.

The second is a cost nobody budgets for. Konstantin Ryabitsev reports that git.kernel.org — the Linux kernel's official repository, across five geo-distributed nodes — has roughly 14 CPU cores at any moment doing nothing but rendering Git commits as HTML for crawlers, more compute than all legitimate access combined, clones included. It is the clearest public figure yet on what he calls the background radiation of abusive crawling, and it belongs on this page because it is the training-data pipeline's infrastructure bill showing up on someone else's invoice. The exposure scales with how useful a site is to index, which puts every large crawlable surface — documentation, package registries, anything Datasette-shaped — in the same position: paying a serving cost proportional to the value of their own content to a third party.

Two Sep 16 items are the first attempts to answer that bill rather than absorb it, and they split cleanly into a price and a permission. A developer priced their own pages at a penny each using the x402 protocol, which states a charge before an agent reads the content; no real payments have arrived, but the flow works end to end in testing, including an agent paying and proceeding. Charging is the third option beside blocking and absorbing, and it is the only one available to people with no leverage — a large publisher negotiates a licensing deal, and everyone else has had nothing between an open door and a robots.txt nobody honours. Cloudflare shipped the permission half the same day: a Disallow AI Training setting that keeps a site indexed for search while refusing training use. That split is the product, because publishers have faced one lever when the two uses have opposite value to them — search returns readers, training does not, and a single crawler increasingly does both — so the cost of refusal was always the traffic rather than the principle. Both share the weakness robots.txt has, which is that a declared purpose is enforced by the party it constrains; Cloudflare’s position at the edge gives it identification and rate limiting, not any view of what a model was trained on. The honest summary is that the kernel.org figure is a measured cost and these are two voluntary mechanisms, and the question neither has answered is whether a commercial crawler pays or complies when the same facts are available unpriced somewhere else.

The hardware end of this page gained an unwelcome datapoint the same day. First-party RTX 5090 inventory has effectively disappeared from US online retail, with third-party listings running roughly $6,400 to $9,500 against about $4,299 in store at Micro Center — a consumer flagship priced beside professional GPUs because its 32 GB of GDDR7 has made it local AI infrastructure and the market is pricing the memory rather than the card. That lands directly on the local-inference thread running through this page. Edge0 streaming a 35B MoE in 2.9 GB of live memory, Rapid-MLX recommending the largest model a machine can actually hold, the sub-2-bit quantization work: each is an argument about what consumer hardware can do, and each quietly assumes the hardware can be bought at list. At two to three times list the arithmetic for running locally to avoid API charges moves substantially, and it makes the memory-capacity-first server designs above — Positron’s commodity LPDDR5X among them — more interesting rather than less, since they are bets against exactly the scarcity now visible at the consumer end.

Vector-index & RAG-pipeline economics

Distinct from token-serving cost, a growing line of work attacks the offline data pipeline that produces the indexes agents query. K-Means clustering sits at the core of vector quantization, embedding clustering, RAG index building, and large-scale dataset preprocessing, and standard implementations (including FAISS) are IO-bound — they waste GPU cycles on redundant memory transfers. Flash-KMeans reimplements exact K-Means (not an approximation) with Triton GPU kernels using batched, memory-efficient access patterns built around the GPU's actual SRAM/HBM hierarchy — the same insight FlashAttention applied to attention. Reported speedups: ~200× over FAISS, ~30× over cuML, and 17.9× end-to-end over the best baselines on an H200. As a drop-in replacement for FAISS clustering, it directly lowers the cost and latency of building and refreshing vector indexes at scale (open source on GitHub). A related result at the data-store layer arrived July 9: Netflix eliminated wide-partition tail latency in its Cassandra-backed TimeSeries Abstraction by dynamically splitting oversized partitions per TimeSeries ID at read time (asynchronous, metadata-driven, no migration), taking reads from multi-second tails to low double-digit milliseconds — the same class of fix applies to the vector and metadata stores production AI systems query. See Research papers for the method angle. The pipeline shrank by one system on Sep 10: Pinecone took full-text search to GA inside the vector index itself — BM25 ranking across multiple text fields, Lucene syntax with boolean, phrase and fuzzy matching, text-match filters that constrain a semantic search to documents containing a term, and tokenization and stemming in 18 languages.

Most production RAG stacks were already running a keyword engine beside the vector store for exactly the failure Pinecone names: a literal string such as a part number, in a catalogue of strings that differ by a character, where the right record lands somewhere in the result set but nothing ranks it first and the agent answers from whatever is on top. Collapsing the two into one index removes a synchronisation problem and a second set of latency and cost to model, which is a larger saving for most teams than any per-query improvement on either half.

A week later Pinecone documented the change underneath that one, and it is the more transferable result. The new Documents API (Sep 16) adds document-centric queries beside the vector-centric ones on a schema-based index, where a schema declares text fields, dense vectors and sparse vectors on a single document rather than flattening searchable text into one field or parking it in unrankable metadata. Hybrid retrieval has always been assembled rather than built, and the obstacle was never the ranking functions — BM25 and cosine similarity both being decades old — but the absence of a record shape holding both kinds of field as first-class and rankable. Existing vectors-API indexes keep working, with the migration documented for Python SDK 10.0.0, and the cost is the standard one for schema-based systems: the shape has to be decided before the data is loaded. Three ceilings moved in the same release and each removes a specific workaround rather than adding a capability — serverless indexes on Enterprise now hold up to 1,000,000 namespaces, which matters to anyone mapping a namespace per tenant and sharding across indexes to get past the old limit; pod-to-serverless migration doubled to 50 million records without contacting Support; and Pinecone Marketplace reached end of life, with Pinecone Assistant underneath it unaffected.

The pricing change shipped alongside them is the one to model before the next billing cycle, because it meters a variable most RAG stacks never tracked. Pinecone now bills reads on egress as well, with a monthly allowance per plan — 1 GB Starter, 10 GB Builder, 100 GB Standard and Enterprise — applying across both On-Demand and Dedicated Read Nodes, while write ingress, index statistics and index-management requests stay unmetered. Past the allowance the two upper tiers keep serving and are billed at the published rate; on Starter and Builder, reads that return record data are blocked until the next period, which turns an overage into an outage and is the detail to plan around. What it prices is not query volume but how much record data each query returns, which makes a common pipeline shape expensive on purpose: fetching whole documents in order to rerank them now costs in proportion to the documents, so returning identifiers and hydrating from cheaper storage becomes a cost decision rather than a matter of taste. Perplexity’s CobbleDB (Sep 16) is the same economics answered by building rather than buying — a purpose-built key-value store for serving prepared web content that took median batch reads from 31.4ms to 5.60ms and p99 from 123ms to 24.2ms against DynamoDB, with at least 20% lower cost estimated and an open-source release planned after more hardening. Batch reads are the shape that decides search latency, since a query fans out across many documents and the p99 sets the answer, so the five-fold tail cut is the larger of the two numbers. Both are instances of one decision: a managed service is right until the access pattern is narrow and enormous, at which point the generality is what you are paying for and the thing to remove.

The same collapse arrived in Postgres the same day, from the other side of the build-versus-buy line. PlanetScale’s TIN is a GA full-text search extension covering boolean, phrase and span queries, fuzzy, wildcard and regex term matching, case and accent folding, and both COUNT(*) and BM25 top-k — the set it argues no existing Postgres text index supplies together, while still handling joins, mixed WHERE clauses, continuous updates, replication and transaction visibility. On an 85 GB Stack Exchange corpus of 150 million documents held to 8 vCPUs and 32 GB of RAM it reports 25× ParadeDB’s QPS on mixed top-10 ranked queries, 541× Postgres GIN on conjunction and phrase queries, and 57× ParadeDB under a concurrent 1,000-updates-per-second write load. The architectural claim is specific enough to argue with: TIN uses Postgres ctid values as document identifiers instead of per-segment sequential IDs, trading the delta-encoding and bit-packing that sequential IDs enable for postings intersections that vectorize on modern CPUs, which is also why it reads an order of magnitude less data per query. Read against Pinecone above, the two are the same recognition reached by opposite routes — that the second system beside the vector store was always the thing to remove — with Pinecone adding a record shape that holds both kinds of field and PlanetScale adding the index to where the rows already live. For anyone running pgvector, that is the more consequential of the two, because it removes the managed service rather than improving it. The numbers are all PlanetScale’s, produced on its own fork of the ParadeDB Benchmarker, the index costs 50.7 GB against GIN’s 28.0 GB, and only two of the four engines tested finished the suite.

Cache & context economics

For a long agent run the list price on a model card is close to irrelevant, and the section finally has an instrument for it. Pi (Sep 11) detects prompt-cache misses as they happen, notifies the developer, and prices the reread — naming the three ordinary actions that cause one: switching models mid-session, editing an earlier message, and leaving the session idle past the cache TTL. None of those looks like a cost event, which is the whole problem; a miss silently reverts every subsequent step from the cached rate to the fresh one. DeepSeek's V4.1-Flash (Sep 11) attacks the same economics structurally rather than diagnostically, with a cache using a quarter of the previous generation's HBM and an eighth of its SSD footprint. The two together complete the argument this section has been assembling: cache behaviour is the dominant cost term in agentic serving, it is now an architectural target for model vendors, and as of this week it is observable from the client side rather than inferable from the invoice.

Two Sep 8–10 items made the point from opposite ends. “The cache is the price” compares the two current frontier models where buyers rarely look: GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 advertise broadly similar per-token rates, and Fable's cache-read rate is about a quarter of Astra's. For a chat completion that is noise; for an agent replaying a large stable prefix on every step, cache reads are most of the bill and the effective difference is several-fold. It generalises the $0.25-cached against $2.00-fresh figure from the Qwen3.8-Max launch into a rule: at current discounts, prefix stability decides agent cost more than prompt length or model choice. Google's Headroom cookbook attacks the same bill from the other side by compressing tool output before it enters the prompt — 76% fewer prompt tokens with no measured loss in task accuracy, wired into Gemini 3.8 Flash, Google ADK and OpenCode. The choice of target is the interesting part: not conversation history, the usual victim and the one whose loss users notice, but tool results, which are the bulkiest and least information-dense thing in a long run. The two pull against each other, and the tension is worth holding: compression rewrites the prefix, and a rewritten prefix is a cache miss. Google's guide is candid that compression breaks down when the discarded field was the one a later step needed — a failure that presents as a model error.

Spotify's Portal result (Sep 14) is the first published number from someone who attacked the same bill by routing rather than by compressing. Dimitri Mazmanov found his agent spending most of its tokens reading files rather than reasoning, and cut token use on routine reads by roughly 90% with three pieces: a reader agent on a cheaper model that ingests large files and returns a short summary, so the raw files never touch the frontier model's context; a writer that generates tests and config from a spec plus an example and writes straight to disk; and a plugin that intercepts large reads and routes them automatically. The trade-offs are stated rather than buried, which is what makes it usable. In one benchmark the cheaper model missed a subtle thread-safety bug the frontier model caught at once, so debugging and safety-critical logic stay on the flagship. Summaries lose exact line numbers, so direct edits do too. And routing small files between lightweight agents burns more overhead than it saves. Gartner's projection that per-developer token spend passes the average developer salary by 2028 is the pressure behind it. Read against the compression work above, the two are complementary and fail differently: compression shrinks the prefix and breaks the cache, while routing leaves the prefix alone and moves whole categories of work off the expensive model — but it introduces a judgement boundary, and the benchmark miss is exactly what being wrong about that boundary looks like.

A sharper objection applies to every cache number on this page, including the ones above. “A cache hit is not proof that you skipped the work” (Sep 12) observes that the hit is asserted by the same system whose bill it reduces, and sets out what would make it evidence instead: an independent oracle expecting the prefix, an engine attesting to it, a prompt path demonstrably skipping it, byte-identical output, a passing evaluator, and a verifier binding all of it to the exact public bundle. Nobody currently meets that bar, and the practical residue is a caution rather than a method — cache-hit rate is a vendor-reported metric with no independent audit, and it is the metric a vendor has the most latitude to define favourably. The same reproducibility problem shows up one layer out in aggregation. Mohamed Moustafa's catalogue of OpenRouter routing (Sep 11) finds that one model ID can be served by providers running different inference software with different settings, that some providers of a vision model lack vision entirely, and that reasoning-effort is interpreted inconsistently between them; provider.only pins the deployment and /endpoints lists the options. A model ID names a family of deployments, not one, and anything meant to be reproducible — a cache measurement above all — has to pin which.

Kubernetes as the AI substrate

Two hyperscalers published Kubernetes-native stacks for AI work within weeks of each other, and the pairing says more than either does alone. Google's Agent Substrate arrived on GKE on Sep 17 as an open-source, secure-by-default execution runtime for agents: millions of sandboxes at around ten times the density of a standard container runtime, sub-500ms resume, more than 500 suspend/resume activations per second, and zero-trust kernel and network isolation built in rather than layered on. It runs on any Kubernetes and is merely optimised for GKE. Those two numbers are the ones that matter for anyone running agents in bulk, because they decide whether an idle session can be parked and revived or has to be kept warm. Ten times the density changes what a fleet costs; a half-second resume is what makes parking invisible to the user. Set against the $19,000 swarm refactor, this is the layer that would make such a run cheaper without changing a single thing about the agents themselves.

Microsoft's TauGrid (MIT, Kubernetes 1.30+ with GPU nodes) attacks the adjacent problem and claims no novelty at all: one Helm install brings the tau CLI, Kueue for queueing, KubeRay, GPU health monitoring and observability, with no telemetry enabled by default. Every piece is something GPU teams were already assembling by hand, and the contribution is that the assembly is now someone else's maintained artifact. Read together, the two releases mark Kubernetes winning a question that was open a year ago. The serving-side arguments on this page were about custom schedulers and bespoke orchestration; both of these put the agent sandbox and the GPU queue inside the cluster manager the rest of the estate already runs, on permissive licences, with the cloud-specific tuning left as an optimisation rather than a requirement. The remaining differentiator moves up to what Agent Substrate actually measures — density and resume — rather than to the orchestration layer itself.

Enterprise deployment patterns

Self-hosted sandboxes for agents (so user code, packages, and data don't leave the customer's network) and MCP tunnels (so MCP servers don't need a public endpoint) are the two patterns most asked about in enterprise rollouts. Procurement is starting to move too. OpenAI cut GPT-5.6 Luna 80% to $0.20/$1.20 per million tokens and Terra 20% to $2/$12 across the API, ChatGPT Work and Codex (Aug 25) — a cut at the bottom of the family, where agent loops do most of their token volume, which is where it changes routing decisions. Cheaper Inference (Sep 8) goes further and resells unused provider capacity through a single OpenAI-compatible API at 30–60% below list across five models. Whether that capacity holds under load is exactly the thing to test before it enters a production path, but the shape is new: spare inference repriced in real time rather than sold on a fixed rate card, and one more argument for keeping the model behind a swappable interface.

The clearest worked example of enterprise inference economics arrived Sep 9 from Uber, and it is worth reading as a method rather than a case study. Agent requests grew 9.4× and users 7× between February and August while spend stayed flat after April, with cost per session for one model down 52% from its June peak — achieved without capping engineer usage or negotiating discounts. The team split the bill into adoption, workload and token price, observed that adoption was supposed to grow and market prices were not theirs to set, and optimized the only remaining term. Three levers did the work: a single harness that routes each job to whichever model balances cost and quality, and keeps re-deciding as new models arrive; delegation of simpler subtasks to weaker, cheaper subagents while a powerful model holds the plan, which was the largest single saving; and sending less context, via caches held for an hour to match engineers' actual working rhythm and a code-mode that bundles many tool calls into one script for a greater-than-50% cut in tokens per query. Ramp's Router (Aug 20) sells the routing lever as a product with a claimed 40% average reduction. The generalizable claim is that an AI bill behaves like any other system under load — it is engineered down, not negotiated down. August supplied the counter-example that proves the rule: a 5,000-developer team whose bill went up 5× in a month, because autonomous coding agents were burning usage-based credits in background debug loops nobody was watching.

The lesson is that the engineering has to include a governor, not just an optimiser — agents differ from every prior workload in that they generate their own retries. Two deployment patterns follow. First, treat model choice and context size as infrastructure parameters subject to routing rather than as per-team preferences: the honest comparison across managed APIs, self-hosted models and local inference has no single winner, since self-hosting trades per-query cost against GPU capacity and operations time while local inference trades API charges for memory limits, prefill latency and lost centralised telemetry. Second, put an AI-aware gateway in front of whatever you host. The agentgateway write-up starts from one unauthenticated request that consumed 1,302 tokens of GPU time with no record of its origin, and adds authentication, stable model names, token accounting and a single endpoint in front of Ollama without changing the models — while being clear about the remaining gaps, a shared rather than per-key budget and a first failed request that still reaches the client before health-based routing evicts the bad target. The governed-MCP companion piece extends it to authorization, which has to be enforced at the tool-call boundary rather than at server access, and which runtime testing caught misconfigured where static review had not.

Meta's ZGateway (Sep 3, surfaced here Sep 17) is the same architectural instinct at a scale nobody else operates at, and it comes with the one figure this pattern usually omits. A stateless proxy tier now fronts ZippyDB, Meta's distributed key-value store, handling more than a billion operations per second and carrying about 40% of ZippyDB traffic at roughly 6% overhead. Six percent is the whole argument: a proxy tier buys routing, protocol translation and client-library churn that can be redeployed without touching either the storage layer or the callers, and the question has always been what that indirection costs at the top of the load curve. It is not deployable outside Meta, so the artifact is the number rather than the software, and it is a useful one to hold against the instinct to push routing logic back into the client to save a hop.

MoE training infrastructure

Training-side throughput is emerging as its own thread alongside inference economics. Moonshot AI’s MoonEP (July 30, MIT) targets the structural bottleneck of expert parallelism — a collective runs at the speed of its slowest rank, so the hottest expert prices every training step — by guaranteeing each EP rank exactly S×K tokens regardless of router skew, using redundant experts planned online on-GPU, prefetched before expert compute, and static shapes that eliminate per-layer host syncs. It shipped on Kimi K3 Open Day alongside FlashKDA and AgentEnv, and joins NVIDIA’s NVFP4 Transformer Engine results (up to 3.48x GEMM speedup on Blackwell) as evidence that the training cost curve is being attacked as systematically as the serving one.
